# Chapter 2: Islamic Legal Framework - Maqasid Shariah Analysis

## Introduction

The classification of cryptocurrency under Islamic law requires a systematic examination of the principles from which Islamic commercial law derives its rulings. Classical jurisprudence offers a mature body of doctrine on property, money and exchange, but it was formed in a world in which every medium of exchange was either a physical commodity or a token issued under the authority of a ruler. Cryptocurrency fits neither model. It has no physical substance, no issuer, and no guarantor, yet it is held, priced and exchanged as if it were property. The jurist therefore cannot simply locate the instrument within an existing category; the category must first be reconstructed from its underlying purposes.

This chapter undertakes that reconstruction through the lens of the *maqasid al-shari'ah*, the objectives that the law is understood to serve. The approach has a long pedigree. Al-Ghazali (d. 505/1111) articulated the five necessities (*al-daruriyyat al-khams*) that the law protects: religion (*din*), life (*nafs*), intellect (*'aql*), progeny (*nasl*) and wealth (*mal*). Al-Shatibi (d. 790/1388) systematised the hierarchy of necessities, needs and embellishments; Ibn 'Ashur (d. 1393/1973) extended the theory to public order and social welfare; and Jasser Auda has more recently proposed reading the objectives as an integrated system rather than as a checklist.

For the purposes of financial assessment, this chapter operationalises the *maqasid* through five evaluative criteria. These criteria are the author's analytical construction and should not be confused with the classical five necessities, although each is anchored in them and in recognised principles of *fiqh al-mu'amalat*:

1. **Al-Darura (stability and preservation of wealth)**, derived from the necessity of protecting *mal*;
2. **Al-Tahaqquq (certainty)**, derived from the prohibition of *gharar*;
3. **Al-Maslaha (public interest)**, derived from the principle of general welfare;
4. **Al-'Adl (justice)**, derived from the Qur'anic command of equity in exchange;
5. **Al-Karamah (dignity and inclusion)**, derived from the honour accorded to the human person and the right to economic participation.

Each criterion is scored on a scale of one to five. The scores are interpretive judgements rather than measurements, and the reasoning behind each is set out so that a reader who weighs the evidence differently can trace where the disagreement lies. The chapter first examines the classical debate on the nature of money (§2.0.1), then applies each criterion in turn (§2.1–2.5), synthesises the results (§2.6), engages the strongest case for permissibility (§2.7), and closes with conclusions and policy implications (§2.8–2.9).

### 2.0.1 Islamic Legal Foundations: The Nature of Money in Islamic Jurisprudence

Before cryptocurrency can be evaluated against any objective of the law, a prior question must be answered: what, in Islamic jurisprudence, makes something money (*thaman*) and what makes something property (*mal*)? Classical and modern jurists have answered the first question in three broad ways.

**The first position: money by creation (*thaman khilqi*).** A significant strand of classical opinion, most clearly associated with the Shafi'i school and represented also within the Maliki and Hanbali schools, holds that gold and silver are money by their very nature. On this view the effective cause (*'illah*) of the *riba* rules applicable to gold and silver is their intrinsic monetary character (*ghalabat al-thamaniyyah*), which is confined to the two metals. Other media of exchange, such as copper coins (*fulus*), may circulate as money, but they do not acquire the full legal status of *thaman*. This is the position closest to an "intrinsic value" doctrine: monetary status is grounded in the substance of the medium itself.

**The second position: money by convention (*thaman istilahi*).** The opposing view grounds monetary status in custom (*'urf*) rather than in substance. Imam Malik (d. 179/795) gave the position its best-known formulation. In the *Mudawwanah* he is reported to have said that if people were to adopt leather (*al-julud*) as their currency, minted and circulating as coin, he would dislike its sale for gold or silver on deferred terms. The statement treats a medium with no monetary substance as capable of becoming money through general acceptance. Ibn Taymiyyah (d. 728/1328) developed the argument explicitly: the dirham and the dinar have no natural or scriptural definition, and their status depends on custom and convention, because money is sought not for its own sake but as a measure of value and a means of exchange.

**The third position: modern institutional synthesis.** When paper currency displaced metallic money, contemporary juristic councils had to decide whether a medium without any intrinsic value could carry the legal consequences of *thaman*. The Council of Senior Scholars of Saudi Arabia (*Hay'at Kibar al-'Ulama'*), in a resolution of 1393 AH (1973), held that paper money is an independent form of *thaman* to which the rules of gold and silver apply. The resolution grounded this status in general public acceptance (*al-qabul al-'amm*) together with the backing of the issuing state. Later bodies, including the International Islamic Fiqh Academy, adopted substantially the same position. The synthesis accepts the conventionalist premise of the second position but adds an institutional condition: acceptance is anchored in public authority.

**Application to cryptocurrency.** The three positions yield different results when applied to a cryptocurrency such as Bitcoin. Under the first, the answer is straightforward. Bitcoin is neither gold nor silver, and it cannot be *thaman* by creation. Under the second, the question becomes empirical: has Bitcoin attained the general acceptance that Malik and Ibn Taymiyyah treated as sufficient? In most Muslim societies the answer is that it has not, because cryptocurrency circulates chiefly as an object of investment and trading rather than as a routine medium of payment. Under the third, the result is again negative, since the synthesis requires the backing of public authority and cryptocurrency is, by design, issued without it. In jurisdictions such as Indonesia, positive law prohibits its use as a means of payment.

**Conclusion on monetary status.** On the classical and modern positions surveyed here, pure cryptocurrency does not presently qualify as money in the juristic sense. This conclusion concerns *thaman* only. It does not settle whether cryptocurrency is *mal*, that is, property capable of being owned and lawfully exchanged as a commodity. That second question is analytically distinct, and in 2026 it became the decisive point of divergence among Islamic authorities. The Darul Uloom Karachi fatwa of June 2026 held that crypto-assets are not *mal*. In contrast, the 2026 determinations of Muhammadiyah (*mal mutaqawwam*) and the Nahdlatul Ulama Muktamar (Bitcoin as *mal* and, in the fiqh sense, *thaman*) treat them as property (Chapter 3, §3.4.5 and §3.5). The NU determination shows that the conventionalist position can be read to reach a different result on the monetary question as well, while still denying lawful use as currency in Indonesia on grounds of positive law. The remainder of this chapter therefore evaluates cryptocurrency primarily as an asset.

## 2.1 Al-Darura (Necessity): Financial Stability and Wealth Preservation

### 2.1.1 Principle Definition

Among the five necessities identified by al-Ghazali, the preservation of wealth (*hifz al-mal*) is the one most directly engaged by financial instruments. The law protects wealth in two directions: positively, by enabling its acquisition and growth through lawful exchange, and negatively, by prohibiting transactions that expose it to destruction through deception, gambling or unjustified risk. The criterion of *al-darura* used in this chapter asks whether an instrument, as it is actually held and traded, preserves the wealth of its holders and the stability of the wider financial system, or whether it systematically endangers them.

### 2.1.2 Islamic Financial Principles

Several established rules of *fiqh al-mu'amalat* give content to this criterion. The prohibition of *gharar* requires that the terms and subject matter of a contract be sufficiently known. The prohibition of *maysir* excludes transactions in which gain accrues to one party purely through chance at the expense of another. Islamic finance does not prohibit risk; profit is legitimised precisely by the bearing of commercial risk (*al-ghunm bi al-ghurm*). It does, however, distinguish risk that is incidental to productive activity from risk that is sought for its own sake. Finally, the jurists' concern with the stability of the means of exchange, voiced most clearly by Ibn al-Qayyim in his discussion of the harm caused by fluctuating *fulus*, reflects a systemic dimension of wealth preservation that extends beyond the individual contract.

### 2.1.3 Cryptocurrency Evaluation Against Al-Darura

#### Price Volatility and Lack of Stability

The salient feature of pure cryptocurrency, measured against this criterion, is the magnitude of its price movements. Bitcoin's price has repeatedly moved by several multiples within a single year, and intraday movements that would be exceptional for an equity index are routine in crypto-asset markets. Price movement as such is not prohibited. What matters for *hifz al-mal* is that the volatility is not anchored in any underlying asset or cash flow. The value of a share reflects, however imperfectly, a claim on a productive enterprise, and the value of a commodity reflects its use. The value of Bitcoin reflects only the market's collective expectation of what others will pay for it. Price movements are therefore driven predominantly by sentiment, liquidity and speculative positioning rather than by any change in economic substance.

#### Risk of Total Loss

A further consequence is that holders face a realistic risk of total loss from causes unrelated to the commercial merits of any enterprise. Three such causes are prominent. The first is market manipulation, which thin regulation and fragmented liquidity make easier than in supervised securities markets. The second is security failure: exchange insolvencies, hacks and lost private keys have each destroyed holdings permanently, without any legal remedy capable of restoring them. The third is technological obsolescence, since a protocol may lose its user base to a competing design. None of these risks is unique to cryptocurrency, but their combination, together with the absence of any underlying claim, places pure crypto-assets at the far end of the risk spectrum. They lie well beyond the incidental risk that *al-ghunm bi al-ghurm* legitimises.

### 2.1.4 Assessment Score: 1/5

Measured against the criterion of wealth preservation, pure cryptocurrency receives the lowest score. Its volatility is unanchored, its holders bear a material risk of total loss from non-commercial causes, and it offers no mechanism by which capital may be protected or recovered. The score is a judgement about the asset class as it is presently held and traded. As §2.6.4 shows, asset-backed designs can perform considerably better on this criterion.

## 2.2 Al-Tahaqquq (Certainty): Transparency and Clear Valuation

### 2.2.1 Principle Definition

The criterion of *al-tahaqquq* expresses the requirement that the essential elements of a transaction be definite and known to the parties. Its doctrinal source is the prohibition of *gharar*, attested in the prophetic tradition reported by Muslim that the Prophet forbade the sale of *gharar*. The jurists did not read this as a prohibition of all uncertainty, which would make commerce impossible. They distinguished excessive uncertainty (*gharar fahish*), which invalidates a contract, from minor uncertainty (*gharar yasir*), which is tolerated, and they located the relevant uncertainty in the object, the price, the quantity and the capacity to deliver.

### 2.2.2 Islamic Requirements for Certainty

Applied to financial instruments, the requirement has five components. The price of the exchanged items must be ascertainable at the time of contract. The quantity must be specified. The quality or characteristics of the object must be defined. Ownership and legal status must be clear, so that the seller is able to transfer what is sold. Finally, the rights and obligations of each party must be understood by all. An instrument satisfies *al-tahaqquq* to the extent that each of these elements can be known.

### 2.2.3 Cryptocurrency Evaluation Against Al-Tahaqquq

#### Blockchain Transparency Advantage

On several of these components cryptocurrency performs well, and in some respects better than conventional instruments. Every transaction on a public blockchain is recorded on a ledger that any party can inspect and that cannot be altered after the fact. The quantity held at any address is therefore objectively verifiable, the protocol fixes total supply and divisibility, and transfers settle according to rules that are public and deterministic. Where smart contracts are used, the terms of execution are themselves published as code. In the dimension of transactional transparency, the technology approaches the ideal that the prohibition of *gharar* was designed to secure.

#### Fundamental Price Uncertainty

The difficulty lies elsewhere. Certainty of record is not certainty of value. Although the price of a cryptocurrency is known at the moment of each trade, and the contract of sale is therefore not void for an unknown price, nothing anchors that price to an assessable underlying value. A buyer of a commodity knows what use the object will serve, and a buyer of a share knows what enterprise the share represents. The buyer of Bitcoin knows only that others presently assign it a price. The resulting uncertainty attaches not to the contract but to the object's worth over time, and it is of a magnitude that makes any expectation about future value a matter of speculation.

### 2.2.4 Legal Certainty Issues

The requirement of certainty also extends to legal status, because a party cannot be certain of what he owns if the law is unsettled about what it is. Here, too, the picture is mixed. Jurisdictions classify crypto-assets variously as currency, commodity, security, financial asset or unregulated property, and the classification determines the applicable tax treatment, the availability of legal recourse, and the remedies open to an investor if an intermediary fails. Indonesia illustrates both the problem and its partial resolution. Until January 2025 crypto-assets were supervised as commodities by Bappebti. Since 10 January 2025 they have been supervised as digital financial assets by the Financial Services Authority (OJK) under POJK 27/2024 (Chapter 4, §4.1). The reclassification improved legal certainty considerably, but it did not settle the Sharia status of the assets concerned.

### 2.2.5 Assessment Score: 3/5

Cryptocurrency therefore receives an intermediate score on this criterion. Transactional transparency, verifiable quantity and deterministic settlement satisfy much of what the prohibition of *gharar* demands, and in these respects the technology is genuinely strong. The absence of any anchor for value, together with residual legal uncertainty in many jurisdictions, prevents a higher score. The criterion is partially met.

## 2.3 Al-Maslaha (Public Interest): Community Benefit and Economic Utility

### 2.3.1 Principle Definition

*Al-maslaha* denotes the benefit that the law seeks to secure for the community and the harm it seeks to avert. Classical theory recognised a category of unregulated benefit (*al-maslaha al-mursalah*), developed most fully in the Maliki school, under which a measure not addressed by specific texts may be adopted if it serves the evident purposes of the law. The broader orientation is captured in the Qur'anic description of the Prophet's mission as "a mercy to the worlds" (al-Anbiya' 21:107). As an evaluative criterion, *al-maslaha* asks whether an instrument, in its actual use, serves productive economic purposes and the welfare of the community.

### 2.3.2 Islamic Finance Applications

In Islamic finance the criterion is usually expressed through the preference for transactions connected to real economic activity. Financial instruments are expected to support production and trade, to finance the acquisition of real assets or the conduct of real enterprises, and to contribute to economic development and financial inclusion. Conversely, instruments whose primary function is to enable bets on price movement are regarded with suspicion even where no specific prohibition applies, because they absorb capital without directing it to productive use.

### 2.3.3 Cryptocurrency Evaluation Against Al-Maslaha

#### Limited Practical Utility

The claimed public benefits of cryptocurrency are real in principle: low-cost cross-border transfers, access to financial services for those excluded from banking, and resistance to censorship of payments. The difficulty is that these benefits remain marginal in practice relative to the instrument's dominant use. The available evidence consistently indicates that most crypto-asset activity is trading and investment for price gain. Use for the purchase of goods and services remains a small fraction of activity, and the share of activity linked to illicit purposes such as ransomware, fraud and sanctions evasion is material. Precise proportions vary considerably by source, methodology and year, and any quantitative statement should rest on a specific, dated dataset such as Chainalysis's annual crypto crime and adoption reports. [Unverified: proportions to be cited]

The Indonesian data confirm the qualitative picture. By August 2026 the market supervised by OJK counted 23.21 million accounts. Monthly spot transaction value was Rp23.16 trillion, and derivatives transactions reached Rp4.56 trillion. Use as a means of payment is prohibited by law (Chapter 4, §4.1.11). The market is, by its legal design, an investment and trading market.

#### Speculative Nature Contradicts Public Interest

The significance of this pattern for *al-maslaha* is not that speculation is present, since every liquid market contains speculation. It is that speculation is the principal reason most participants hold the asset. Where the dominant use of an instrument consists of bets on future price, the benefit it confers on the community is largely limited to the redistribution of wealth among participants, while the costs fall on those who enter last or exit late.

The contrast with established Islamic financial instruments is instructive, as Table 2.2 shows.

### 2.3.4 Negative Externalities

The assessment must also account for harms that fall on third parties. The first is environmental. Proof-of-work networks, of which Bitcoin is the largest, consume electricity on a scale comparable to that of a medium-sized country. Published estimates of Bitcoin's annual consumption have been on the order of 100–150 TWh in recent years, though they vary with method and period. [Unverified: cite a specific estimate, e.g., the Cambridge Bitcoin Electricity Consumption Index, with date.] That consumption sits uneasily with the stewardship (*khilafah*) entrusted to humanity over the earth. The second is social. The volatility of crypto-assets has inflicted significant losses on retail participants, the distribution of gains has favoured early holders, and the instrument continues to be used to facilitate crime. These externalities do not by themselves render the asset prohibited, but they weigh against it in any assessment of public benefit.

### 2.3.5 Assessment Score: 2/5

Pure cryptocurrency receives a low score on the criterion of public interest. Its theoretical benefits for payments and inclusion are genuine but, in practice, subordinate to its use as a speculative asset, and its negative externalities further reduce its net contribution to the welfare of the community. The criterion is not met.

## 2.4 Al-Adl (Justice): Fairness and Equitable Treatment

### 2.4.1 Principle Definition

Justice (*'adl*) is among the most frequently invoked values of the Qur'an: "Indeed, Allah commands justice and good conduct" (al-Nahl 16:90). In the law of exchange it is expressed in the requirement of mutual consent free from deception, in the prohibition of *riba* as an unjust transfer of value, and in the prohibition of practices that exploit the ignorance or weakness of a counterparty. As an evaluative criterion, *al-'adl* asks whether an instrument distributes risks and rewards equitably among its participants and whether its market structure protects the weaker party.

### 2.4.2 Islamic Justice Requirements

Islamic finance gives effect to justice through several mechanisms. In partnership contracts, profit is shared by agreement and loss in proportion to capital, so that no party is guaranteed a return at another's expense. *Riba* is prohibited to prevent the systematic transfer of wealth from borrower to lender. Transactions are expected to occur at a fair price, and the classical prohibitions of *tadlis*, *najsh* and *talaqqi al-rukban* address the exploitation of informational advantage. The law also extends particular protection to parties who are vulnerable by reason of ignorance, need or inexperience.

### 2.4.3 Cryptocurrency Evaluation Against Al-Adl

#### Early Adopter Advantage Creates Injustice

The distribution of cryptocurrency holdings is highly concentrated. Bitcoin's earliest participants acquired coins at negligible cost when mining required little computational effort, and the mining rewards that accrued to them in the network's first years account for a substantial share of the total supply. As a result, a small proportion of addresses holds a large share of all coins. Address-level statistics overstate individual concentration, because a single exchange address may represent many customers. Even so, the pattern of early accumulation and later diffusion at much higher prices is not in doubt. [Unverified: a citable estimate of concentration, adjusted for exchange addresses, is required.] The relevance to *al-'adl* is that the gains of early holders are realised largely through sales to later entrants, so that the structure resembles a transfer from later to earlier participants rather than a return on shared productive effort.

#### Information Asymmetry and Market Manipulation

Crypto-asset markets also exhibit the informational and manipulative practices that classical law prohibits. Project developers and early investors frequently possess material information about token supply, unlocking schedules and partnerships that retail purchasers lack. Under thin regulation, coordinated pump-and-dump schemes, spoofing and wash trading distort prices and volumes. Retail participants typically have little practical recourse when harmed by such conduct. Each of these practices corresponds to a category prohibited in classical law: *tadlis* (concealment), *najsh* (artificial bidding), *ghabn* (exploitative pricing) and *taghrir* (deception). Indonesian fatwa practice has already catalogued these practices for the securities market in DSN-MUI Fatwa No. 80/2011 (Chapter 3, §3.4.6). Their prevalence in crypto-asset markets is a defect of conduct rather than of the asset itself, but it bears directly on whether participants in those markets are treated justly.

#### Volatility Creates Unfair Outcomes

Volatility compounds these inequities. Consider two investors who buy the same asset six months apart, one at $40,000 and the other at $60,000, and who both hold it as the price falls to $30,000. The first loses a quarter of the capital and the second half. The difference in outcome reflects nothing about the merits of any enterprise or the effort of either party; it reflects only the timing of entry relative to price movements that neither could reasonably foresee. In a partnership, by contrast, losses are borne in proportion to the capital each party contributes to a common venture.

#### Exploitation of Retail Investors

The consequences fall disproportionately on retail participants. Professional traders with superior information, execution and capital are positioned to profit from volatility. Leveraged derivatives, which are now lawfully offered in Indonesia under POJK 23/2025 (Chapter 4, §4.1), allow retail traders to lose more than their initial stake. Research on retail day trading in conventional markets consistently finds that a large majority lose money over time. A comparable crypto-specific estimate should be cited before any figure is stated. [Unverified]

### 2.4.4 Contrast with Islamic Financial Principles

The contrast with the partnership model of Islamic finance clarifies the nature of the deficit. In a *musharakah*, risks and returns are shared in proportion to contribution, no party is structurally advantaged by the timing of entry, and the partnership itself creates an obligation of disclosure among the partners. In the market for pure cryptocurrency, early holders capture a disproportionate share of gains, later entrants bear a disproportionate share of risk, and informational asymmetry systematically favours insiders. The two models embody different conceptions of how gain is legitimately earned.

### 2.4.5 Assessment Score: 2/5

Pure cryptocurrency receives a low score on the criterion of justice. The concentration of holdings, the prevalence of informational advantage and manipulation, and the exposure of retail participants to losses determined by timing rather than merit place the market structure in tension with *al-'adl*. Part of this deficit, especially manipulation and abusive conduct, is attributable to market conduct and could in principle be remedied by regulation. The structural transfer from later to earlier entrants cannot be remedied in the same way. The criterion is not met.

## 2.5 Al-Karamah (Dignity): Economic Participation and Inclusion

### 2.5.1 Principle Definition

The Qur'an affirms that "We have certainly honoured the children of Adam" (al-Isra' 17:70). In economic terms, this honour is realised through the capacity of each person to participate in economic life, to earn a lawful livelihood, and to avoid both exploitation and dependence. As an evaluative criterion, *al-karamah* asks whether an instrument widens access to the economy for those presently excluded from it, and whether it does so in a way that strengthens rather than endangers their position.

### 2.5.2 Islamic Dignity Requirements

Islamic finance pursues dignity through several overlapping aims: enabling individuals of all economic backgrounds to save, invest and build wealth; supporting the passage from poverty to self-sufficiency; protecting individuals from debt servitude; preventing systematic exclusion based on wealth or status; and securing the possibility of an honest livelihood. Zakat, waqf and *qard hasan* serve these aims directly, and commercial instruments are expected at least not to undermine them.

### 2.5.3 Cryptocurrency Evaluation Against Al-Karamah

#### Financial Inclusion Potential

This is the criterion on which cryptocurrency makes its strongest claim. A cryptocurrency wallet requires neither a bank account nor a credit history; a mobile phone and an internet connection suffice. In economies with limited banking infrastructure, this lowers the threshold of participation considerably. Cross-border transfers can be made without correspondent banks, potentially reducing the cost of remittances on which many migrant workers and their families depend. Decentralised finance also enables small-value transactions and access to financial markets that were previously closed to those without significant capital or institutional relationships.

#### Practical Limitations on Inclusion

These advantages are, however, constrained in practice by four factors. The first is volatility. A person living on daily income cannot hold the next week's expenses in an asset whose value may fall by a fifth in a day, and a wage earner cannot budget in such a unit. The second is the technological and literacy threshold: secure wallet management demands technical knowledge that the excluded populations are least likely to possess, so that a new form of digital divide replaces the old. The third is the risk to the poor themselves. Households without savings buffers are the least able to absorb losses, and an instrument that transfers volatility risk onto them may deepen rather than relieve poverty. The fourth is that actual adoption for inclusion purposes remains marginal. El Salvador's adoption of Bitcoin as legal tender in 2021 produced limited everyday use, and the legal tender status was substantially scaled back in 2025. Where crypto-assets are used for payments in emerging markets, users tend to prefer stablecoins over volatile tokens.

#### Stablecoins vs. Volatile Cryptocurrencies

This last observation draws attention to a distinction that recurs throughout the dissertation. Table 2.3 summarises it for the present criterion.

**Table 2.3: Volatile cryptocurrencies and stablecoins against Al-Karamah**

| Aspect | Volatile Cryptocurrency | Stablecoin |
|--------|----------------|------------|
| Daily use by the unbanked | Unsuitable (volatility) | Possible |
| Cross-border transfers | Possible, but costly in value risk | Suitable |
| Preservation of savings | Unsuitable (risk of loss) | Suitable, subject to reserve quality |
| Contribution to financial dignity | Moderate | High |
| Al-Karamah score | 3/5 | 4/5 |

Most of the inclusion benefits claimed for cryptocurrency are realised by stablecoins rather than by volatile tokens. Stablecoins do, however, depend on the quality and verifiability of their reserves, which raises its own questions of *gharar*. Notably, the June 2026 Darul Uloom Karachi fatwa excluded stablecoins as well (Chapter 3, §3.3.2).

### 2.5.4 Assessment Score: 4/5

Cryptocurrency, taken as a technology inclusive of stablecoin designs, receives its highest score on the criterion of dignity. Its capacity to lower the threshold of economic participation is genuine and, for unbanked populations, potentially significant. The score reflects potential rather than realised impact. For volatile tokens considered alone, the score would be closer to 3/5, as Table 2.3 indicates. The criterion is partially met, and it represents the strongest point of alignment between cryptocurrency and the objectives of the law.

## 2.6 Comprehensive Maqasid Assessment: Synthesis and Conclusions

### 2.6.1 Summary Evaluation

Taken together, the five assessments yield a predominantly negative evaluation of pure cryptocurrency, as Table 2.4 summarises.

**Table 2.4: Maqasid assessment of pure cryptocurrency**

| Criterion | Score | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Al-Darura (stability and wealth preservation) | 1/5 | Not met |
| Al-Tahaqquq (certainty and transparency) | 3/5 | Partially met |
| Al-Maslaha (public interest) | 2/5 | Not met |
| Al-'Adl (justice) | 2/5 | Not met |
| Al-Karamah (dignity and inclusion) | 4/5 | Partially met |
| **Overall (unweighted mean)** | **2.4/5 (48%)** | **Majority of criteria not met** |

The overall figure is an unweighted mean and should be read with caution. As §2.7.2 argues, the *maqasid* are an integrated system rather than a set of independent scores, and a high score on one criterion does not offset a failure on another. The mean is reported as a summary indicator, not as a ruling.

### 2.6.2 Critical Deficiencies

Three structural deficiencies account for the low overall score. The first is the failure of wealth preservation: unanchored volatility and the absence of any underlying claim expose holders to losses that are unrelated to the merits of any enterprise and that cannot be recovered. The second is the subordination of public benefit to speculation. The instrument's dominant use is trading for price gain rather than productive exchange, and its environmental and social externalities further reduce its net contribution to welfare. The third is the deficit of justice: the transfer of gains from later to earlier entrants, combined with informational asymmetry and manipulation, distributes risk and reward in a way that is difficult to reconcile with *al-'adl*.

### 2.6.3 Mitigating Factors

Two features of the technology mitigate this assessment. The first is transactional transparency. A public, immutable ledger satisfies the demand for certainty of record more completely than most conventional instruments, and this is the reason the asset scores in the middle range on *al-tahaqquq*. The second is the potential for inclusion. The capacity to lower the threshold of economic participation represents the strongest point of alignment between cryptocurrency and the objectives of the law, even though that potential is presently realised more by stablecoins than by volatile tokens.

### 2.6.4 Asset-Backed Cryptocurrencies: Higher Compliance

The most important result of the analysis is that the deficiencies are concentrated in pure cryptocurrency and are substantially reduced in designs backed by identifiable assets. A commodity-backed token, such as one representing allocated gold, improves on *al-darura* because its value is anchored in the backing asset, and on *al-tahaqquq* because that asset supplies an assessable basis of value. It may also improve on *al-maslaha* where the backing connects the token to real economic activity. Such designs may be expected to score in the range of 3.5–4.0/5. A fiat-collateralised stablecoin performs strongly on *al-darura* and *al-tahaqquq* because its value is pegged to a stable reference, and moderately on *al-maslaha* because its use is less speculative. It also mitigates the early-adopter problem under *al-'adl*, and may be expected to score in the range of 4.0–4.5/5. These ranges are indicative. Each design must be assessed on its own terms, including the quality of reserves, the rights of holders and the verifiability of backing, which is the task of the five-dimensional framework in Chapter 5.

### 2.6.5 Jurisprudential Implications

The *maqasid* analysis can be set alongside the positions of the principal Islamic authorities, examined in detail in Chapter 3. As of October 2026, AAOIFI has issued no Shari'ah Standard specific to crypto-assets (§3.1.2). Dar al-Ifta al-Misriyyah, in a fatwa issued at the end of December 2017 and reported on 1 January 2018, held Bitcoin trading impermissible. The reasons reported were that it is not an accepted medium of exchange, is not subject to state supervision, carries a risk of fraud, and is highly volatile (§3.2.2). The fatwa of Darul Uloom Karachi of 10 June 2026, signed by Mufti Muhammad Taqi Usmani and others, held that crypto-assets, including stablecoins, are not *mal* and that transactions in them are impermissible (§3.3.2). In Indonesia, the Seventh Ijtima' Ulama of MUI (11 November 2021) held cryptocurrency *haram* as currency and, as a commodity, valid only where the conditions of *sil'ah*, a clear underlying and a clear benefit are met. Muhammadiyah and the Nahdlatul Ulama have since, in 2026, permitted crypto as an asset subject to conditions (§3.4.5).

The *maqasid* analysis supports the common ground among these authorities, which is that pure cryptocurrency is unsuitable as money. It does not settle the point on which they now diverge, namely whether crypto-assets are *mal* at all. That divergence turns on how the threshold concept of property is defined, a question the *maqasid* inform but cannot by themselves resolve.

## 2.7 Counterarguments: The Case for Conditional Permissibility

The maqasid assessment above reaches a predominantly negative conclusion for *pure* cryptocurrency (2.4/5). Intellectual honesty—and the standard of doctoral argumentation—requires that the strongest opposing position be reconstructed in its most compelling form before it is answered. A substantial and growing body of scholarship contends that cryptocurrency, properly understood, *advances* rather than undermines the objectives of the Shariah. This section presents that permissibility thesis at full strength (§2.7.1) and then explains why the analysis nonetheless holds to a conclusion of *conditional* rather than general permissibility (§2.7.2).

### 2.7.1 The Permissibility Thesis (Steelman)

The pro-permissibility position rests on four mutually reinforcing arguments, each grounded in recognised principles of *fiqh al-muamalat*.

**First, the presumption of permissibility and the burden of proof.** The governing maxim of Islamic commercial law is *al-asl fi al-muamalat al-ibaha*—the default ruling in transactions is permissibility unless a specific, authenticated prohibition applies (Kamali, *Principles of Islamic Jurisprudence*, 2003; Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya, *I'lam al-Muwaqqi'in*). This is the mirror image of the rule for acts of worship, where the default is prohibition. On this view the evidentiary burden falls on the party asserting prohibition: to forbid a novel but potentially beneficial instrument requires a definitive textual basis (*nass qat'i*) or a firmly established harm, not merely the presence of risk. Proponents such as Mufti Faraz Adam ("Bitcoin: Shariah Compliant?", Amanah Finance Consultancy, 2017) and Charles W. Evans ("Bitcoin in Islamic Banking and Finance," *Journal of Islamic Banking and Finance*, 2015) argue that no such definitive prohibition of the *asset class as such* exists.

**Second, cryptocurrency as *mal* by customary recognition.** The prohibitionist objection that cryptocurrency "lacks intrinsic value" assumes that *mal* (property) requires intrinsic substance. Classical Hanafi jurisprudence defines *mal* functionally—as that toward which human nature inclines and which can be stored for time of need (Al-Kasani, *Bada'i al-Sana'i*; Ibn 'Abidin, *Radd al-Muhtar*)—and the Maliki tradition admits *'urf* (settled custom) as a validating source for new exchange instruments (Ibn Rushd, *Bidayat al-Mujtahid*). On this basis, an asset that a community widely accepts as a medium of exchange acquires the status of *mal* through recognition, exactly as fiat currency and pre-modern *fulus* (copper token money) did despite lacking intrinsic worth. Widespread acceptance, proponents argue, supplies precisely the "social consensus" that classical jurists treated as sufficient.

**Third, service to the higher objectives—Al-Karamah and Al-Maslaha.** The analysis in §2.5 already scores cryptocurrency 4/5 on Al-Karamah for its financial-inclusion potential. Proponents extend this: for unbanked Muslim populations, low-cost cross-border remittance and permissionless access to store-of-value instruments serve the preservation of wealth (*hifz al-mal*) and human dignity more effectively than an exclusionary formal sector (Habib Ahmed, *Maqasid al-Shari'ah and Islamic Financial Products*, 2011). Jasser Auda's systems-based reading of the maqasid (*Maqasid al-Shari'ah as Philosophy of Islamic Law*, 2008) supports weighing such realised benefits rather than presuming harm from novelty.

**Fourth, the decisive concession—structured instruments.** As §2.6.4 demonstrates, asset-backed tokens and fiat-collateralised stablecoins score 3.5–4.5/5. Proponents argue this establishes the principle: the maqasid deficiencies the analysis identifies are contingent features of *particular designs*, not necessary properties of the technology. If some digital assets satisfy the objectives, then a categorical prohibition of "cryptocurrency" is over-broad, and the correct juristic task is discrimination among designs rather than blanket exclusion (Mohd Daud Bakar, public rulings on digital assets, 2019–2022).

### 2.7.2 Response: Why the Conclusion Is Conditional, Not Categorical

The permissibility thesis is powerful, and this dissertation accepts a substantial part of it. It is answered not by rejection but by *boundary-setting*.

**On the burden of proof.** The presumption of permissibility is rebuttable, and it is rebutted where a preponderant (*ghalib al-zann*), not merely possible, harm is established. The empirical predominance of speculative over transactional use (documented in §2.3 and Chapter 4) converts the abstract *possibility* of *maisir* into its realised *predominance*. Where the operative characteristic of an instrument's actual use is gambling-like speculation under severe *gharar*, the doctrine of *sadd al-dhara'i* (blocking the means to harm) supplies the "specific harm" the maxim demands. The burden is therefore met—but only for the speculative use, not for the technology as such.

**On *mal* by custom.** Recognition confers *mal*-status only through a *sound* custom (*'urf sahih*)—one not built upon a prohibited foundation. A convention of acceptance that exists primarily to facilitate leveraged price speculation is not a sound *'urf* in the classical sense, even if it is widespread. The argument from custom thus validates cryptocurrencies whose acceptance rests on genuine exchange and settlement utility, while leaving untouched those whose "acceptance" is functionally a speculative venue.

**On the maqasid.** The Al-Karamah benefit is real and is scored accordingly; but a single strongly-satisfied objective does not override failures on Al-Darura, Al-Maslaha, and Al-Adl when the maqasid are weighed as an integrated system rather than tallied. Auda's own methodology cautions against optimising one objective at the expense of the whole. Financial inclusion delivered through an instrument that transfers volatility risk onto the very populations it purports to serve is a defective realisation of dignity.

**The point of agreement.** Critically, the permissibility thesis and this analysis converge on the operative outcome. The thesis succeeds precisely where the analysis already concedes—asset-backed and stablecoin designs (§2.6.4)—and fails precisely where the analysis finds against pure speculative tokens. The disagreement is therefore not over *whether* the maqasid can be satisfied by digital assets, but over *which* designs satisfy them. This is a divergence of *tahqiq al-manat* (verifying the operative cause in the concrete case), not of principle. The resulting position—**conditional permissibility contingent on verified design characteristics**—is the thesis this dissertation defends, and it is precisely what the five-dimensional framework of Chapter 5 operationalises.

## 2.8 Chapter Conclusion

### Summary of Findings

The application of the *maqasid* to cryptocurrency yields a differentiated rather than a uniform result. Pure cryptocurrency, assessed as it is presently held and traded, fails the majority of the criteria. It does not preserve wealth, because its volatility is unanchored by any underlying asset and its holders face a material risk of total loss. It does not, on balance, serve the public interest, because its dominant use is speculative and its environmental and social externalities are significant. And it sits uneasily with justice, because the structure of the market transfers gains from later to earlier entrants and exposes retail participants to informational disadvantage and manipulation.

The technology nevertheless has genuine merits. Its transactional transparency satisfies much of what the prohibition of *gharar* demands, and its potential to widen economic participation is the strongest point of alignment with the objectives of the law. Both merits are realised more fully in asset-backed and stablecoin designs, which perform substantially better across the criteria. The central conclusion of the chapter is therefore that the Sharia evaluation of a digital asset depends on its design. The analysis does not support a categorical prohibition of the technology. It supports a conditional permissibility that turns on verifiable characteristics of each asset, which Chapter 5 sets out to operationalise.

The chapter also shows the limits of the *maqasid* method. The objectives of the law can establish that an instrument is harmful or beneficial in its effects, but they cannot by themselves determine the threshold question of whether an intangible ledger entry is property at all. On that question, as the following chapter shows, Islamic authorities diverged sharply in 2026.

### Transition to Next Chapter

Chapter 3 therefore turns from principles to positions. It examines how AAOIFI, Dar al-Ifta al-Misriyyah, Mufti Muhammad Taqi Usmani and the Darul Uloom Karachi, and Indonesian authorities (MUI, Muhammadiyah and the Nahdlatul Ulama) have applied classical jurisprudence to cryptocurrency, and it traces the methodological differences that explain their divergent conclusions.

## 2.9 Policy Implications for Regulators

The *maqasid* framework also has practical implications for the authorities that supervise Islamic financial institutions and crypto-asset markets. Its principal value to a regulator is that it provides a principled and transparent basis for assessing digital assets, one that can be applied across jurisdictions and that does not depend on the opinion of any single scholar. Four actions follow from the analysis.

**Incorporating maqasid-based assessment into licensing criteria.** A regulator could define a minimum level of *maqasid* alignment as a condition for classifying a digital asset as Sharia-compliant (Category A in the framework of Chapter 5). A threshold of satisfying at least three of the five criteria, or an overall score of at least 60 percent, would be a reasonable starting point. Such a threshold would need to be operationalised consistently by a Sharia advisory body. It should be aligned with AAOIFI's general standards while leaving room for jurisdiction-specific implementation.

**Requiring public disclosure of an Islamic compliance score.** Digital asset issuers, or the platforms that list them, could be required to disclose an assessment of each asset against the five criteria, together with the methodology used. Publication would allow investors and institutions to compare assets on a common basis and would narrow the informational gap between sophisticated and retail participants that §2.4 identified as a source of injustice.

**Establishing a Sharia advisory council with technical competence.** The assessment requires scholars who combine command of *fiqh al-mu'amalat* with an understanding of the technology. A council of five to seven such members could review the classification methodology, assess new types of digital asset, and liaise with counterpart authorities in other jurisdictions. Its members should be independent of the regulated entities, to guard against capture, and its scoring guidelines should be reviewed at regular intervals, for example quarterly, to keep pace with the market. In Indonesia, the natural location for such a function is the existing relationship between OJK and DSN-MUI. According to OJK, the two bodies are still coordinating a joint study on the Sharia basis for standards in the digital asset industry (Chapter 4, §4.1).

**Creating a distinct pathway for asset-backed and stablecoin instruments.** Because the analysis distinguishes sharply between pure cryptocurrencies and backed designs, a regulator could establish a separate licensing track for asset-backed digital assets. That track would carry defined requirements on the type of backing asset, the reserve ratio and the frequency of independent audit. Such a pathway would allow Islamic financial institutions to participate in the segment of the digital asset market that the analysis finds most capable of compliance.

Taken together, these measures promise greater consistency, by reducing discretion; greater legitimacy, by grounding classifications in Islamic legal reasoning; greater efficiency, by reducing compliance uncertainty; and better risk management, by supporting prudential requirements that reflect the actual characteristics of each asset.

Three challenges must be anticipated. The first is institutional capacity: the framework requires trained staff, which can be developed through training programmes, regional cooperation (including the Inter-Islamic Finance Regulatory Council proposed in Chapter 6) and phased implementation beginning with the most widely held assets. The second is jurisprudential disagreement over how the criteria should be operationalised. This is best managed by giving the advisory council institutional authority, documenting its reasoning, and reviewing the methodology periodically, while accommodating legitimate differences of interpretation. The third is market pressure to lower the standard, which the independence of the council and the transparency of public scoring are designed to resist.
