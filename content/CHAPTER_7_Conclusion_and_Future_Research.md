# CHAPTER 7: CONCLUSION AND FUTURE RESEARCH

## 7.1 Synthesis of Dissertation Findings

This dissertation set out to answer a single question: how can cryptocurrency be classified and regulated within the framework of Islamic finance? The question arose from a threefold fragmentation identified in the introduction. Islamic authorities disagree about the legal status of crypto-assets. Jurisdictions with Muslim-majority populations regulate them in markedly different ways. And no principled methodology exists for moving from the general rules of *fiqh al-mu'amalat* to a determination about a particular digital asset. Each chapter addressed one dimension of this fragmentation. This section draws their findings together and states what has, and what has not, been resolved.

### 7.1.1 Islamic Legal Framework Resolution (Chapter 2)

Chapter 2 proposed the *maqasid al-shari'ah* as the principled foundation for assessing digital assets. Because the classical five necessities (*din*, *nafs*, *'aql*, *nasl* and *mal*) are too general to apply directly to a financial instrument, the chapter operationalised them through five evaluative criteria. These are stability and the preservation of wealth (*al-darura*), certainty (*al-tahaqquq*), public interest (*al-maslaha*), justice (*al-'adl*) and dignity (*al-karamah*). Each is anchored in established doctrine, but their selection and scoring are the author's construction.

Measured against these criteria, pure cryptocurrency scores 2.4 out of 5. The deficits are concentrated in stability, public interest and justice: unanchored volatility, the predominance of speculative use, and a market structure that transfers gains from later to earlier entrants. Transparency of record and the potential for financial inclusion partly offset them. Asset-backed tokens and fiat-collateralised stablecoins perform substantially better, with indicative scores of 3.5 to 4.5.

The value of this finding is not that it settles the jurisprudential disagreement but that it locates it. The *maqasid* establish whether an instrument is harmful or beneficial in its effects. They cannot by themselves decide the threshold question of whether an intangible ledger entry is property (*mal*) at all, and it is on that threshold question that Islamic authorities now divide. Their disagreement is therefore not evidence of error on one side. It reflects different answers to a prior question that the *maqasid* inform but do not resolve.

### 7.1.2 Jurisprudential Convergence (Chapter 3)

Chapter 3 examined the positions of AAOIFI, Dar al-Ifta al-Misriyyah, Mufti Muhammad Taqi Usmani and the Darul Uloom Karachi, and the Indonesian authorities: MUI, Muhammadiyah and the Nahdlatul Ulama. The analysis found neither uniform prohibition nor general permission. It found a pattern of agreement on one question and divergence on another.

On the monetary question, the authorities agree. None of the bodies examined accepts pure cryptocurrency as money, and in Indonesia the exclusion is reinforced by the Currency Law. Even the Nahdlatul Ulama Muktamar of August 2026, which characterised Bitcoin as *thaman* in the fiqh sense, held its use as currency in Indonesia to be *haram* on grounds of obedience to positive law.

On the asset question, the authorities diverge. Dar al-Ifta al-Misriyyah (late December 2017, reported 1 January 2018) held Bitcoin trading impermissible on grounds of *gharar*, absence of state supervision, fraud risk and volatility. The Darul Uloom Karachi fatwa of 10 June 2026, signed by Usmani and others, went further and denied that crypto-assets, including stablecoins, are *mal* at all. AAOIFI has issued no Shari'ah Standard specific to crypto-assets. MUI's Seventh Ijtima' Ulama (11 November 2021) held cryptocurrency *haram* as currency. As a commodity, it held trading invalid where the asset is defective but valid where the conditions of *sil'ah*, a clear underlying and a clear benefit are met. Muhammadiyah (4 March 2026) and the NU Muktamar (29 August 2026, with specific reference to Bitcoin) have since permitted crypto as an asset, subject to conditions of conduct.

Asset-backed designs occupy a distinct position. They find support in the exception of the 2021 MUI decision and in Malaysia's regulated framework. In Indonesia, the gold-backed GOIDR token graduated from the OJK regulatory sandbox and is listed on both licensed exchanges. Stablecoins, by contrast, are expressly excluded by the 2026 Karachi fatwa.

The temporal pattern is therefore not one of steady convergence. Between 2017 and 2026, positions in Southeast Asia moved from conditional exception towards conditional permissibility of crypto as an asset. In the same year, the Karachi fatwa moved in the opposite direction. The result in 2026 is sharper divergence on the threshold question of *mal*, while agreement on the monetary question remains intact.

It would be inaccurate to describe this state of affairs as consensus, still less as *ijma'* in the technical sense. What can be said is narrower. There is agreement that pure cryptocurrency is unsuitable as money. A significant body of opinion accepts that asset-backed digital assets merit consideration. And there is broad, if implicit, acceptance that assessment must attend to the objectives of the law and to the characteristics of each asset rather than to the label "cryptocurrency". The remaining disagreement concerns not merely the precision of methodology but the definition of property itself.

### 7.1.3 Regulatory Landscape Mapping (Chapter 4)

Chapter 4 compared the regulatory approaches of five jurisdictions. They range across the spectrum from restriction to licensing, as Table 7.1 summarises.

**Table 7.1: Regulatory approaches in the five jurisdictions examined**

| Jurisdiction | Approach | Principal rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Indonesia | Supervision as digital financial assets by OJK (since January 2025), with exchange-level lists of tradeable assets | Consumer protection and market integration; Sharia assessment left to religious bodies |
| Malaysia | Regulated digital-asset framework | Technological innovation and fintech leadership |
| Saudi Arabia | Restrictive; de facto prohibition of crypto trading | Monetary control and caution |
| UAE | Comprehensive licensing (VARA in Dubai; FSRA in Abu Dhabi) | Positioning as a financial centre; innovation |
| Bahrain | Prudential regulation | Financial stability and risk management |

The chapter's principal finding is that this diversity reflects legitimate differences of policy rather than error or inconsistency. The jurisdictions share more of the underlying jurisprudential premises than their contrasting rules suggest. What separates them is a set of second-order judgements about the gravity of residual risk, the capacity of the supervisor, and the weight to be given to financial-sector development relative to investor protection.

Understood in this way, fragmentation has a constructive side. It allows regulatory experimentation and comparison: the Indonesian, Malaysian and Emirati regimes generate evidence on the risks and benefits of licensed markets, while the Saudi approach tests whether objectives of digital finance can be pursued through a central bank digital currency without recognising decentralised cryptocurrency. The Indonesian case also exposes a gap that the comparison makes visible. Legality is not Sharia screening. Indonesian law licenses trading in more than a thousand assets, but no Sharia standard governs which of them a Muslim investor may lawfully hold (§4.1.12).

### 7.1.4 Unified Classification Framework (Chapter 5)

Chapter 5 drew the jurisprudential and regulatory findings together into a five-dimensional classification system. The five dimensions are asset backing, price stability, economic utility, governance and transparency, and recognition by Islamic institutions. Each asset is scored on these dimensions and assigned to a category. Category A comprises assets compatible with Islamic law, Category B those that comply conditionally, Category C problematic assets, and Category D prohibited assets. Central bank digital currencies form a separate Category E.

The central claim of the framework is that compliance is a matter of degree rather than a binary choice between *halal* and *haram*. A dimensional assessment has four practical virtues. It makes the basis of classification explicit and therefore open to challenge. It shows each project which dimension it must improve. It allows different authorities to compare their assessments on a common basis. And it is more consistent than case-by-case determination, although the scores remain judgements rather than measurements. Chapter 4's Indonesian case study adds a refinement: asset backing and regulatory recognition must be scored independently, because the same asset can satisfy the first while carrying a heightened-monitoring flag under the second.

### 7.1.5 Regulatory Harmonization Mechanism (Chapter 6)

Chapter 6 proposed an institutional mechanism for coordination: the Inter-Islamic Finance Regulatory Council (IIFRC). The proposal does not seek a uniform regulation. It establishes minimum standards, defined by the requirements of Category A, as a floor. Above that floor, each jurisdiction may adopt one of three implementation models (full integration, conditional, or conservative). A protocol of mutual recognition allows practical convergence without surrendering national regulatory authority.

The finding is that harmonisation is more likely to succeed through structured coordination than through unification. The proposed roadmap proceeds in three phases, from information-sharing to mutual recognition and finally to integration with central bank digital currencies. It is intended as a realistic path, though its timetable depends on political will that the dissertation cannot assume.

### 7.1.6 Limitations of the Study

The conclusions above are subject to five limitations that should be stated plainly.

First, the study is doctrinal and qualitative. The scores assigned in Chapters 2 and 5 are reasoned judgements, not empirical measurements, and a reader who weighs the evidence differently could reach different scores. The empirical agenda set out in Chapter 8 is a prospectus, not completed research.

Second, several of the authoritative positions analysed in Chapter 3 were read from secondary sources rather than from their primary texts. This applies to the 2017/2018 Dar al-Ifta fatwa, the 2026 Darul Uloom Karachi fatwa, and the 2026 determinations of Muhammadiyah and the Nahdlatul Ulama. For the Indonesian decisions, the source is OJK's summary presented to the 2026 Ijtima' Sanawi. The characterisations are believed accurate, but verbatim citation awaits consultation of the primary instruments.

Third, the reconstruction of AAOIFI's likely position (§3.1.3–3.1.5) is the author's inference from existing AAOIFI standards, because AAOIFI has issued no standard on crypto-assets.

Fourth, the regulatory landscape is moving quickly. The comparative analysis in Chapter 4 reflects the position as at October 2026, and several jurisdictions have amended their frameworks during the period of writing.

Fifth, the case-specific analyses, notably of GOIDR, did not include a review of issuer documentation. Their conclusions on ownership, redemption and *sarf* are therefore provisional.

## 7.2 Methodological Contributions

Beyond its substantive findings, the dissertation offers three contributions of method to the study of Islamic finance.

### 7.2.1 Maqasid Shariah as Analytical Framework

The *maqasid* are an established part of Islamic legal theory, from al-Ghazali and al-Shatibi to Ibn 'Ashur and Auda. Their application to digital assets, however, requires a step that the classical theory does not supply: the translation of abstract objectives into criteria that can be applied to a specific technological artefact. The dissertation attempts that translation in three ways. It converts the objectives into explicit evaluative criteria. It uses a scoring scheme that allows comparison across assets. And it builds a framework that can accommodate new types of digital asset without revision of its foundations. The same method could be applied to other emerging technologies, such as decentralised finance protocols, tokenised securities and AI-driven financial services, without rebuilding the theoretical foundation each time. Its principal weakness, the subjectivity of the scores, is acknowledged in §7.1.6.

### 7.2.2 Comparative Jurisprudential Analysis

Chapter 3 demonstrates a method for comparing the positions of Islamic authorities that does not treat disagreement as error. The method tracks how positions change over time as understanding of the technology develops. It identifies the points on which authorities agree despite methodological differences. And it asks whether a given disagreement reflects different value priorities or different assessments of fact. Applied to cryptocurrency, the method showed that the decisive disagreement in 2026 concerned neither values nor facts but a definitional threshold: whether an intangible, issuer-less record can be *mal*. The method is not specific to cryptocurrency and could be applied to other contested questions of contemporary *fiqh*.

### 7.2.3 Regulatory Comparative Analysis

Chapter 4 moved beyond a catalogue of national rules to identify the policy objectives that drive them: innovation, stability, consumer protection and monetary control. Identifying those drivers makes three things possible. Jurisdictions can learn from one another's experience. Analysts can distinguish areas where convergence is feasible from areas where divergence reflects legitimate differences of priority. And coordination mechanisms can be designed to respect national authority rather than override it.

## 7.3 Contributions to Islamic Finance Scholarship

### 7.3.1 Principled Assessment Framework

Islamic finance has lacked a developed methodology for assessing new financial technologies. The Qur'anic prohibitions of *riba*, *gharar* and *maysir* supply the foundation, but applying them to a novel instrument requires a chain of interpretation that is rarely made explicit. The dissertation offers a systematic, reproducible and transparent framework for that task. Different users applying it to the same asset should reach comparable assessments, and the reasons for any difference can be traced. Such a framework can support the work of Sharia boards and supervisory authorities when assessing new technologies. It does not, however, replace the fatwa of a competent authority, and it should not be read as licensing institutions to dispense with one.

### 7.3.2 Jurisprudential Innovation Support

The framework also shows how particular architectures can be aligned with the requirements of Islamic law: asset-backed tokens, stablecoins with verifiable reserves, and digital assets that generate revenue from real activity. This has practical value for three groups. For Islamic financial institutions, it marks out a segment of the digital asset market in which participation may be defensible. For technology developers, it identifies the design choices that matter for compliance. For investors, it identifies business models that combine economic viability with Sharia compliance. More broadly, it supports the view that Islamic finance is not a doctrine of reflexive prohibition but a framework capable of accommodating innovation that satisfies its requirements.

### 7.3.3 Regulatory Coordination Opportunity

The IIFRC proposal contributes to the institutional development of Islamic finance. At present, Islamic finance is governed largely by national institutions with limited regional coordination. The proposal shows how regulatory cooperation can proceed without surrendering sovereign authority, how minimum standards can secure convergence while permitting variation in implementation, and how institutional capacity can be built collectively. The same architecture could serve other regulatory domains, including Islamic banking supervision, sukuk and takaful.

## 7.4 Policy Implementation Pathways

The findings translate into recommendations for four groups of stakeholders.

### 7.4.1 For Central Banks and Regulators

For central banks and regulators, the first recommendation is to adopt a dimensional classification framework in place of binary determinations. The second is to participate in coordination mechanisms, beginning with information-sharing and joint assessment and moving towards mutual recognition, in order to reduce regulatory arbitrage. The third is to treat central bank digital currencies as a possible alternative to private cryptocurrencies, designed with attention to Islamic finance principles. The fourth is to establish regulatory pathways for assets that meet the requirements of Category A, so that institutional participation can proceed on a sound footing. The fifth is to invest in the supervisory technology and expertise needed to oversee digital asset activity across borders. In Indonesia specifically, the most immediate need is a Sharia screening standard for the assets listed on licensed exchanges. OJK and DSN-MUI are already coordinating a joint study on this question.

### 7.4.2 For Islamic Financial Institutions

Islamic financial institutions should build the capacity to assess digital assets, either through their Sharia boards or through external advisers. They should develop risk management frameworks that incorporate the dimensions of the classification system: asset backing, stability and utility. Where the assessment supports it, they may develop products for qualified customers, such as custody, trading and investment services. They should also invest in customer education on the risks and Sharia requirements of digital assets, and participate in the development of Islamic digital assets and fintech that align with the objectives of the law.

### 7.4.3 For Cryptocurrency Projects

Projects seeking recognition as Sharia-compliant should use the dimensional framework to identify where improvement is most needed, typically in asset backing and stability. They should strengthen their governance by reducing developer control and widening participation, and engage early with the regulators and Sharia authorities of their target jurisdictions. They should also retain qualified Sharia advisers and document the basis of their compliance. Over time, they can plan a deliberate progression towards the requirements of Category A.

### 7.4.4 For Islamic Finance Scholars

For scholars, the agenda is fourfold. The first task is to refine the application of the *maqasid* to digital assets, with particular attention to the threshold question of *mal* on which authorities now divide. The second is to build agreement through comparative work of the kind undertaken in Chapter 3. The third is to engage with institutions, including national Sharia boards and, should it be established, the Sharia advisory council of the IIFRC. The fourth is to collaborate with technical experts and economists so that juristic assessment rests on an accurate understanding of how each asset actually works. Educational materials and academic programmes on Islamic finance and digital assets are a necessary complement to all four.

## 7.5 Future Research Directions

The dissertation addresses the classification of cryptocurrency, but it opens several further lines of research.

### 7.5.1 Emerging Digital Asset Technologies

The analysis has concentrated on established cryptocurrencies and stablecoins. Several newer technologies require separate study. Decentralised finance protocols for lending, insurance and trading raise questions about the contractual characterisation of automated arrangements. Tokenised securities, including sukuk, equities and real estate, raise the question whether tokenisation alters the Sharia character of the underlying asset. Central bank digital currencies raise questions of design. Layer-2 scaling solutions, such as the Bitcoin Lightning Network and Ethereum rollups, may alter the analysis of ownership and delivery. Cross-chain bridges may affect the assessment of asset backing and governance.

### 7.5.2 Maqasid Shariah Application Refinement

The *maqasid* framework itself can be developed further. Future work could examine how environmental and intergenerational concerns should enter the assessment, how the benefit of financial inclusion should be weighed against the risk of instability, and what weight the preservation of national monetary sovereignty deserves. Comparative work could also examine how the application of the *maqasid* varies across the schools of Islamic law.

### 7.5.3 Empirical Validation Studies

Because the dissertation is doctrinal and qualitative, its claims invite empirical testing. Four lines of inquiry are especially promising. The first is the comparative effectiveness of regulatory approaches (sandboxes, licensing regimes and restriction) in managing risk while preserving benefit. The second is which characteristics of digital assets drive their adoption by Islamic financial institutions. The third is how regulatory approval in one jurisdiction affects adoption and price in others. The fourth is whether stablecoins and asset-backed tokens in fact maintain their stability during market crises. Chapter 8 sets out a research design for part of this agenda.

### 7.5.4 Comparative Religious Finance

Research could also extend beyond Islamic finance to ask how other religious and ethical traditions approach digital assets: Jewish *halakhah*, Christian moral teaching on finance, Hindu conceptions of *dharma*, and Buddhist economics. Such comparison may identify principles common to several traditions, which could inform the ethical dimension of digital asset regulation more broadly.

### 7.5.5 Technical Standards Development

Finally, the framework needs technical standards before it can be applied at scale. Precise definitions of each dimension are required, for example of what counts as asset backing and how stability is to be measured. Automated tools could track scores continuously. Standards for reserve transparency would allow asset-backed tokens to demonstrate their collateral. And governance-token models need to be designed that satisfy the requirements of Islamic law while remaining institutionally workable.

## 7.6 Broader Implications for Financial Regulation

Although the dissertation is set within Islamic finance, its findings bear on financial regulation more generally.

### 7.6.1 Religious Considerations in Finance

The dissertation shows that religious jurisprudence can be operationalised as a systematic criterion of financial regulation, rather than being treated as an optional or peripheral concern. Three implications follow. Regulators serving religiously observant populations should address the relevant religious perspectives explicitly in policy development. Jurisdictions with strong traditions of Islamic finance, such as Malaysia, Saudi Arabia and Pakistan, have a comparative advantage in developing finance that is attentive to religious ethics. More broadly, financial regulation can incorporate ethical frameworks that look beyond the maximisation of profit.

### 7.6.2 Stakeholder Coordination

The IIFRC proposal offers a model of coordination for regulators that share values but operate in different contexts. Comparable mechanisms could serve the coordination of environmental, social and governance (ESG) regulation, cross-border consumer protection in finance, and supervision for anti-money laundering and countering the financing of terrorism.

### 7.6.3 Technology-Neutral Regulation

Because the classification framework assesses characteristics rather than technologies, it can accommodate new instruments without revision. This contrasts with approaches that require new rules for each new technology. The lesson for regulation generally is to define outcomes, such as compliance with the objectives of the law, rather than to regulate particular technologies. A dynamic methodology of this kind can assess technologies not yet invented, provided it is revised as capabilities and understanding develop.

## 7.7 Final Reflections: Cryptocurrency and Islamic Law

### 7.7.1 Islamic Law as Innovation Framework

The analysis lends no support to the stereotype of Islamic law as a regime of restrictive prohibition. Islamic law, on the reading defended here, does not prohibit cryptocurrency as a technology. It requires that a given digital asset satisfy identifiable criteria, and assets designed to meet those criteria can be compliant. This finding must be stated with care. It is the position of this dissertation and of a significant body of opinion, but not of every authority: the 2026 Karachi fatwa denies that crypto-assets are property at all. What the analysis shows is that Islamic jurisprudence offers a systematic method for evaluating technology against established ethical and economic principles, comparable in structure to environmental law, consumer protection or data privacy regulation.

### 7.7.2 Convergence Between Islamic Jurisprudence and Regulatory Best Practice

The dimensions that Islamic law brings to the assessment of digital assets correspond closely to the concerns of contemporary financial regulation. Asset backing corresponds to prudential and reserve requirements. Stability corresponds to the objectives of monetary and financial stability. Utility corresponds to concerns about systemic risk and the efficient allocation of capital. Governance corresponds to consumer protection and market conduct. This correspondence suggests that the principles of Islamic finance are not parochial religious requirements. They express concerns that well-designed financial regulation shares, which strengthens the case for their incorporation into regulatory frameworks.

### 7.7.3 Role of Cryptocurrency in Islamic Finance Future

Digital assets may contribute to the future of Islamic finance in several ways. Stablecoins can extend access to financial services for those without bank accounts, and crypto-based remittances can lower the cost of transfers for migrant communities. Digital assets can make cross-border settlement among Islamic financial institutions more efficient. The experience of private cryptocurrency can inform the design of central bank digital currencies, and blockchain technology can make the issuance and trading of sukuk and other Islamic securities more transparent and efficient. These benefits, however, are realised by properly structured digital assets, not by cryptocurrency as such. Pure cryptocurrencies designed for speculation rather than use remain problematic, however sophisticated their technology.

### 7.7.4 Regulatory Leadership Opportunity

Jurisdictions with established Islamic finance sectors have an opportunity to lead in the regulation of digital assets. They can do so by adopting a principled assessment framework grounded in the *maqasid*, coordinating their approaches through a mechanism such as the IIFRC, creating clear approval pathways and mutual recognition for assets in Category A, and supporting innovation through regulatory sandboxes. In doing so they can show that regulation can accommodate innovation while protecting stability, consumers and the integrity of the financial system. Such regulation is also likely to attract projects, talent and capital to Islamic financial centres.

## 7.8 Convergent Practice: Indonesia's Regulatory Developments, 2025–2026

While this dissertation was being written, Indonesia's Financial Services Authority (OJK) developed a body of regulatory practice that bears directly on its proposals. OJK did not adopt the framework proposed here, and the relationship should not be described as one of validation. It is better described as convergence: an authority facing the same practical problem arrived independently at several of the same distinctions. This section examines the points of convergence and the questions that Indonesian practice raises for future research.

### 7.8.1 OJK's Sandbox Criteria and the Five-Dimensional Framework

OJK's regulatory sandbox assesses new business models in the digital financial asset sector before licensing, and its assessment parameters include the clarity of the underlying asset and of the economic benefit (§4.1.8). The sandbox criteria and the dimensions of Chapter 5 correspond in several respects, as Table 7.2 shows.

**Table 7.2: Dimensions of the framework and observed OJK practice**

| Dimension (Chapter 5) | Observed OJK practice (as of October 2026) |
|---|---|
| Asset backing | Sandbox parameters include clarity of underlying; graduates include gold tokenisation (GOIDR, gold held by Pegadaian), tokenised government securities (ID Digital Bonds) and tokenised property usufruct (GORO) |
| Stability | A rupiah stablecoin issuer (IDRP) has graduated from the sandbox |
| Utility | Sandbox parameters include clarity of economic benefit; a non-trading digital-asset custodian (Tennet) has graduated |
| Governance | Supervision of exchanges, custodians and clearing institutions under POJK 27/2024 |
| Recognition by Islamic institutions | No Sharia standard yet applies; OJK reports that it is coordinating a joint study with DSN-MUI |

The correspondence is closest on the first three dimensions. On the fifth it is absent: Indonesian practice confers legal recognition on assets without any Sharia assessment. That gap is precisely the one identified in Chapter 4 (§4.1.12). The correspondence suggests that the dimensions of the framework reflect distinctions a regulator finds practically necessary. It does not show that the framework itself has been adopted.

### 7.8.2 GOIDR and the Asset-Backing Pathway

Chapter 5 proposed asset backing as the principal route by which a digital asset may move towards compliance. GOIDR, a gold-tokenisation model of PT Indonesia Blockchain Persada, graduated from the OJK sandbox on 8 August 2025. By October 2026 it was listed on both licensed exchanges: on ICEX with a "Pass With Notes" flag denoting additional monitoring, and on CFX following a change of ticker from GIDR to GOIDR (§4.1.9).

The case illustrates the asset-backing pathway, but with qualifications. GOIDR was designed as a backed token from the outset; it is not a pure cryptocurrency that was later transformed. OJK's 2025 presentation stated that one token equals one gram of gold, that it can be redeemed for physical gold, and that the model meets point 3 of the 2021 MUI decision. That is a regulator's reading, however; no Sharia authority has issued a determination on the token, so it has not acquired the status of Category A by any formal process. Its compliance still depends on matters not verified from issuer documentation, namely reserve verification, the detailed terms of redemption and the treatment of gold-for-rupiah exchange under the rules of *sarf*.

The case nevertheless offers a natural subject for empirical research. Such research could compare adoption patterns among Muslim and other investors, test whether the token's price tracks gold closely enough to justify a high stability score, examine institutional participation, and assess whether redemption works in practice.

### 7.8.3 Industry Associations and the IIFRC Model

The dissertation proposes the IIFRC as a formal mechanism of coordination among regulators. Indonesian practice suggests a different model at the national level: coordination through industry associations operating under the incentives of a single regulator. OJK's material identifies AFSI, the Islamic fintech association, as the locus of the Sharia segment of the industry. Its members in the relevant segment numbered three in 2024 and seven in 2025, and OJK describes the segment as at an early stage, without a settled Sharia standard (§4.1.7). The two models differ as Table 7.3 shows.

**Table 7.3: The IIFRC proposal and the Indonesian association model**

| Aspect | IIFRC proposal | Indonesian practice |
|---|---|---|
| Institutional form | Formal inter-governmental council | Industry associations coordinated by the national regulator |
| Membership | Central banks and regulators | Industry participants, with OJK as regulator |
| Authority | Binding minimum standards | Regulatory incentives, including sandbox conditions |
| Sharia coordination | IIFRC Sharia advisory council | Joint study by OJK and DSN-MUI (in progress) |
| Scope | Cross-border | National |

The Indonesian model raises concerns of sovereignty less acutely, but it has so far not produced a Sharia standard, and it does not address cross-border coordination. Whether formal or association-based models better achieve the benefits of coordination is a question for future comparative research.

### 7.8.4 Regulatory Sandbox as Continuous Innovation Pathway

Indonesian experience also suggests that regulatory sandboxes serve functions beyond the testing of individual projects. OJK reports 372 consultation requests, 39 applications and seven graduates as of October 2026. The sandbox therefore generates evidence on market demand, on the mechanisms by which business models can meet regulatory requirements, and on the protections consumers need. It also reveals risks that theoretical analysis may miss, such as operational risks in new custody arrangements and difficulties of interoperability. Experience gained in the sandbox can inform the refinement of regulatory standards. For research, the implication is that sandboxes are an important source of evidence, and that academic researchers, not only regulators, should have access to sandbox data to test frameworks such as the one proposed here.

### 7.8.5 Emerging Research Questions from Indonesian Practice

Indonesian practice raises five questions that theoretical analysis alone cannot answer:

1. **Adoption.** Does the Sharia profile of an asset, as measured by a dimensional score, predict its adoption by Muslim investors? Or is adoption driven by other factors, such as volatility and ease of access?
2. **Institutional participation.** Will Islamic banks, insurers and pension funds participate in asset-backed digital assets? If not, what barriers other than regulatory uncertainty stand in the way?
3. **Cross-border treatment.** How would an asset such as GOIDR be treated by regulators in Malaysia or Saudi Arabia, and can dimensional assessments be translated across jurisdictions with different priorities?
4. **Stability under stress.** During market crises, do stablecoins and asset-backed tokens maintain their stability better than pure cryptocurrencies?
5. **Governance.** Do decentralised governance structures serve Islamic financial instruments effectively, or do Muslim investors prefer institutional arrangements such as regulated custody?

## 7.9 Concluding Remarks

This dissertation asked how cryptocurrency can be classified and regulated within the framework of Islamic finance. It established a jurisprudential foundation in the *maqasid*, compared the positions of the principal Islamic authorities, mapped the regulatory landscape of five jurisdictions, constructed a dimensional classification framework, and proposed an institutional mechanism for coordination.

The conclusion is neither unqualified prohibition nor uncritical acceptance. On the reading defended here, Islamic jurisprudence permits digital assets conditionally, contingent on characteristics of design that satisfy the objectives of the law. Asset-backed digital assets and properly designed stablecoins can be compliant; pure cryptocurrencies designed for speculation remain problematic. This position is shared by a significant body of Islamic opinion, most notably in Indonesia, but it is not uncontested. The 2026 Karachi fatwa denies that crypto-assets are property at all, and the future of the debate will turn on how that threshold question is resolved.

The dissertation's practical contribution lies in four things. It offers a systematic methodology for assessing compliance whose reasoning is transparent and open to challenge. It proposes an institutional framework for regulatory coordination across jurisdictions. It identifies the design changes by which a digital asset may move towards compliance. And it provides a reasoned foundation for policy that balances innovation, stability and fidelity to Islamic law.

The future of Islamic finance lies neither in rejecting technological innovation nor in accepting every technology uncritically. It lies in principled engagement: evaluating each new instrument systematically against the requirements of Islamic law, implementing that evaluation through coordinated regulation, and deploying compliant instruments in the service of the objectives that Islamic finance exists to advance, namely financial inclusion, economic justice and ethical practice. This dissertation offers a framework for that engagement. Its operationalisation now rests with regulators, financial institutions, technology developers and scholars.
