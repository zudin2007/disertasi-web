# Chapter 3: Jurisprudential Positions and Islamic Authority Perspectives

## Introduction

Chapter 2 assessed cryptocurrency against the objectives of the law. This chapter turns from principles to positions. It examines how the principal Islamic authorities that have addressed cryptocurrency have applied classical jurisprudence to it, and what explains the differences between their conclusions. The inquiry serves four purposes: to establish what, if anything, the authorities agree on; to identify the areas in which qualified scholars legitimately disagree; to trace how positions have changed as the technology and its regulation have developed; and to provide a basis for guidance to Islamic financial institutions and individual Muslims.

The chapter examines four reference authorities: AAOIFI, Dar al-Ifta al-Misriyyah, Mufti Muhammad Taqi Usmani, and Majelis Ulama Indonesia (MUI). The treatment of MUI is extended to Indonesia's two largest mass organisations, Muhammadiyah and the Nahdlatul Ulama. Positions are classified throughout using the taxonomy defined in Chapter 1 (§3). Position A holds that crypto-assets are conditionally permissible as digital property. Position B holds that they are prohibited, at least under present conditions. Position C defers judgment (*tawaqquf*).

On the verified record as of October 2026, the authorities do not form a smooth spectrum. Two have issued prohibitive rulings: Dar al-Ifta (2017/2018) and Usmani, through the Darul Uloom Karachi fatwa of June 2026. AAOIFI has issued no crypto-specific standard, and the International Islamic Fiqh Academy has called for further study. The Indonesian bodies permit crypto as an asset subject to conditions while excluding it as money. Several of the rulings are available to the author only through press reports or official summaries; where this is so, the text says so, and verbatim quotation is avoided.

## 3.1 AAOIFI (Accounting & Auditing Organization for Islamic Financial Institutions)

### 3.1.1 Institutional Background

The Accounting and Auditing Organization for Islamic Financial Institutions (AAOIFI), established in 1991 and headquartered in Bahrain, is the most widely recognised international standard-setting body in Islamic finance. Its Shari'ah Standards are developed through a deliberative process involving scholars, Islamic financial institutions, central banks and regulators, and they combine juristic analysis with practical application. They represent the mainstream institutional perspective of the industry.

The authority of the standards depends on adoption. They are mandatory in several jurisdictions, including Bahrain, Oman, Jordan, Sudan, Syria and the Qatar Financial Centre, and the Higher Shari'ah Authority of the Central Bank of the UAE has adopted them for the UAE's Islamic financial institutions. Elsewhere they are persuasive rather than binding. Malaysia, notably, has its own Shari'ah governance framework under Bank Negara Malaysia and the Securities Commission. Many Islamic financial institutions nonetheless follow AAOIFI standards voluntarily. [Unverified: the list of adopting jurisdictions should be confirmed against AAOIFI's current adoption data.]

### 3.1.2 AAOIFI and Cryptocurrency: No Dedicated Standard (Status as of October 2026)

**Evidentiary status.** As of October 2026, AAOIFI has issued no Shari'ah Standard, statement or resolution dedicated to cryptocurrency or crypto-assets. Its work on the subject is so far preparatory. Digital assets were the subject of a roundtable at AAOIFI's annual Shari'ah conference in 2023. In March 2026, AAOIFI's Centre for Islamic Studies convened a closed expert roundtable on digital assets, whose outcomes are to inform a forthcoming position paper on digital assets and Shari'ah compliance. These points come from secondary reports and AAOIFI-affiliated announcements; the position paper had not been located at the time of writing.

Some secondary literature cites an AAOIFI "Shariah Standard No. 62 on cryptocurrency and digital tokens" (2021). That standard could not be verified in AAOIFI's published standards, and this chapter does not rely on it.

**Consequence for this dissertation.** AAOIFI is nonetheless retained as a reference authority, because its *existing* standards already contain the rules against which any crypto-asset would be assessed. These are the standard on trading in currencies (*sarf*), the standards on financial papers and on investment *sukuk*, and the general rules on *gharar* and on the object of sale. Sections 3.1.3–3.1.5 are therefore an analytical reconstruction by the author. They show how those existing standards would apply to crypto-assets. They are not a statement of a position that AAOIFI has adopted, and the ratings in §3.1.4 are the author's.

### 3.1.3 Applying AAOIFI's Existing Standards: An Analytical Reconstruction

Read through AAOIFI's existing standards, four principles bear on the assessment of crypto-assets.

**Value and backing.** The first is the classical concern that a financial instrument should either possess value in itself or represent a claim on real assets. The Qur'anic treatment of exchange presupposes goods and established measures of value, and the jurisprudence on money requires that a medium of exchange rest on recognised value. Ibn Taymiyyah grounded the status of money in collective acceptance and convention; the modern institutional synthesis adds the backing of public authority (Chapter 2, §2.0.1). Pure cryptocurrencies have no state backing and no underlying asset, and their value depends on market expectation. On a reconstruction from existing standards, this is a serious deficiency, though, as Chapter 2 showed, it is not by itself decisive under the conventionalist view of money.

**Gharar.** The second principle is the prohibition of excessive uncertainty. Applied to pure cryptocurrency, AAOIFI's general rules would raise concerns of four kinds: uncertainty of price, given monthly movements that can exceed 20 percent; uncertainty of valuation basis, given the absence of an underlying asset; uncertainty of future utility and acceptance; and technological uncertainty arising from the risk of obsolescence. The classical paradigm is the prohibition of the sale of fish in the water or birds in the air, where the object cannot be ascertained. A reconstruction must, however, distinguish uncertainty in the object of a particular contract from uncertainty in the future value of an asset, since the latter is present to some degree in every traded commodity.

**Maysir.** The third principle is the prohibition of gambling. Several features of the crypto-asset market resemble *maysir*: profit derives predominantly from price movement rather than productive activity, gains are transfers from losing to winning participants, informational asymmetry advantages insiders, and outcomes have a winner-take-all character. As §3.4.6 argues, these are largely features of market conduct rather than of the asset.

**Productive purpose.** The fourth principle is that AAOIFI's standards presuppose that Shari'ah-compliant finance serves productive economic purposes. The predominant use of pure cryptocurrency, speculative trading, sits uneasily with that presupposition.

### 3.1.4 Reconstructed Assessment Grid (Author's Ratings)

Applying AAOIFI's existing standards by analogy yields a graded rather than categorical assessment. Table 3.1 sets out the author's reconstruction.

**Table 3.1: Reconstructed assessment under AAOIFI's existing standards (author's ratings)**

| Asset type | Reconstructed status | Principal conditions | Rating |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pure cryptocurrency (Bitcoin, Ether) | Not compliant | None identified | 1/5 |
| Commodity-backed tokens | Conditionally permissible | Physical backing; clear ownership; no fractional reserve; sound custody; transparent valuation; *sarf* rules where the backing is gold or silver | 4/5 |
| Utility tokens (applications, decentralised finance) | Case by case | Real underlying utility and economic value; clearly defined rights; sound governance | 3/5 |
| Fiat-backed stablecoins | Potentially permissible | Full backing; redemption at par; transparent reserves; regulatory compliance; *sarf* conditions on exchange | 4.5/5 |

The reconstruction suggests that AAOIFI's existing standards do not compel a categorical prohibition of all digital assets. Applied by analogy, they assess each type of asset against established principles, and asset-backed designs can, if properly structured, meet them. The reconstruction is, however, more favourable to stablecoins than the 2026 Karachi fatwa, which was signed by a former chairman of AAOIFI's Shari'ah Board (§3.3). Whether AAOIFI's eventual position paper follows the reconstruction or the fatwa is an open question of some importance.

### 3.1.5 Implications for Institutions (Author's Recommendations)

Pending a dedicated AAOIFI standard, the reconstruction implies a cautious course for institutions that follow AAOIFI. Islamic banks should not recommend investment in pure cryptocurrency. They should develop frameworks for assessing digital assets on the basis of AAOIFI's existing standards, conduct a full Sharia review before offering any crypto-related product, and apply robust risk management to emerging digital assets. Individual investors following AAOIFI should likewise avoid pure cryptocurrency. They should consider asset-backed alternatives only after proper Sharia review, ensure that any digital asset investment is consistent with the objectives of the law, and consult qualified scholars before investing.

## 3.2 Dar al-Ifta al-Misriyyah (Egypt's Grand Mufti Office)

### 3.2.1 Institutional Background

Dar al-Ifta al-Misriyyah, Egypt's official institution for the issuance of fatwas, traces its authority to the late nineteenth century and is among the most influential fatwa bodies in the Sunni world. Dr Shawki Allam served as Grand Mufti from 2013 until August 2024, when he was succeeded by Dr Nazir Ayyad; the cryptocurrency ruling discussed below was issued under Allam. The institution is generally characterised by a strict application of classical principles, caution towards financial innovation, priority for the protection of ordinary Muslims from financial harm, and a preference for clear guidance over conditional permissibility.

### 3.2.2 Dar al-Ifta Position on Cryptocurrency (December 2017 / January 2018)

**The ruling.** Egypt's Grand Mufti, Shawki Allam, ruled in late December 2017 that trading in Bitcoin is impermissible under the Shari'ah. The ruling was widely reported on 1 January 2018. Before it, an adviser to the Grand Mufti had already described Bitcoin dealing as *haram*. As reported, the ruling rested on four grounds: the currency is not accepted as a medium of exchange by the legitimate authorities; it is not subject to state financial supervision or backed by the Central Bank of Egypt; it exposes users to fraud and deception (*gharar*, *ghish*); and its volatility can harm individuals and states. The ruling followed consultation with economic experts. In the taxonomy of this dissertation, it is a clear instance of Position B.

**Note on sources.** The ruling is cited here from contemporaneous press reports, and the official text on dar-alifta.org should be consulted before verbatim quotation. A "2019 clarification" of the ruling could not be located. The scope set out in §3.2.5 (investment, holding, mining) is the author's reading of the ruling's reasoning, not an enumeration made by Dar al-Ifta.

### 3.2.3 Dar al-Ifta's Jurisprudential Reasoning

The reported grounds can be reconstructed into four lines of jurisprudential reasoning. The elaboration below is the author's; it shows how each ground connects to classical doctrine.

**Gharar.** The first and most fundamental ground is the prohibition of *gharar*. The Prophet is authentically reported to have prohibited sales whose object cannot be ascertained, of which the sale of fish in the water and birds in the air is the classical example. On the reasoning attributed to the ruling, cryptocurrency exhibits *gharar* in an aggravated form: its price is wholly uncertain, it has no intrinsic value or tangible underlying asset, and its future utility is speculative. On this reading, the *gharar* is severe enough to taint transactions in the asset generally, not merely particular contracts.

**Maysir.** The second ground, implicit in the concern with volatility and harm, is the prohibition of gambling in Qur'an 5:90. Trading in cryptocurrency displays the characteristics of *maysir*: wealth passes from losers to winners, no value is created through productive activity, profit depends on speculation on price movements, informational asymmetry favours insiders, and a participant may lose everything even after predicting the market correctly.

**Absence of value and authoritative backing.** The third ground is that cryptocurrency lacks the attributes the jurisprudence on money requires. The classical treatment of the dinar and dirham, as read by the ruling, presupposes collective recognition, the backing of public authority and a relationship to real economic value. Cryptocurrency is backed by no government or authority. Apart from the brief experiment in El Salvador, it has not been legal tender anywhere. Its value depends entirely on market expectation, and it has no connection to productive assets.

**Facilitation of illicit activity.** The fourth ground, connected to the ruling's concern with fraud and with harm to the state, is that cryptocurrency is widely used for prohibited purposes: money laundering, ransomware payments, the financing of prohibited activities, tax evasion and sanctions evasion. On this reasoning, an instrument whose characteristics facilitate such activities is difficult to reconcile with Islamic ethics.

### 3.2.4 Dar al-Ifta's Narrow Exception

The prohibition is directed at cryptocurrency as a financial instrument, not at the technology that underlies it. On the reading adopted here, blockchain technology may have legitimate uses: record-keeping and documentation, transparent verification of transactions, the verification and tracking of contracts, and administrative applications. The technology itself is neutral, and its permissibility depends on its application. This distinction, which is consistent with the reported grounds of the ruling, shows that the concern of Dar al-Ifta is with the speculative financial asset rather than with distributed-ledger technology as such.

### 3.2.5 Implications of Dar al-Ifta Position

Read in light of its grounds, the prohibition would extend to buying cryptocurrency for investment, trading it for profit, holding it as an asset, mining it for gain, and any other profit-seeking activity in it. This scope is the author's reading, not an enumeration made by Dar al-Ifta. The use of blockchain for legitimate non-financial purposes, academic study of the technology, and regulatory oversight would remain permissible.

The ruling is stricter than the positions of most other authorities examined in this chapter, with the exception of the 2026 Karachi fatwa. Its strictness reflects a protective orientation towards ordinary Muslims, an emphasis on certainty in Islamic law, a concern with speculative financial products, and an insistence on substantive economic backing.

## 3.3 Mufti Muhammad Taqi Usmani

### 3.3.1 Biographical Background

Mufti Muhammad Taqi Usmani (b. 1943) is among the most influential living scholars of Islamic law and the leading figure in the jurisprudence of modern Islamic finance. He received a classical education in Qur'an and fiqh at Darul Uloom Karachi, where he is now vice-president, together with degrees in law and Arabic literature. He served as a judge of the Federal Shariat Court of Pakistan (1980–1982) and of the Shariat Appellate Bench of the Supreme Court of Pakistan (1982–2002). He chaired the Shari'ah Board of AAOIFI for many years, has advised numerous Islamic banks, and has written extensively on Islamic finance and jurisprudence. His positions carry particular weight in the Islamic banking sector worldwide, and he is known for applying classical principles carefully to modern contexts. [Unverified: dates of judicial service and the period of the AAOIFI chairmanship should be confirmed from a biographical source.]

### 3.3.2 Taqi Usmani's Position on Cryptocurrency: The 2026 Darul Uloom Karachi Fatwa

**The fatwa.** On 10 June 2026 (24 Dhu al-Hijjah 1447 AH), the Darul Ifta of Jamia Darul Uloom Karachi issued a fatwa signed by Mufti Muhammad Taqi Usmani and five other scholars. It became public in early July 2026, and Usmani's son confirmed its authenticity. As reported in the Pakistani and international press, the fatwa holds three things. First, cryptocurrency is not *mal* (wealth) in the Shari'ah sense, but a record of notional numbers in an account. Second, buying goods with cryptocurrency is therefore impermissible, and a buyer should return goods so purchased. Third, the ruling extends beyond Bitcoin and Ether to blockchain tokens and to stablecoins such as USDT: "cryptocurrency", "virtual currency", "token" and "stablecoin" are treated as labels for a single category.

Press accounts differ on scope. Some describe a prohibition of crypto-based *purchases*; others describe a prohibition of crypto trading and investment generally. The primary text should be consulted before verbatim citation. Pakistan's Virtual Assets Regulatory Authority subsequently met Usmani and argued that fully reserved stablecoins pegged to national currencies should be treated differently.

**Significance.** No published source has been located for the view, sometimes attributed to Usmani, that stablecoins and asset-backed tokens are "potentially permissible"; the 2026 fatwa contradicts that view as regards stablecoins. On the evidence now available, Usmani's position is the strictest form of Position B. It is restrictive on pure crypto and on stablecoins alike, and its reasoning rests on the denial of *mal*-status. That is precisely the premise that the Indonesian determinations of 2026 reject: the Nahdlatul Ulama holds that Bitcoin meets the criteria of *mal* and *thaman*, and Muhammadiyah that crypto may be *mal mutaqawwam* (§3.4.5). The disagreement between Karachi and the Indonesian bodies is therefore a disagreement over the threshold question of *mal*, not over *gharar* or *maysir*.

**Works.** Usmani's best-known English work on Islamic finance is *An Introduction to Islamic Finance* (1998).

### 3.3.3 Analytical Categories for Digital Assets (Author's Framework)

The categories in this section are the author's analytical framework. They are not attributed to Taqi Usmani. Under the 2026 fatwa, Usmani treats Category 3 (stablecoins) as impermissible. Whether a tokenised claim on specific physical assets (Category 2) falls within the fatwa's reasoning depends on whether the token is regarded as representing the asset itself rather than a notional number, a question the press reports do not address.

**Category 1: pure cryptocurrency.** Bitcoin, Ether and similar assets are not Sharia-compliant on this framework. They lack intrinsic value or productive backing, exhibit *gharar* in their valuation, are characterised by speculation rather than productive investment, and have no underlying asset or utility-based value. Tokens with genuine functional use within an ecosystem may carry somewhat less *gharar* than purely speculative ones, but the difference does not change their category.

**Category 2: asset-backed tokens.** These are potentially permissible, subject to strict conditions. The token must be backed by tangible assets, such as gold, commodities or real estate, in proportion to the quantity issued and without fractional reserves. Holders must have defined legal rights to the backing assets, so that a transfer of the token corresponds to a transfer of the claim on the asset, without ambiguity. Custody must be transparent, with independent verification and regular audit of reserves and clearly defined redemption rights. Valuation should reflect the underlying assets, without artificial inflation and with transparent pricing. If properly structured, such tokens approach the standards of established Islamic financial instruments. Where the backing is gold or silver, exchange against money is also subject to the rules of *sarf* (§3.4.4).

**Category 3: fiat-backed stablecoins.** On this framework, such stablecoins are potentially compliant, subject to conditions. They require full backing by fiat currency or a similarly stable asset, without fractional reserves or leverage, and a guarantee of redemption at par. Reserves must be verified regularly and audited independently, with public accountability for their composition. The issuer must comply with banking and financial regulation in its jurisdiction and be properly licensed and supervised, with mechanisms of consumer protection. The advantage of stablecoins over volatile cryptocurrencies is that they remove the principal *gharar* concern, uncertainty of price, while retaining the benefits of the technology, such as faster settlement, accessibility and programmability. This assessment is directly contrary to the 2026 Karachi fatwa.

**Category 4: decentralised finance tokens.** These require case-by-case evaluation. Four questions structure that evaluation. The first is purpose: does the token represent productive investment and enable real economic activity, or is it primarily speculative? The second is the distribution of risk: are risks fairly shared, do the profit and loss arrangements comply with Islamic principles, and is information symmetric? The third is governance: who controls the protocol's decisions, are stakeholders' interests protected, and are the arrangements transparent and fair? The fourth is compliance: does the instrument serve the objectives of the law, is it free of *gharar*, *maysir* and *riba*, and is the underlying activity permissible? On this approach, liquidity pools may be permissible if structured as partnerships (*musharakah*), lending protocols are problematic if they generate interest but may be compliant if structured as Islamic financing, and derivatives are generally problematic because of *gharar*.

### 3.3.4 Methodological Notes

Usmani's general methodology in Islamic finance, as set out in *An Introduction to Islamic Finance* (1998) and his other writings, has four features relevant to the analysis of digital assets. These features are drawn from his general work, not from any statement on cryptocurrency.

The first is the priority of substance over form. In his critique of certain Islamic banking practices, Usmani has insisted that a change of form or name does not change the ruling of a transaction whose substance is unchanged. Genuine functional differences, however, may affect the assessment. The 2026 fatwa can be read as an application of this principle: relabelling a crypto-asset as a "token" or "stablecoin" does not, on its reasoning, change its nature as a record of notional numbers.

The second is a focus on underlying economics. The questions that matter are what an instrument represents, what economic activity it facilitates, what risks its holders bear, and how profits and losses are distributed.

The third is the analysis of new instruments by reference to established Islamic contracts. On this method, an asset-backed token would be analysed by analogy with commodity-based financing and a stablecoin by analogy with instruments representing currency. A decentralised finance arrangement would be compared with profit-sharing partnerships.

The fourth is a balance between allowing legitimate innovation and protecting investors and society from harmful speculation, while maintaining the integrity of Islamic financial principles. The 2026 fatwa shows that, applied to cryptocurrency, this balance has led Usmani to a restrictive conclusion.

### 3.3.5 Evolution of Taqi Usmani's Position

The only datable crypto-specific ruling located is the Darul Uloom Karachi fatwa of 10 June 2026 (§3.3.2). Narratives of an "earlier restrictive (2019)" and a "nuanced (2024)" Usmani position could not be traced to published sources and are not relied on here. What can be said is that Usmani's 2026 ruling moves in the opposite direction from the Indonesian bodies in the same year: it denies *mal*-status and extends the prohibition to stablecoins. This shows that 2026 did not bring convergence across jurisdictions; it brought sharper divergence on the threshold question of *mal*.

## 3.4 Majelis Ulama Indonesia (MUI) - Indonesian Islamic Scholars Council

### 3.4.1 Institutional Background

Majelis Ulama Indonesia (MUI) is the national council of Islamic scholars in Indonesia, the country with the world's largest Muslim population. Through its Fatwa Commission it issues fatwas on matters of general religious concern, and through the National Sharia Board (DSN-MUI) it issues fatwas on Islamic finance. It advises the government on matters of Islamic law, provides guidance to Muslim citizens, and coordinates with regulators, including the Financial Services Authority (OJK) and Bank Indonesia.

MUI's fatwas are not in themselves legally binding. DSN-MUI fatwas acquire binding force in Islamic finance when they are incorporated into regulation by OJK or Bank Indonesia, and Sharia supervisory boards (DPS) in Islamic financial institutions are required to supervise compliance with them. MUI's influence nonetheless extends well beyond its formal authority: it shapes regulatory policy, the practices of banks and financial institutions, public understanding of Islamic law, and, to a degree, jurisprudential discussion across Southeast Asia. Its views are, however, not the only authoritative ones in Indonesia. Muhammadiyah and the Nahdlatul Ulama, the country's two largest mass organisations, maintain their own fatwa institutions (§3.4.5).

### 3.4.1.1 The Author's Prior Framework: Twelve Sharia Issues in Financial Technology

*Disclosure.* The framework described in this subsection was developed by the author of this dissertation and presented in his capacity as a member of the Sharia Supervisory Board (DPS) of Dompet Dhuafa, at the *muzakarah* of MUI's East Jakarta branch (2025–2026). It is the author's own analytical framework. It is not a decision of MUI, and it is reported here as a methodological antecedent of the analysis in this chapter, not as an independent authority.

The framework identifies twelve Sharia issues that arise when financial transactions are conducted through digital technology (*inovasi aset keuangan digital*, IAKD):

1. the lawfulness and wholesomeness (*halal wa tayyib*) of the object and purpose of the transaction;
2. the contract (*'aqd*) employed;
3. freedom from the prohibited elements of *riba*, *gharar* and *maysir*;
4. the validity of the contractual session (*majlis al-'aqd*) when the transaction is conducted online;
5. offer and acceptance (*ijab* and *qabul*) through electronic media, whether audio, visual or text;
6. the moment at which the contract becomes valid;
7. possession of the object of the contract (*qabd*) and the rights of third parties;
8. electronic signatures (*al-tawqi' al-iliktruni*);
9. standard-form contracts (*'uqud al-idh'an*);
10. option rights (*khiyar*) to continue or withdraw from the transaction;
11. the right to rescind the contract (*faskh*);
12. consumer protection (*himayat al-mustahlik*).

Applied to crypto-assets, the framework separates two kinds of question. Issues 4–12 concern the form of the transaction. Digital execution does not in itself invalidate a contract, and most of these issues can be satisfied by appropriate platform design and terms of service. Issues 1–3 concern the object and the substance of the transaction, and it is here that the Sharia assessment of a crypto-asset is decided. On this framework, the decisive questions are whether the asset is a lawful object of sale with a clear benefit (Issue 1), which contract governs its acquisition and custody (Issue 2), and whether the particular transaction is free of *riba*, *gharar* and *maysir* (Issue 3). These questions correspond to the asset layer and the conduct layer of the two-layer assessment developed in §3.4.6, and to the *sil'ah* conditions of the 2021 MUI decision (§3.4.3). Issue 7, possession, is of particular importance for assets held in custody on exchanges, where the holder's possession is constructive (*qabd hukmi*) rather than actual.

### 3.4.2 The 2021 Ijtima' Ulama Decision: What MUI Actually Ruled

**Instrument and status.** MUI's position on cryptocurrency is contained in the *Keputusan Ijtima' Ulama Komisi Fatwa se-Indonesia VII* (Decision of the Seventh Forum of Indonesian Fatwa Commissions), adopted on 11 November 2021. It is a decision of the MUI Fatwa Commission forum, not a numbered fatwa of the National Sharia Board (DSN-MUI). As of October 2026 DSN-MUI has issued no numbered fatwa on crypto-assets; DSN-MUI Fatwa No. 04 (04/DSN-MUI/IV/2000) concerns *murabahah* and should not be confused with the 2021 decision.

**Substance.** The 2021 decision has three operative points. As summarised by the Indonesian Financial Services Authority (OJK) at the 2026 Annual Meeting (*Ijtima' Sanawi*) of Sharia Supervisory Boards:

1. **As currency: prohibited (*haram*).** The use of crypto-assets as currency is unlawful because it involves *gharar* and *dharar* and contradicts the statutory currency regime (Law No. 7/2011 on Currency).
2. **As a digital commodity or asset: not valid where defective.** Trading is not lawful where the asset contains *gharar*, *dharar* or *qimar* and fails the conditions of a valid object of sale (*shurut al-sil'ah*).
3. **Exception: valid where the conditions are met.** A crypto-asset that satisfies the conditions of *sil'ah*, has a clear underlying (*underlying*), and offers a clear benefit may lawfully be traded.

In the explanatory statements accompanying the decision, the *sil'ah* conditions are summarised as follows: the object exists, has value, is of known quantity, is owned, and is deliverable to the buyer. The exact wording of these conditions should be checked against the official text of the decision before it is quoted verbatim.

**Analytical reading.** The decision is therefore not a categorical prohibition of crypto-assets. It separates two functions: crypto as *thaman* (money) and crypto as *sil'ah* (commodity). It closes the first function entirely. It leaves the second open, subject to conditions. In the taxonomy of this dissertation, the 2021 decision belongs to **Position B on the monetary question** and to **Position A, in a conditional form, on the asset question**. Characterisations of MUI as having declared cryptocurrency "haram as a speculative investment commodity", which appear in some secondary literature, collapse this distinction and should not be relied upon.

**Grounds of the decision.** The reasoning cited is *gharar*, *dharar* and *qimar*, together with conflict with positive law on currency. The decision does not rest on a theory of *riba*.

### 3.4.3 Applying the *Sil'ah* Conditions to Bitcoin

Because the 2021 exception turns on the *sil'ah* conditions, the decisive question for Bitcoin is factual. Table 3.4a applies each condition to Bitcoin's technical architecture.

**Table 3.4a: Bitcoin against the *sil'ah* conditions of the 2021 Ijtima' Ulama decision**

| Condition | Argument that it is met | Objection |
|---|---|---|
| Existence (*wujud*) | Holdings are recorded immutably on a public ledger verifiable by anyone | The "object" is a ledger entry, not a corporeal thing |
| Value (*qimah*, *taqawwum*) | Recognised by markets and by the regulator as a digital financial asset | Value derives from collective acceptance, with no underlying asset or cash flow |
| Known quantity | Protocol supply is capped at 21 million BTC and divisible to the satoshi | — |
| Ownership (*milk*) | Control is exercised through the private key | On custodial exchanges the asset is held by the platform, not by the client |
| Deliverability (*qudrah 'ala al-taslim*) | On-chain transfer settles within minutes | On exchanges, "delivery" is an internal book entry |

Four of the five conditions are met on the protocol level. The contested element is the additional requirement of a clear underlying and benefit. This is where Bitcoin differs from asset-backed tokens. The analysis is consistent with the five-dimensional framework of Chapter 5, in which Bitcoin scores lowest on *asset backing* rather than on legal certainty of ownership. It also implies that custody architecture is a jurisprudential variable, not merely a prudential one: the same asset may satisfy *milk* and *taslim* when self-custodied but satisfy them only constructively (*qabd hukmi*) when held on an exchange.

### 3.4.4 The *Thaman* Question and the Law of *Sarf*

A further implication follows if Bitcoin is characterised as *thaman*. This is the view the Nahdlatul Ulama Muktamar adopted in 2026 (see §3.4.5). Exchanging one *thaman* for another is governed by the rules of currency exchange (*al-sarf*). DSN-MUI Fatwa No. 28/DSN-MUI/III/2002 on currency exchange permits *sarf* only on four conditions:

- (a) it is not undertaken for speculation (*untung-untungan*);
- (b) it serves a transactional or precautionary need;
- (c) for the same currency, the exchange is equal in value and spot;
- (d) for different currencies, it is executed at the prevailing rate and spot.

If Bitcoin is *thaman*, a Bitcoin–rupiah trade is by analogy (*qiyas*) a *sarf* transaction. Short-horizon trading motivated by price differentials would then collide with condition (a).

This is the author's analytical inference, not a holding of any Indonesian body. Its significance is counter-intuitive. Recognising Bitcoin as "money" in the fiqh sense would **tighten** the conditions on trading, not relax them. The commodity characterisation, by contrast, places Bitcoin under the ordinary law of sale, in which profit from price movement is permissible provided the object and the transaction are free of *riba*, *gharar*, *maysir* and manipulation.

| Jan 2022 | Majelis Tarjih dan Tajdid, PP Muhammadiyah (*Suara Muhammadiyah* 01/2022) | Crypto impermissible both as currency and as an investment instrument [Unverified: reported in secondary sources; superseded in 2026] |
| 4 Mar 2026 | Majelis Tarjih dan Tajdid, PP Muhammadiyah (fatwa "Kripto sebagai Aset Keuangan Digital") | Crypto may constitute *mal mutaqawwam*; transactions and investment *mubah* with conditions (lawful object and benefit; free of *riba*, *gharar*, *maysir* and manipulation). Futures, short selling and interest-bearing leverage prohibited. Not permitted as a means of payment in Indonesia |
| 29 Aug 2026 | 35th Muktamar of Nahdlatul Ulama | Bitcoin specifically meets the fiqh criteria of *mal* and *thaman*; transactions as a digital asset are valid. Use as currency in Indonesia remains *haram* because it contravenes the Currency Law |

Three conclusions follow:

1. **Consensus on the monetary question.** Every Indonesian determination since 2021 excludes crypto as a means of payment. In the NU determination, the exclusion rests explicitly on obedience to positive law (*ta'at ulil amri*) and monetary *maslahah*, not on a defect in the asset.
2. **Movement on the asset question.** The direction is from a conditional exception (MUI 2021) and regional disagreement (PWNU East Java versus Yogyakarta) towards affirmative conditional permissibility at the national level of both mass organisations (2026). Muhammadiyah's case is a reversal: its 2026 fatwa replaced a 2022 Tarjih view that held crypto impermissible.
3. **The conditions converge on conduct.** Muhammadiyah's explicit prohibition of futures, short selling and interest-bearing leverage locates the residual prohibitions in transactional conduct rather than in the asset itself (§3.4.6).

As of October 2026, MUI has not issued a revised decision. Media reports that DSN-MUI is preparing a fatwa on crypto-assets remain unconfirmed by any published instrument. OJK reports that it is coordinating with DSN-MUI on a study intended to provide a Sharia-standard foundation for the digital-asset industry, but no standard or fatwa had resulted by October 2026 (OJK, 2026; see §4.1.4).

### 3.4.6 Conduct-Based Prohibitions: DSN-MUI Fatwa No. 80/2011 as an Operational Benchmark

Much of the *gharar* and *maysir* analysis in the literature conflates two objects: the asset and the market behaviour surrounding it. Indonesian fatwa practice already contains a detailed catalogue of prohibited market conduct.

DSN-MUI Fatwa No. 80/DSN-MUI/III/2011 governs the application of Sharia principles to equity trading on the regular market of a securities exchange. It prohibits speculation and manipulation involving *dharar*, *gharar*, *riba*, *maysir*, *risywah*, *taghrir*, *ghisysy*, *tanajusy/najsy*, *ihtikar*, *bay' al-ma'dum*, *talaqqi al-rukban*, *ghabn* and *tadlis*. It maps them onto named practices:

| Prohibited category | Practice named in Fatwa 80/2011 |
|---|---|
| *Tadlis* | Front running; misleading information |
| *Taghrir* | Wash sale; pre-arranged trade |
| *Najsy* | Pump and dump; hype and dump; creating fake demand or supply |
| *Ihtikar* | Pooling interest; cornering |
| *Ghisysy* | Marking at the close; alternate trade |
| *Ghabn fahisy* | Insider trading |
| *Bay' al-ma'dum* | Short selling |
| *Riba* | Margin trading financed by interest-bearing credit |

The fatwa addresses securities, not crypto-assets, and its application here is analogical. The analogy is nonetheless close: every practice in the table is documented in crypto-asset markets. The catalogue also coincides with the conduct conditions in the 2026 Muhammadiyah determination.

The methodological consequence for this dissertation is a two-layer assessment:

- **Asset layer.** Is the token a valid object of sale? This is assessed through the *sil'ah* conditions and the asset-backing dimension of Chapter 5.
- **Conduct layer.** Is the transaction free of the practices catalogued in Fatwa 80/2011? This is assessed per transaction or platform, not per asset.

On this reading, much of what is presented as an intrinsic *gharar* or *maysir* objection to Bitcoin is a conduct-layer problem. The objection that genuinely attaches to the asset itself is the absence of an underlying.

## 3.5 Synthesis: Points of Consensus and Divergence

### 3.5.1 Major Points of Agreement

Measured against the verified record (§3.1.2, §3.2.2, §3.3.2, §3.4), the points of agreement among the authorities are narrower than a simple "consensus against pure cryptocurrency".

**First point of agreement: crypto is not money.** No authority examined accepts pure cryptocurrency as money. The grounds differ. For Dar al-Ifta, cryptocurrency is unbacked, unsupervised and volatile. For Usmani, it is not *mal* at all. For MUI, Muhammadiyah and the Nahdlatul Ulama, its use as money in Indonesia is also excluded by the Currency Law.

**No agreement on crypto as an asset.** On the question whether pure cryptocurrency may be held and traded as an asset, the authorities divide, as Table 3.2 shows.

**Table 3.2: Positions on pure cryptocurrency as an asset (verified, October 2026)**

| Authority | Instrument and date | Position | Taxonomy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dar al-Ifta al-Misriyyah | Fatwa, late Dec 2017 (reported 1 Jan 2018) | Trading prohibited | B |
| Darul Uloom Karachi (Usmani) | Fatwa, 10 Jun 2026 | Impermissible: not *mal*; extends to stablecoins | B (strict) |
| AAOIFI | — | No dedicated standard | — |
| International Islamic Fiqh Academy | Resolution 237 (24/8), Nov 2019 | Further research called for | C |
| MUI | Ijtima' Ulama VII, 11 Nov 2021 | *Haram* as currency; valid as a commodity if the *sil'ah* conditions, an underlying and a benefit are clear | B (money); A (asset) |
| Muhammadiyah | Majelis Tarjih, 4 Mar 2026 | *Mubah* with conditions | A |
| Nahdlatul Ulama | 35th Muktamar, 29 Aug 2026 | Bitcoin is *mal* and *thaman*; valid as an asset; *haram* as currency | A |

The split is not random. It runs along the threshold question of *mal*. The two most restrictive rulings rest on the absence of value or backing (Dar al-Ifta) or deny *mal*-status outright (Usmani). The Indonesian bodies affirm *mal*-status, the NU expressly and Muhammadiyah through the concept of *mal mutaqawwam*, and move the analysis to the conditions of trading.

**Second point of agreement: backing improves the case, though not universally.** The MUI exception expressly requires a clear underlying asset. Indonesian sandbox practice has admitted a gold-backed token, which is now listed on both licensed exchanges (§4.1.9). Malaysia's Shariah Advisory Council permits assets on registered exchanges subject to its criteria. Usmani's 2026 fatwa, however, extends to stablecoins pegged to fiat currency, so that even backing does not secure permissibility in his view, and AAOIFI has not ruled.

**Third point of agreement: blockchain technology itself is not prohibited.** None of the rulings examined targets distributed-ledger technology as such. The rulings concern the asset and its uses.

### 3.5.2 Points of Divergence

**The degree of restrictiveness.** The verified positions on pure cryptocurrency as an asset can be arranged from the most restrictive to the most permissive. Usmani's 2026 fatwa, which denies *mal*-status and extends to stablecoins, is the most restrictive, followed by Dar al-Ifta's prohibition of trading (2017/2018). MUI's conditional exception (2021) occupies the middle. Muhammadiyah and the NU (2026), which hold transactions *mubah* or valid subject to conditions, are the most permissive. AAOIFI and the International Islamic Fiqh Academy have issued no ruling and cannot be placed on the spectrum.

Three factors explain this divergence. The first, and decisive, is *mal*-status (§3.5.3, first area). The second is institutional setting. The Indonesian bodies ruled against the background of a licensed and supervised domestic market (§4.1). Dar al-Ifta in 2017/2018 and Darul Uloom Karachi in 2026 ruled where no comparable regime existed or where one was only emerging. The third is the object of the question. The Karachi fatwa answered a question about purchasing goods with crypto, which is a monetary use. The Indonesian determinations, by contrast, distinguish monetary use from holding crypto as an asset.

**Stablecoins.** The authorities have said little about stablecoins. Usmani's 2026 fatwa names USDT expressly and holds stablecoins impermissible. Dar al-Ifta's 2017/2018 ruling, MUI, Muhammadiyah and the NU do not address them expressly, and AAOIFI has no standard. Among regulators, Indonesia has admitted a rupiah stablecoin issuer (IDRP) to graduation from the OJK sandbox (2026), and Pakistan's Virtual Assets Regulatory Authority has argued that fully reserved stablecoins should be treated differently. The question is therefore largely open, and the one express ruling is negative.

**Decentralised finance and smart contracts.** None of the verified rulings addresses decentralised finance expressly. The case-by-case analysis in §3.3.3 is the author's.

### 3.5.3 Scholarly Disagreement: Areas of Legitimate Dispute

Islamic jurisprudence recognises scholarly disagreement (*ikhtilaf*) as legitimate when qualified scholars differ on valid grounds. Four areas of the cryptocurrency debate fall into this category.

**First area: the requirement of intrinsic value.** On one side are Dar al-Ifta, which treats the absence of intrinsic value and authoritative backing as a ground of prohibition, and, in the strongest form, the Darul Uloom Karachi fatwa, which holds that cryptocurrency is not *mal* but a record of notional numbers. On the other side are the 2026 Indonesian determinations. The NU held that Bitcoin meets the criteria of *mal* and *thaman*, and Muhammadiyah that crypto may constitute *mal mutaqawwam*. On this view, customary value and acceptance can ground the status of property without intrinsic value. The classical treatment of money supports both sides in part. Fiat money lacks intrinsic value yet is accepted as valid *thaman*, but its acceptance rests on general acceptance together with state backing (Chapter 2, §2.0.1). The question for modern application is whether general acceptance without state backing, of the kind Bitcoin enjoys among its users, is sufficient to establish *mal*-status.

**Second area: technological innovation and adaptation.** One approach, which can be discerned in Dar al-Ifta's ruling, holds that classical principles are sufficient and that an innovation which appears to contradict them is impermissible. Another approach, shared by Muhammadiyah, the NU and the Malaysian Shariah Advisory Council, applies the classical criteria of property and sale to the new object rather than presuming its invalidity, and asks whether genuine innovation requires a reassessment of how those principles apply. This characterisation of the two approaches is the author's. Historical precedent favours the second approach to some extent, since Islamic jurisprudence has adapted to paper money, modern banking and corporate structures. That adaptation, however, proceeded by reassessing new objects against old principles, not by abandoning the principles.

**Third area: tolerance of risk and protection of investors.** Dar al-Ifta, and MUI on monetary use, give priority to protecting ordinary Muslims and hold that speculative products should be prohibited to prevent harm. Muhammadiyah (2026), which conditions permissibility on literacy, research and regulatory compliance, and regulators that operate licensed markets take the view that innovation is permissible and that investor sophistication and risk disclosure are appropriate safeguards. Classical jurisprudence offers support for a middle course. It permits complex financial structures for sophisticated participants while protecting vulnerable parties.

**Fourth area: the categorical-prohibition thesis itself.**

The three preceding disagreements assume that cryptocurrency admits of degrees of compliance. The most fundamental *ikhtilaf*, however, is whether that assumption is itself permissible—whether pure cryptocurrency is *categorically* void (*batil*) rather than merely deficient. Because this dissertation's five-dimensional framework (Chapter 5) presupposes gradation, the strongest form of the categorical objection must be stated here before it is engaged.

*Position 1 (Dar al-Ifta 2017/18; Taqi Usmani / Darul Uloom Karachi 2026; the strict camp, including the Diyanet ruling in Turkey and, among Indonesian bodies, PWNU East Java 2021; MUI 2021 belongs here only on monetary use):*
Pure cryptocurrency is *haram* as such, on textually proximate grounds rather than inferential *maslaha*. The argument is a syllogism: (i) legitimate *mal* requires intrinsic value, productive backing, or recognised authority (§3.2.3); (ii) pure cryptocurrency has none; (iii) its dominant realised function is speculation—*maisir* under severe *gharar*, falling under the explicit prohibition of Qur'an 5:90 and the authenticated *hadith* against sale of an indeterminate object. On this reading a *compliance gradient is incoherent*: one cannot be partially engaged in a void contract. This position claims the firmest footing in explicit prohibitory texts (*nusus*) and the fewest inferential steps.

*Position 2 (MUI 2021 on asset use; Muhammadiyah and NU 2026; SAC Malaysia 2020; the graded-assessment camp adopted by this dissertation):*
The prohibition of speculative *use* is conceded, but the inference from *some* prohibited uses to a *universal* prohibition of the asset class is rejected. *Mal*-status rests on customary, storable benefit—as it did for fiat and *fulus*, which lack intrinsic value yet are valid *thaman*—so premise (i) proves too much. *Gharar* and *maisir* attach to the transaction, not the token (*tahqiq al-manat*), so the operative cause must be verified case by case rather than presumed for the whole class. Most restrictive authorities themselves carve out exceptions (Dar al-Ifta for blockchain applications; MUI for crypto traded as a commodity under conditions), which refutes true categoricality for them and relocates the dispute to *where the line falls*. The Karachi fatwa admits no such exception; against it the dispute reduces to the definition of *mal*.

*Methodological root:*
This is a divergence in *usul al-fiqh*, not merely in conclusion: Position 1 reasons from *sadd al-dhara'i* and a presumption of invalidity until a novel instrument is affirmatively validated; Position 2 reasons from *al-asl fi al-muamalat al-ibaha* disciplined by *tahqiq al-manat*. The full steelman-and-rebuttal of the categorical thesis—showing how the framework bounds rather than dismisses it—is developed in §5.5.5.

The timeline does not show a single trend. In Indonesia, positions moved from a conditional exception (2021) to conditional permissibility at the national level of both mass organisations (2026), while every Indonesian body continued to exclude crypto as money. MUI has not revised its 2021 decision. In Pakistan, the Karachi fatwa of the same year categorically denied *mal*-status. Divergence on the question of *mal* therefore sharpened in 2026.

### 3.6.2 Factors Driving Evolution (2017-2026)

Five factors help to explain how positions have changed.

**The evolution of the market.** In 2017, cryptocurrency activity was overwhelmingly speculative and consisted largely of trading in Bitcoin and a small number of other tokens. By the mid-2020s, the market included stablecoins, institutional participation, tokenised real-world assets and applications with genuine utility. More differentiated products permit more differentiated assessment.

**The development of technical understanding.** Early scholarship on cryptocurrency often lacked a deep understanding of blockchain technology. By the mid-2020s, juristic analysis had begun to engage technically with decentralised finance, stablecoins and asset-backed tokens, and to recognise the legitimate applications of the technology beyond speculation.

**Institutional adoption and regulatory frameworks.** Traditional financial institutions adopted blockchain technology, and some Islamic banks explored its applications. Between 2019 and 2026, comprehensive regulatory frameworks were developed in Malaysia (the Securities Commission), Bahrain (the Central Bank of Bahrain), the UAE (VARA in Dubai and the FSRA in Abu Dhabi) and Indonesia (OJK). These frameworks regulate the market but do not, in general, determine Sharia status. In Indonesia, OJK assumed supervision of crypto-assets in January 2025 and began a study on the Sharia compliance of digital financial assets, and the religious bodies issued new determinations.

**Jurisprudential development.** The *maqasid* methodology has become more sophisticated, and classical principles are increasingly applied to modern instruments through *tahqiq al-manat* rather than by categorical analogy. Among some authorities, there has been a shift towards frameworks of assessment rather than categorical rulings. The Karachi fatwa shows that this shift is not universal.

**Institutional pressure for harmonisation.** In Indonesia, the transfer of supervision to OJK under Law No. 4/2023 made visible the gap between the legal tradability of more than a thousand assets and the absence of any Sharia standard. OJK's 2026 study sets out criteria for the Sharia compliance of digital financial assets: a clear asset and benefit; freedom from *riba*, excessive *gharar*, *maysir* and *dharar*; and safe and transparent management. OJK reports ongoing coordination with DSN-MUI on a study towards a Sharia standard for the industry. As of October 2026, however, there is no numbered DSN-MUI fatwa on crypto-assets, and the gap remains open.

### 3.6.3 Direction of Jurisprudential Development (2017-2026)

As of October 2026, the state of jurisprudential development can be summarised under six headings.

- **Pure cryptocurrency.** Prohibition as money is universal among the authorities examined. Prohibition as an asset is contested. Dar al-Ifta and Usmani are restrictive, AAOIFI and the International Islamic Fiqh Academy have issued no ruling, MUI (2021) is conditional, and Muhammadiyah and the NU (2026) are conditionally permissive.
- **Asset-backed tokens.** These are increasingly recognised as potentially compliant, but the recognition comes chiefly from the MUI exception, from Malaysian practice and from regulators. No authority has issued an express ruling on a specific token.
- **Stablecoins.** The only express ruling, the Karachi fatwa, is negative. Regulatory practice in Indonesia and the arguments of Pakistan's regulator point the other way, so there is no emerging consensus.
- **Smart contracts and decentralised finance.** There is no verified ruling, and the field remains open for application-specific evaluation.
- **Blockchain technology.** There is consensus that the technology itself is not prohibited and that the ruling depends on its application.
- **The relationship with regulation.** In Indonesia, the combination of OJK supervision, OJK's Sharia compliance study and the 2026 determinations of Muhammadiyah and the NU suggests that regulatory frameworks can make Sharia assessment more concrete, and that religious authorities increasingly reason with reference to a supervised market. It does not show that regulation can substitute for Sharia assessment.

Several developments seem likely from 2026 onward. Conditional permissibility as property (Position A) is likely to consolidate in jurisdictions with supervised markets, particularly in Southeast Asia. The strict form of Position B is likely to remain influential in South Asia and among authorities that follow the Karachi fatwa. Frameworks of assessment that integrate regulatory compliance with Sharia principles are likely to develop, together with institutional coordination between Sharia boards and regulators. Regulatory design will increasingly be used to distinguish speculative from productive uses. The central question, whether a digital record can be *mal*, is unlikely to be resolved soon, and practice will therefore continue to diverge along the line it defines.

### 3.7.2 Authority Profiles

**AAOIFI as standard-setting authority.** AAOIFI's strength lies in its widely adopted standards, whose general rules on currency exchange, *gharar* and the object of sale apply to crypto-assets by analogy. **Dar al-Ifta as protective authority.** Dar al-Ifta's strength lies in the clarity of its guidance and its protection of vulnerable populations. It prioritises certainty and the avoidance of risk, and it is best suited to individual Muslims seeking conservative guidance. Its limitation is that it leaves little room for legitimately structured alternatives.

**Taqi Usmani as scholarly authority.** Usmani's strength lies in his very great influence in Islamic finance and in the fact that his reasoning goes to the threshold question of *mal*. His 2026 fatwa denies *mal*-status to crypto-assets, including stablecoins. The limitation of the present analysis is that press reports differ on whether the ruling covers only purchases or all trading, so the primary text is needed.

**MUI and the Indonesian mass organisations as fatwa authorities in a licensed market.** Their strength lies in distinguishing monetary use from asset use and in setting operational conditions: the *sil'ah* conditions, an underlying asset, a clear benefit and prohibitions of particular conduct. Their position is conditional permissibility as an asset (the MUI 2021 exception; Muhammadiyah and the NU in 2026) and prohibition as money. Their limitation is that their determinations are not linked to the regulator's listing regime and do not extend to individual assets.

## 3.8 Implications for Understanding Cryptocurrency Classification

### 3.8.1 Key Implications

Four implications follow from the analysis.

**Prohibitions are mostly contingent rather than absolute.** Most of the authorities that restrict pure cryptocurrency do so on grounds tied to its present characteristics, such as volatility, the absence of backing, the absence of supervision and its speculative use. Such grounds leave open the possibility that properly structured alternatives may be permissible and that future developments may change the ruling. The Karachi fatwa is the important exception. By denying that crypto-assets are *mal*, it rests on a ground that changes in market structure would not remove. Even so, qualified scholars can, and do, reach different legitimate positions.

**Assessment is displacing categorisation.** In place of categorical rulings, much of the recent jurisprudence employs a framework of assessment. It evaluates the economic substance of the asset, applies the objectives of the law, considers the distribution of risk and the degree of transparency, and distinguishes productive from speculative use.

**Disagreement is legitimate.** The divergence among leading authorities reflects neither error nor confusion. It reflects legitimate jurisprudential differences, different methodologies and traditions, different assessments of risk, and the proper exercise of independent reasoning (*ijtihad*).

**The evolution of technology and products matters.** Positions taken in 2017–2019, when the market was overwhelmingly speculative, may appropriately differ from positions taken in 2026, when the market includes institutional participants, stablecoins, asset-backed alternatives and supervisory frameworks. The 2026 Karachi fatwa shows, however, that the later date does not of itself produce a more permissive conclusion.

## 3.9 Mapping Disagreement Sources: Jurisprudential vs. Empirical vs. Methodological

The preceding analysis establishes that the positions of Islamic authorities on cryptocurrency show both areas of convergence and areas of legitimate disagreement. For practical purposes, however, it is not enough to know *where* disagreement exists. Regulators, institutions and projects need to know *why* it exists, and in particular whether a disagreement is of one of three kinds. A jurisprudential disagreement arises when scholars apply Islamic law differently, reflecting different interpretations of its principles. An empirical disagreement arises when scholars agree on the law but disagree on facts about cryptocurrency that evidence could settle. A methodological disagreement arises when scholars disagree on which analytical framework should apply, a disagreement that institutional design may be able to accommodate.

The distinction matters because each kind of disagreement calls for a different response. Jurisprudential disagreements require deeper scholarship and dialogue and are unlikely to be fully resolved. Empirical disagreements can be resolved through research, provided the testable claims are identified. Methodological disagreements can be managed through regulatory and institutional innovation that allows different frameworks to coexist.

### 3.9.2 Implications for Framework Development

The matrix suggests that the disagreements are not all of the same kind, and that each kind can be addressed differently.

**Empirical disagreements** can be addressed through the collection of evidence. The five-dimensional framework of Chapter 5 helps by making explicit which attributes scholars treat as decisive, such as backing, stability and utility. It also asks what thresholds they would regard as sufficient, such as a minimum backing ratio, a maximum volatility or a minimum base of genuine use, and how they weigh the dimensions against one another.

**Jurisprudential disagreements** reflect genuine differences of legal interpretation and are unlikely to be fully resolved. The most important of them, over the definition of *mal*, is of this kind. They can, however, be accommodated. Multiple implementation models can reflect different jurisprudential preferences, institutions can apply the framework in accordance with the authority they follow, and regional thresholds can vary to reflect legitimate diversity.

**Methodological disagreements** reflect different frameworks for applying Islamic law to digital assets and can be addressed through institutional innovation. Hybrid governance models can combine institutional and decentralised elements, coordination mechanisms such as the IIFRC can allow different approaches to coexist, and regulatory frameworks can permit several pathways to compliance.

### 3.9.3 Research Agenda Implications

The mapping of disagreement informs the empirical research agenda set out in Chapter 8 in three phases. The first phase would use expert elicitation to establish which claims about cryptocurrency scholars actually disagree on, and which of those could be resolved by evidence. The second would test empirical claims about the characteristics of cryptocurrency: whether market participants perceive backing, stability and utility as scholars suppose, and whether these attributes predict institutional adoption. The third would test methodological solutions: whether coordination mechanisms allow different approaches to coexist, and whether hybrid governance models satisfy diverse jurisprudential preferences. The aim is to convert as much of the disagreement as possible from unresolvable debate into answerable research questions, while recognising that the core disagreement over *mal* will remain jurisprudential.

## 3.10 Policy Implications for Regulators

The jurisprudential analysis shows that, among the authorities that accept graded assessment, there is substantial agreement on the attributes that matter: the importance of backing, the requirement of stability and the need for transparent governance. Legitimate disagreement remains on specific thresholds and methods of application, and the Karachi fatwa rejects the premise of graded assessment altogether. Regulatory frameworks should build on the areas of agreement while accommodating the disagreement. The key finding is that regulatory convergence is feasible on the attributes on which the graded-assessment authorities agree, while flexibility can be preserved where they differ. This permits both harmonisation and legitimate variation between jurisdictions.

**Building regulation on the areas of agreement.** Regulators should set clear requirements for asset backing and establish volatility thresholds for different regulatory categories. They should require demonstrated use cases before approval, and accept institutional or decentralised governance models provided they are transparent and accountable.

**Using the areas of disagreement to allow flexibility.** Jurisdictions can permit different governance models and set different minimum backing ratios according to their regulatory priorities. They can also treat fiat-backed, commodity-backed and algorithmic stablecoins differently. The result is a unified framework with customisable implementation. A jurisdiction whose Sharia authority follows the Karachi fatwa would, on this approach, simply decline to recognise stablecoins, without undermining the framework for others.

**Coordinating with other authorities.** Regulators should take part in a coordination mechanism such as the IIFRC for setting standards in the areas of agreement. They should also conclude bilateral memoranda of understanding with neighbouring regulators, commission joint research on the empirical disagreements, and work towards mutual recognition of classifications in Categories A and B.

**Using research to narrow disagreement.** Regulators should commission research that tests the empirical claims underlying disagreement, for example whether a given backing ratio provides sufficient certainty. They should share the findings with Islamic authorities and adjust their thresholds as evidence accumulates.

The expected outcomes are coherence, because regulation would be grounded in the actual state of jurisprudential opinion, and legitimacy, because stakeholders would see regulation as based on Islamic law rather than as arbitrary. Variation between jurisdictions would be accommodated within a coherent framework, and research would allow progressive convergence. A regulator could implement the approach within about two years. It would map its jurisdiction's positions onto the areas of agreement and disagreement in the first three months and set thresholds in the areas of agreement in the following three. It would harmonise with other regulators through a coordination mechanism in the second half of the first year, and participate in empirical research in the second year.

## 3.11 Chapter Conclusion

### Summary of Jurisprudential Landscape

This chapter has examined the positions of four reference authorities, together with the Indonesian mass organisations, on cryptocurrency. The agreements among them are real but narrow. No authority accepts pure cryptocurrency as money, and none prohibits blockchain technology as such. Among the authorities that accept graded assessment, asset-backed alternatives are regarded as potentially permissible. Beyond these points the authorities diverge, and the divergence is structured. On pure cryptocurrency as an asset, Dar al-Ifta and the Darul Uloom Karachi are restrictive, while MUI is conditional and Muhammadiyah and the NU are conditionally permissive. On stablecoins, the only express ruling, from Karachi, is negative. On decentralised finance there is no verified ruling. The decisive variable is the threshold question of whether a crypto-asset is *mal*. Differences of institutional setting and of the question put to the authority also play a part.

Islamic jurisprudence therefore offers enough agreement to guide practice on the monetary question and on the importance of backing. It also leaves enough room for legitimate disagreement to accommodate different conclusions on the asset question. In jurisdictions with supervised markets, the emerging approach is systematic assessment according to Islamic financial principles rather than categorical prohibition. It is not the only approach, however, and the Karachi fatwa represents a coherent and influential alternative.

### Evolution and Future Trajectory

The positions surveyed have evolved as markets, technology and regulation have matured. In Indonesia and Malaysia, the movement has been from caution towards conditional permissibility of crypto as an asset, with recognition of asset-backed alternatives and the development of systematic evaluation. In Pakistan, by contrast, the Karachi fatwa of 2026 moved in the opposite direction.

Future development is likely to clarify the status of stablecoins and asset-backed tokens and to produce more sophisticated frameworks for assessing decentralised finance and smart contracts. It is also likely to integrate blockchain technology more fully into Islamic financial innovation while maintaining strict standards against speculation. Chapter 4 examines how regulators in five jurisdictions have translated these jurisprudential positions, or failed to translate them, into administrative practice.
