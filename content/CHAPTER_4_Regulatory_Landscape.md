# Chapter 4: Comparative Regulatory Analysis Across Islamic Jurisdictions

## Introduction

Chapters 2 and 3 examined cryptocurrency through the lenses of Islamic legal principle and jurisprudential position. Turning from jurisprudence to regulation reveals a strikingly different picture. Regulatory approaches are fragmented across jurisdictions, policies diverge, and no coordinated framework for the Islamic financial regulation of digital assets exists.

This chapter examines the regulatory frameworks of five jurisdictions that represent key centres of Islamic finance:

- **Indonesia**, where the Financial Services Authority (OJK) has licensed crypto-assets as digital financial assets since January 2025;
- **Malaysia**, where the Securities Commission regulates digital assets and its Shariah Advisory Council screens them;
- **Saudi Arabia**, where the Saudi Central Bank (SAMA) and other authorities maintain a restrictive posture;
- **the United Arab Emirates**, where VARA in Dubai, the FSRA in Abu Dhabi and the Central Bank of the UAE operate licensing frameworks;
- **Bahrain**, where the Central Bank of Bahrain (CBB) applies prudential regulation.

The chapter has five objectives. It documents each jurisdiction's regulatory approach, analyses it against Islamic financial principles, and identifies the gaps and divergences between jurisdictions. It assesses the consistency between regulatory approaches and the jurisprudential positions of Chapter 3, and it identifies regulatory practices that are compatible with Islamic finance. The comparison reveals both challenges and opportunities for harmonised regulation.

**Analytical framing.** The five jurisdictions surveyed here are not arbitrary. Together they span the spectrum of regulatory posture towards digital assets in the Muslim world. At one end is de facto prohibition (Saudi Arabia). Next comes licensing with exchange-level asset lists but without Sharia screening (Indonesia), and then conditional recognition with *ex ante* Sharia screening (Malaysia). Prudential licensing (Bahrain) and comprehensive licensing (the UAE) complete the spectrum. Selecting jurisdictions that occupy distinct positions permits a genuinely comparative reading. Rather than describing five parallel systems in isolation, the chapter treats each framework as a natural experiment in how a state translates jurisprudential conviction, or the absence of it, into administrative practice. Where Chapter 3 mapped the *doctrinal* divergence among scholars, this chapter maps the *institutional* divergence among regulators. It asks a question the doctrinal literature rarely confronts directly: when a fatwa or a school of thought must be operationalised as licensing rules, capital requirements and enforcement powers, what survives the translation, and what is lost? A recurring finding is that in most of these jurisdictions the translation has not occurred at all. Crypto-asset regulation is, with the exception of Malaysia, religiously neutral.

**A recurring tension.** A theme that surfaces repeatedly below is the tension between two legitimate objectives that Islamic financial regulation must reconcile. The first is *ḥifẓ al-māl*, the preservation of wealth, which counsels caution, consumer protection and restraint against the speculative excess (*gharar*, *maysir*) that classical jurists identified as the core hazard of unbacked digital assets. The second is *maṣlaḥa*, the public interest in economic development, financial inclusion and technological competitiveness. This counsels engagement rather than exclusion, on the reasoning that prohibition merely displaces activity to unregulated and therefore less Sharia-observant channels. Each of the five regulators can be read as striking a different balance between these two objectives, and the comparative section (§4.6) makes that balance explicit. The chapter's central claim is that the divergence among these frameworks is driven less by disagreement over Islamic legal principles than by divergent judgments about institutional capacity, market maturity and the relative weight of caution and engagement.

**Method and evidence.** The analysis proceeds by close reading of primary regulatory instruments (statutes, central bank regulations and supervisory rulebooks), supplemented by the jurisprudential materials examined in Chapters 2 and 3. Regulatory facts are reported as stated in official instruments where these have been consulted. Where an instrument has been read only through secondary sources, such as law-firm commentary or press reports, the text says so. Where a framework's practical effect is inferred rather than documented, the text flags the inference as interpretive. Statements that could not be verified are marked [Unverified].

## 4.1 Indonesia: OJK Licensing of Digital Financial Assets

Indonesia occupies a distinctive position among the five jurisdictions examined in this chapter. It is the most populous Muslim-majority country in the world and, until recently, one of the more cautious digital-asset regulators in the group. This combination makes its evolving posture a bellwether for how demographic weight and jurisprudential prudence can be reconciled in practice. The Indonesian model was built on a foundational choice: crypto-assets are classified as tradable assets, not as currency or as securities. Until 2025 they were *commodities* under the Commodity Futures Trading Regulatory Agency (Bappebti); since then they have been *digital financial assets* under OJK. Much of what follows elaborates the consequences of that choice.

Analysed against the *maqāṣid* framework developed in Chapter 2, the original commodity approach reads as a deliberate prioritisation of *ḥifẓ al-māl*: the containment of speculative harm to retail investors through licensing, asset listing and market-conduct rules. Since 2025 that balance has shifted. Supervision moved from Bappebti to OJK, crypto was reclassified as a digital financial asset, and POJK 23/2025 opened the market to derivatives. Indonesian regulation nonetheless remains Sharia-neutral. Sharia assessment is left to religious bodies, whose determinations changed markedly between 2021 and 2026 (§3.4).

### 4.1.1 Indonesian Financial Context

Indonesia has a population of roughly 280 million, of whom about 87 percent are Muslim. As a major emerging economy, its regulatory choices carry weight across Southeast Asia. It has the largest Islamic finance sector in the region outside Malaysia by number of institutions, including Islamic commercial banks, Islamic business units of conventional banks, Islamic rural banks and a growing number of Islamic fintech companies. [Unverified: asset figures and growth rates for Islamic banking should be cited from OJK's Islamic banking statistics for a stated date.]

The crypto-asset market is large and regulated. In August 2026, OJK counted 23.21 million consumer accounts, spot turnover of Rp23.16 trillion and derivative turnover of Rp4.56 trillion (§4.1.11). Thirty-four licensed entities operate in the market, including two exchanges (CFX and ICEX). Retail participation, particularly among younger investors, is substantial, and the government's concern focuses on speculation and consumer protection.

### 4.1.2 Regulatory Framework: POJK No. 27/2024 (amended by POJK No. 23/2025)

**Legal basis and transfer of supervision.** Law No. 4/2023 on the Development and Strengthening of the Financial Sector (UU P2SK) transferred the regulation and supervision of crypto-assets from the Commodity Futures Trading Supervisory Agency (Bappebti) to OJK (Art. 312). The handover took effect on 10 January 2025. Law No. 4/2026, which amends UU P2SK, adds statutory definitions of *LJK Aset Kripto* and *LJK Aset Keuangan Digital selain Aset Kripto*.

**Primary instruments** (verified against the official texts):

| Instrument | Dates | Content |
|---|---|---|
| POJK No. 27/2024 on the Conduct of Trading in Digital Financial Assets including Crypto-Assets | Enacted 10 Dec 2024; promulgated 12 Dec 2024; in force 10 Jan 2025 | Licensing of exchanges, clearing houses, custodians and traders (PAKD); asset listing; consumer protection; market conduct |
| POJK No. 23/2025 amending POJK 27/2024 | Enacted 31 Oct 2025; in force on promulgation | Extends scope to **derivatives** of digital financial assets and strengthens the role of market operators |
| PADK No. 3/2026 | In force 1 Sep 2026 | Reporting obligations for operators |

**Classification: from commodity to digital financial asset.** Under Bappebti, crypto-assets were traded as commodities within the futures-trading regime. POJK 27/2024 reclassifies them. It defines *Aset Keuangan Digital* as "a financial asset stored or represented digitally, including crypto-assets", and *Aset Kripto* as "a digital representation of value that can be stored and transferred using distributed-ledger technology such as blockchain". Crypto is therefore now a **financial asset supervised by the financial-services authority**. It is neither a commodity nor legal tender. Under Law No. 7/2011 on Currency, the rupiah remains mandatory for payment transactions in Indonesia.


**What the POJK does not contain.** None of POJK 27/2024, POJK 23/2025 or PADK 3/2026 contains a Sharia provision. The word *syariah* does not occur in any of the three texts. There is no requirement of verification by a Shariah board, and no Shariah screening guideline, in the crypto-asset regulation; Article 7 of POJK 27/2024, for example, governs the settlement of consumer holdings in assets whose trading has been halted. Indonesian regulation of crypto is therefore **Sharia-neutral**: it establishes legality of trading, not Sharia compliance. The two questions are institutionally separated, and that separation is the analytical core of the Indonesian case (§4.1.12).


### 4.1.3 Key Regulatory Provisions

The regulatory regime established by POJK 27/2024 and its amendment can be described under three headings. The description below is a summary of the framework's principal features, not a provision-by-provision analysis.

**Trading.** Only licensed exchanges and licensed traders (PAKD) may operate, subject to licensing requirements, capital requirements, periodic audit and continuing obligations of consumer protection. Market manipulation, including wash trading and spoofing, and insider trading are prohibited. Spot trading is confined to assets on an exchange's Digital Financial Asset List (DAKD). Since POJK 23/2025, derivatives of digital financial assets are also permitted; CFX lists 49 perpetual contracts, while ICEX had no derivative products as of August 2026.

**Investor protection.** Customers must be identified and the source of their funds verified, with enhanced due diligence for higher-risk customers. Transactions are monitored and reported, and suspicious transactions are reported under the anti-money laundering regime. Operators must provide dispute resolution mechanisms, segregate customer funds and make clear disclosure.

**Business conduct.** Exchanges and custodians must meet operational, technological and cybersecurity standards, maintain business continuity plans, and report regularly. Custodians must manage private keys under defined protocols, reconcile holdings regularly and submit to independent verification. [Unverified: specific requirements, such as insurance and position limits, should be cited to the relevant articles of POJK 27/2024 before final submission.]

### 4.1.4 OJK's Jurisprudential Coordination

**Coordination with DSN-MUI.** OJK supervises Islamic banking and other Islamic financial services in coordination with the National Sharia Board (DSN-MUI), whose fatwas are incorporated into regulation for those sectors. In the crypto-asset sector no such incorporation has occurred. OJK states that it is coordinating with DSN-MUI on a study to provide a Sharia-standard foundation for the digital-asset industry. It is also preparing its own study on the Sharia compliance of digital financial assets and crypto-assets. That study is supported by a survey conducted with the Cambridge Centre for Alternative Finance between late April and 25 May 2026, with 767 existing users and 133 respondents from the Muslim community. As of October 2026 no standard had been issued.

**The fintech precedent: DSN-MUI Fatwa No. 117/2018.** The most relevant existing instrument is DSN-MUI Fatwa No. 117/DSN-MUI/II/2018 on information-technology-based financing services under Sharia principles. The fatwa recognises six model schemes:

- factoring (*anjak piutang*);
- financing of goods ordered by third parties;
- financing of goods for online sellers;
- financing of goods for online sellers paid through a payment gateway;
- financing for employees;
- community-based financing.

Each scheme is structured through contracts specified in the fatwa, among them *wakalah bi al-ujrah*, sale (*bay'*), *ijarah*, *musyarakah*, *mudharabah* and *qard*. The fatwa also defines the prohibited elements (*riba*, *gharar*, *maysir*, *tadlis*, *dharar*) in terms this dissertation adopts (Chapter 1, §3.2.3).

The fatwa does not address crypto-assets, and its models concern financing rather than the trading of assets. Its significance for this chapter is methodological. It shows the institutional pathway by which a new class of financial technology came to be governed by Sharia standards in Indonesia: an industry request, a DSN-MUI fatwa, and incorporation of the fatwa into OJK regulation. A comparable pathway for crypto-assets would require a fatwa on the object (which assets are valid *sil'ah*) and on the conduct (which trading practices are permissible), neither of which yet exists. The author's own framework of twelve Sharia issues in digital financial transactions, set out in Chapter 3 (§3.4.1.1), was developed with the Fatwa 117 schemes in view. Applied to crypto-assets, it locates the decisive questions in the object and substance of the transaction rather than in its digital form.

**The religious determinations Indonesian Muslims actually face.** OJK's framework does not adopt or cite any religious determination. The determinations relevant to Indonesian Muslims are analysed in Chapter 3 (§3.4). The Seventh Ijtima' Ulama of MUI (11 November 2021) held crypto *haram* as currency and, as a commodity, valid only if the *sil'ah* conditions, a clear underlying and a clear benefit are satisfied. Muhammadiyah (4 March 2026) held transactions *mubah* with conditions, prohibiting futures, short selling and interest-bearing leverage. The 35th Muktamar of the Nahdlatul Ulama (29 August 2026) held that Bitcoin meets the criteria of *mal* and *thaman* and is valid as a digital asset, while remaining *haram* as currency in Indonesia.

**The practical effect.** The practical effect is a separation of functions. Legal tradability, determined by OJK and the exchange lists, and Sharia permissibility, determined by religious bodies, are assessed by different institutions with no formal link between them. Islamic financial institutions do not offer crypto-asset products. Sharia-oriented activity is concentrated in sandbox models with clear underlying assets: gold, government securities, property usufruct and a rupiah stablecoin (§4.1.8).

### 4.1.5 Assessment: Strengths and Weaknesses

The Indonesian framework has four principal strengths. The first is clarity of classification. Since January 2025, crypto-assets have been unambiguously classified as digital financial assets supervised by OJK, which reduces regulatory uncertainty and simplifies compliance. POJK 23/2025 extended the scope to derivatives. The second is its focus on consumer protection, through identification and anti-money laundering requirements, transaction monitoring, dispute resolution and the segregation of customer funds. The third is market integrity. Measures against manipulation are reinforced by the exchange-level asset lists, and on ICEX by a heightened-monitoring flag ("Pass With Notes") that identifies assets subject to additional scrutiny. The fourth is an emerging coordination with Islamic finance. OJK and DSN-MUI are conducting a study towards a Sharia standard, and the sandbox has graduated models with clear underlying assets, which could provide a pathway for conditional Islamic participation without disturbing the established Islamic banking framework.

The framework's weaknesses are equally clear. The most important for this dissertation is its Sharia neutrality. POJK 27/2024, POJK 23/2025 and PADK 3/2026 contain no Sharia provision, so a listing on the exchange lists signals legality only, and none of the more than 1,500 listed tickers has been screened for Sharia compliance. The derivatives permitted since 2025 are excluded by Muhammadiyah's 2026 determination and, by analogy, by DSN-MUI Fatwa 80/2011. A second weakness is exposure to regulatory arbitrage. Offshore platforms remain accessible to Indonesian investors, and the global nature of crypto-assets limits the reach of any national regulation. A third concerns innovation. Islamic fintech development in this area is constrained by the absence of a Sharia standard more than by the licensing regime itself, and Indonesia risks ceding leadership in Islamic digital finance to jurisdictions with clearer Sharia pathways.

### 4.1.6 Compliance Burden and Market Impact

OJK's 2026 figures (§4.1.11) show a large and growing licensed domestic market: 23.21 million consumer accounts, 34 licensed entities, and spot and derivative turnover of Rp23.16 trillion and Rp4.56 trillion respectively in August 2026. These data do not support the view that Indonesian crypto activity is dominated by offshore channels. Offshore activity by Indonesian users remains plausible but is not measured here.

The regime imposes substantial costs of compliance. Operators bear licensing and application fees, investment in technological and cybersecurity infrastructure, compliance staff and operating costs, regular audit and reporting, and the costs of custody services. These costs create barriers to entry and limit competition. They also raise the question, relevant to Chapter 6, of whether a Sharia screening layer could be added without disproportionately increasing the burden on licensed operators.

### 4.1.7 OJK Institutional Ecosystem: IAKD and Regulatory Sandbox Implementation

Beyond formal regulation, OJK has fostered an institutional ecosystem for innovation in financial-sector technology, digital financial assets and crypto-assets (*Inovasi Teknologi Sektor Keuangan, Aset Keuangan Digital dan Aset Kripto*, IAKD). The ecosystem is coordinated through industry associations. The figures below for association membership are drawn from OJK material and the associations' own descriptions as of 2025. [Unverified: membership figures should be confirmed for a stated date.]

**AFTECH.** The Indonesian Fintech Association (AFTECH) represents the mainstream fintech industry. It reports more than 350 member companies. Among them are providers of financial-sector technology services (ITSK), digital and crypto-asset companies, innovative credit scoring operators and financial aggregators.

**AFSI.** The Indonesian Sharia Fintech Association (AFSI) represents the Islamic segment. According to OJK, its members in the IAKD segment numbered three in 2024 (efunding, Dompet Aman and ArtaKu) and seven in 2025. Those seven comprise alternative credit scoring providers (Tongdun, Cloudun AI and iziDATA) and financial aggregators (BPS, efunding, Dompet Aman and ArtaKu) (OJK, 2026). OJK describes the Sharia segment as being at an early stage, with limited business models and no settled Sharia standard. AFSI's role is to connect the Islamic finance sector with IAKD innovation, but it does not itself determine Sharia compliance.

**ABI.** The Indonesian Blockchain Association (ABI) represents blockchain and distributed-ledger companies. It reports more than 60 members, including digital asset custody providers and their candidates and banks with blockchain projects.

OJK's broader ecosystem also connects IAKD development with Islamic financial institutions: Islamic banks and rural banks, Islamic cooperatives, institutions managing zakat, infaq, sadaqah and waqf (ZISWAF), and Islamic fintech providers. The opportunities for collaboration that OJK identifies include wider access to financing for unbanked and underbanked segments, mitigation of credit risk through digital profiling, development of Sharia-compliant products based on that profiling, more efficient customer identification through digital integration, and interoperability across platforms.

A business model identical to a graduate's may then register directly without a sandbox trial. OJK's assessment parameters include clarity of underlying and economic benefit.

**Status (OJK, October 2026).** OJK reports 372 consultation requests, 39 applications and 7 graduates. Four participants are in trial (1 digital-asset, 3 market-support) and five under evaluation (2 digital-asset, 3 market-support). The graduates named in OJK's material are:

### 4.1.9 Case Study: GOIDR (Gold Indonesia Republic): Tokenised Gold from Sandbox to Exchange Listing

**Background.** GOIDR is a gold-tokenisation model by PT Indonesia Blockchain Persada. It graduated from the OJK sandbox on 8 August 2025, and OJK states that Pegadaian holds the physical gold serving as its underlying. OJK's presentation to the 2025 Ijtima' Sanawi described the model, then under the name GIDR, as follows: one token is equivalent to one gram of gold, may be traded through licensed traders (PAKD), and may be redeemed for physical gold through the GIDR wallet (OJK, 2025). Of all Indonesian digital assets, it is the one that most directly operationalises the exception in the 2021 Ijtima' Ulama decision, which validates a crypto-asset with a clear underlying and benefit.

**From sandbox to market.** By October 2026 the token was listed on both exchanges:

- ICEX, as *Gold Indonesia Republic* (ticker GOIDR, decree of 30 Sep 2026), carrying the **Pass With Notes** flag that ICEX applies to assets under additional monitoring;
- CFX, renamed from GIDR to GOIDR in the decree of 12 Oct 2026.

**OJK's own reading.** The same presentation stated that tokenised gold "meets point 3 of the 2021 MUI fatwa" because it has a gold underlying of clear value that can be delivered, and it described other sandbox participants (tokenised bonds, tokenised property usufruct, a crypto index fund and a fully reserved rupiah stablecoin) in the same terms (OJK, 2025). This is the regulator's reading of the MUI decision, not a ruling of a Sharia authority. It nevertheless shows that the regulator itself frames the sandbox as a pathway to the 2021 exception.

**Analysis against the 2021 MUI conditions** (author's assessment; issuer documentation not reviewed):

| Condition | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Clear underlying | Physical gold held by a state-owned pawnshop, per OJK |
| Existence, value, known quantity | Satisfied: one token equals one gram of gold, per OJK (2025); reserve verification to be confirmed from issuer documentation |
| Ownership and deliverability | Redemption for physical gold through the GIDR wallet, per OJK (2025); for tokens held on an exchange, depends on whether custody gives the holder constructive possession (*qabd hukmi*) |
| *Sarf* consideration | Gold is a *ribawi* item; exchanging a gold-backed token for rupiah should satisfy spot settlement (cf. DSN-MUI Fatwa 28/2002 by analogy) |

**Significance.** The case shows three things. First, asset backing can move an asset from the contested to the conditionally permissible category under the 2021 decision. Second, regulatory standing is a separate matter: the same asset carries a heightened-monitoring flag on one exchange. Third, the *sarf* dimension of gold-backed tokens needs its own analysis. Chapter 5's framework should therefore score asset backing and regulatory recognition independently.


### 4.1.10 Strategic Development Pillars: OJK's Institutional Approach

OJK's strategy for developing the IAKD sector rests on six pillars. The first is to position OJK as the coordinating authority of the fintech ecosystem, through events such as the Indonesia Fintech Summit and National Fintech Month and through partnerships with industry associations. The second is the development of innovation in the financial sector, through benchmarking of national and regional sandboxes, industrial sandbox models and a "penta helix" innovation hub. The third is engagement with new technologies: sandbox testing of distributed ledger technology, exploration of blockchain-based financial infrastructure, and the use of artificial intelligence and automation, with attention to consumer protection and risk management. The fourth is the integration of financial data through an open finance framework with standardised protocols for data interchange and interoperability between platforms. The fifth is a regulatory approach for IAKD that is risk-based, adaptive, collaborative and balanced, with requirements tailored to different types of innovation and coordinated with other regulators. The sixth is the strengthening of cybersecurity, through best-practice standards and international cooperation.

None of the six pillars addresses Sharia compliance specifically. That omission is consistent with the Sharia neutrality of the crypto-asset regulation itself (§4.1.2) and underlines the significance of the OJK–DSN-MUI study (§4.1.4).

### 4.1.12 Analytical Implications: Legality Is Not Sharia Screening

Four implications follow for the classification framework of this dissertation.

**1. Two regimes, not one.** Indonesia separates the legal question from the Sharia question. OJK decides *whether an asset may be traded*: licensing, DAKD listing and market conduct. Religious bodies decide *whether a Muslim may trade it*: MUI 2021, Muhammadiyah 2026 and NU 2026 (§3.4). No instrument links the two. A DAKD listing is therefore evidence of legality, not of Sharia compliance. OJK itself states that it is still coordinating with DSN-MUI on a study to provide a Sharia standard for the digital-asset industry. That study sets three criteria:

- the asset and its benefit are clear;
- transactions are free of *riba*, excessive *gharar*, *maysir* and *dharar*;
- management is safe and transparent.

OJK also notes that the Sharia segment of the industry is small: Sharia members of AFSI in this field grew from three in 2024 to seven in 2025.

**2. The listing universe is too large and too fast-moving for asset-by-asset fatwas.** Across the two exchanges more than 1,500 distinct tickers are listed, the lists change monthly, and ICEX alone flags 154 assets for additional monitoring. A case-by-case fatwa approach cannot keep pace. This supports the dissertation's argument (Chapter 5) for a rules-based classification that DPS can apply to a list. The DAKD structure suggests a practical starting point:

- the *Pass With Notes* flag already identifies the assets least likely to meet the *sil'ah* and benefit conditions;
- tokenised real-world assets on the lists are the most likely candidates for the 2021 MUI exception (clear underlying).

**3. Derivatives create a conduct-layer problem that listing does not address.** Since POJK 23/2025, perpetual derivative contracts are legal on CFX, and derivative turnover reached Rp4.56 trillion in August 2026. Muhammadiyah (2026) prohibits futures, short selling and interest-bearing leverage explicitly. DSN-MUI Fatwa 80/2011, by analogy, treats short selling as *bay' al-ma'dum* and interest-financed margin as *riba* (§3.4.6). For a Muslim investor, the legally available universe therefore includes products that every Indonesian religious determination that addresses them excludes.

**4. Tokenised gold shows the exception in practice.** GOIDR (Gold Indonesia Republic) is the clearest Indonesian instance of the asset type the 2021 MUI exception contemplates. It graduated from the OJK sandbox in August 2025, and OJK states that the state pawnshop Pegadaian holds the physical gold serving as the token's underlying. By October 2026 it was listed on both exchanges:

- on ICEX, under ticker GOIDR, but carrying the *Pass With Notes* flag;
- on CFX, renamed from GIDR to GOIDR in the October 2026 decree.

Even a backed asset can therefore sit in the regulator's heightened-monitoring category. Asset backing (Chapter 5, Dimension 1) and regulatory standing (Dimension 5) are independent variables and must be scored separately.

## 4.2 Malaysia: Securities Commission Licensing with Sharia Screening

If Indonesia's framework is organised around licensing without Sharia screening, Malaysia's is organised around *conditional recognition*. Digital assets are admitted into the regulated capital market, and those offered as Sharia-compliant must pass through a Sharia filter administered by the Shariah Advisory Council of the Securities Commission. This is a materially different regulatory technology, and its significance for the dissertation's argument is considerable. Malaysia demonstrates that a Muslim-majority state can operationalise Islamic legal scrutiny not as a barrier to participation but as a gateway to it, converting an abstract jurisprudential judgment into an enumerated universe of screened, tradable assets. The Malaysian approach thus functions as a working proof of concept for the *ex ante* screening logic that the dissertation's proposed framework (Chapters 5 and 6) generalises. The subsections that follow examine both the mechanics of that apparatus and the gaps that continue to constrain its reach: limited asset coverage, uneven user awareness and cross-border arbitrage.

The competent regulator for digital assets in Malaysia is the Securities Commission (SC). Bank Negara Malaysia (BNM) is responsible for banking, payments and the status of legal tender.

### 4.2.1 Malaysian Financial Context

Malaysia is one of the world's most developed Islamic finance markets, with a comprehensive legal and Sharia governance framework for Islamic banking (under BNM) and the Islamic capital market (under the SC). Islamic banking accounts for a substantial share of the domestic banking system, and Malaysia is a leading issuer of sukuk. [Unverified: market-share and asset figures should be cited from BNM and SC statistics for a stated date.]

Malaysia's regulated digital asset market is growing. According to press reports of official data, regulated trading grew from RM13.93 billion in 2024 to RM17.14 billion in 2025. [Unverified: secondary reports.] The number of users is less well documented, and estimates should be treated with caution.

### 4.2.2 Regulatory Framework: The Securities Commission's Digital Asset Regime

Malaysia regulates digital assets as part of its capital market. Under the Capital Markets and Services (Prescription of Securities) (Digital Currency and Digital Token) Order 2019, digital currencies and digital tokens are prescribed as securities, which brings them within the SC's jurisdiction. The SC's guidelines on digital assets establish three categories of regulated intermediary:

- digital asset exchanges (DAX);
- initial exchange offering platforms;
- digital asset custodians.

No person may offer, issue or distribute digital assets in Malaysia without registration with, or authorisation by, the SC. BNM, for its part, has stated that digital assets are not legal tender in Malaysia. [Unverified: the title and dates of the SC's current guidelines on digital assets should be confirmed against the SC's published instruments.]

### 4.2.3 Key Regulatory Provisions

**Licensing and supervision of intermediaries.** Registered exchanges and custodians are subject to requirements on capital, governance, risk management, operational resilience and technology and cybersecurity standards. In operation they must conduct market surveillance, monitor transactions and report suspicious activity, identify customers and segregate customer assets. The SC supervises them through examination and reporting and may take enforcement action.

**Sharia screening.** The distinctive feature of the Malaysian framework is its integration of Sharia assessment. In 2020 the SC's Shariah Advisory Council resolved that investing and trading in digital currencies and tokens on registered exchanges is permissible in principle, subject to its requirements. The Council maintains a list of digital assets it has endorsed as Sharia-compliant; Stellar, for example, was added following a meeting in December 2024. On 30 March 2026 the SC revised its Guidelines on Islamic Capital Market Products and Services to require exchanges that offer a Sharia-compliant digital currency to obtain the Council's endorsement before offering it. Islamic legal assessment thus operates within the supervisory apparatus, not outside it. [Unverified: the 2026 revision is cited from a law-firm alert and should be confirmed against the published guidelines.]

**Stablecoins and tokens.** Digital tokens, including those representing assets or utility, fall within the SC's framework, and tokenised securities are subject to the ordinary securities law. The treatment of stablecoins is developing. [Unverified: the current position on stablecoins should be confirmed.]

### 4.2.4 Regulatory Sandbox and Innovation

Malaysia supports innovation through regulatory sandboxes. BNM operates a financial technology sandbox for products within its remit, and the SC has its own facilitation channels for capital market innovation. These arrangements offer time-limited regulatory relief, a supervised environment for testing new products, and a pathway to full authorisation. They have been used for Islamic fintech, tokenisation and cross-border payment initiatives. [Unverified: specific digital-asset sandbox participants should be cited.]

### 4.2.5 Assessment: Strengths and Weaknesses

The Malaysian framework has four main strengths. The first is the integration of Sharia assessment into the supervisory process. Because an authoritative Sharia body screens assets before they are offered as compliant, investors receive a clear public signal, and the problem of legality without Sharia assessment that characterises Indonesia does not arise. The second is the coherence of placing digital assets within an established capital market regime, with mature rules on licensing, conduct and enforcement. The third is support for innovation through sandboxes and a clear pathway to authorisation. The fourth is market development: the regulated market has grown, and Malaysia has positioned itself as a hub of Islamic digital finance.

Its weaknesses are also significant. The universe of screened assets is narrow relative to the global market, which leaves an incentive to trade unscreened assets offshore. Public understanding of what Sharia endorsement means, and of its limits, is uneven. Cross-border coordination remains limited, so that assets screened in Malaysia have no recognised status elsewhere. The criteria of screening, though public in outline, are not always explained in detail for individual assets. And the framework's treatment of stablecoins and of more complex products, such as derivatives, is still developing.

### 4.2.6 Market Impact and Development

Malaysia's approach has contributed to the development of a licensed exchange sector, a growing fintech and blockchain ecosystem, and institutional interest in digital assets. Its most important contribution, from the perspective of this dissertation, is demonstrative. It shows that a public Sharia authority can maintain an enumerated list of digital assets endorsed for trading, and that a regulator can make that endorsement a condition of offering an asset as compliant. That is the model that Chapters 5 and 6 seek to generalise and to coordinate across jurisdictions.

## 4.3 Saudi Arabia: SAMA Restrictive Regulatory Approach

Saudi Arabia anchors the restrictive pole of the regulatory spectrum. Its framework is theoretically important precisely because it cannot be dismissed as jurisprudentially unsophisticated. Saudi Arabia has a deep tradition of Islamic legal scholarship and a large Islamic banking sector. Yet it has chosen to withhold recognition from crypto-assets rather than to supervise them. Reading Saudi Arabia alongside the UAE, two Gulf states with comparable Islamic legal traditions that have reached opposite regulatory conclusions, is the sharpest available demonstration of this chapter's central claim: the divergence among frameworks is driven less by disagreement over fiqh than by divergent judgments about risk and institutional capacity. The Saudi framework also foregrounds the enforcement question raised in §4.6.4. A prohibition protects consumers only to the extent that the state can make it real. The discussion below, including the central bank's experiments with digital currency, is best read as an account of how a jurisdiction pursues the objectives of digital finance while withholding recognition from decentralised cryptocurrency itself.

### 4.3.1 Saudi Arabian Financial Context

Saudi Arabia is home to Islam's holiest cities and to the Islamic Development Bank (Jeddah), and it has one of the world's largest Islamic banking sectors. AAOIFI, by contrast, is headquartered in Bahrain. The Saudi Central Bank, which retains the acronym SAMA from its former name (Saudi Arabian Monetary Authority), supervises banking and payments, and the Capital Market Authority supervises securities. Retail participation in crypto-assets is not permitted through licensed domestic channels. The government's concerns focus on monetary control, financial stability and the prevention of fraud.

### 4.3.2 Regulatory Framework: Non-Recognition and Official Warnings

Saudi Arabia has no comprehensive licensing framework for crypto-assets. Its posture rests instead on official warnings and on the absence of licensing. In 2018 the government's Standing Committee for awareness of unauthorised Forex activity, on which SAMA is represented, warned that virtual currencies such as Bitcoin are not regulated in the Kingdom, are not legal tender, and are illegal in the sense that no party is licensed to deal in them. It cautioned the public against dealing in them. The Ministry of Finance later warned against dealing or investing in virtual currencies, which it said are not recognised by legal entities in the Kingdom.

The practical effect is a de facto prohibition of domestic crypto-asset services. No exchange is licensed to operate, financial institutions do not offer crypto-asset products, and residents who wish to transact must use offshore platforms outside Saudi supervision. Blockchain technology for other purposes and research on central bank digital currency are, by contrast, actively pursued.

[Unverified: no comprehensive SAMA framework for crypto-assets has been located; the current, 2026 position of SAMA and the Capital Market Authority should be confirmed from their official websites; reports since 2022 suggest SAMA has established a programme on virtual assets and CBDC, which may signal a changing posture.]

### 4.3.3 SAMA's Explicit Positions

The official position, as stated in the warnings of 2018 and subsequent statements, can be summarised in four points. Virtual currencies are not legal tender in Saudi Arabia. No party is licensed to deal in them. Marketing virtual currencies using the Kingdom's name, currency or emblems is subject to legal action. And the public is warned against dealing or investing in them. The statements do not, so far as has been established, impose an express criminal prohibition on individual holding. The practical effect is that Saudi residents cannot trade crypto-assets through licensed domestic platforms, cannot use domestic financial institutions for that purpose, and can participate only through offshore channels, at their own risk.

### 4.3.4 Rationale for Restrictive Approach

The rationale for the Saudi posture can be reconstructed from official statements and from the general policy of the Saudi authorities. This reconstruction is the author's.

The first consideration is financial stability. The volatility of crypto-assets, large-scale speculation and the potential for leverage and contagion are seen as risks to the financial system and the foreign exchange market. The second is consumer protection: retail investors are vulnerable to manipulation and fraud and to high losses. The third is monetary control. Crypto-assets could weaken the transmission of monetary policy and the riyal's peg to the dollar, and could provide channels for capital flight. The fourth is Sharia, though no Saudi fatwa body has issued a formal crypto-asset ruling of the kind examined in Chapter 3. Members of the Council of Senior Scholars have expressed reservations, and a restrictive reading of general principles concerning *maysir*, *gharar* and the absence of intrinsic value is consistent with the posture (Chapter 1, §3.2.3). The fifth is the prevention of money laundering, terrorist financing and sanctions evasion.

### 4.3.5 CBDC Alternative Approach

Instead of permitting private crypto-assets, Saudi Arabia has pursued central bank digital currency. In 2019–2020 SAMA and the Central Bank of the UAE conducted Project Aber, a wholesale experiment in cross-border settlement using a shared digital currency. In 2024 SAMA joined Project mBridge, a multi-CBDC platform for cross-border payments involving the central banks of China, Hong Kong, Thailand and the UAE. [Unverified: dates of participation should be confirmed.] A state-issued digital currency offers, from the authorities' perspective, the technological benefits of digital settlement while preserving monetary control, legal certainty and supervision. Whether a retail digital riyal will be issued, and how its design would be assessed under Islamic law, remain open questions.

### 4.3.6 Assessment: Strengths and Weaknesses

The Saudi approach has real strengths. It reduces the exposure of the domestic financial system to crypto-asset risk, protects retail investors who rely on domestic channels from speculative losses and fraud, and preserves monetary control and the riyal's peg. It is also consistent with the strict jurisprudential positions examined in Chapter 3. And it provides regulatory clarity of a kind: the absence of licensing is unambiguous.

Its weaknesses are equally clear. It forgoes the development of a domestic industry and may encourage fintech talent and capital to move to more permissive neighbours. It does not prevent residents from reaching offshore platforms, where they enjoy no Saudi protection and where activity escapes supervision. In that respect it exports rather than eliminates the activity it disfavours. It also limits the exploration of Sharia-compliant digital assets within the Kingdom. Finally, a central bank digital currency is not a substitute for all the functions that private digital assets perform, and it raises its own concerns about privacy and state control.

### 4.3.7 Regional Influence and Implications

Given its economic weight and its religious standing, Saudi Arabia's restrictive posture influences the approaches of other jurisdictions in the region and the perception of crypto-assets in Islamic finance more broadly. A restrictive stance in a major Islamic finance centre may slow the development of Sharia-compliant digital assets. If Saudi Arabia were to move towards a licensing regime, as some reports since 2022 suggest it may be considering, the regional balance could shift significantly.

## 4.4 UAE: Comprehensive Licensing (VARA, FSRA and CBUAE)

The United Arab Emirates occupies the permissive pole of the spectrum and offers the most fully elaborated licensing architecture of the five jurisdictions. Its importance to the dissertation is twofold. First, it shows that a jurisdiction with an established Islamic finance sector can build a comprehensive regime for virtual assets. That regime, however, is religiously neutral. It does not itself screen assets for Sharia compliance, and the Emirati case therefore illustrates both the possibilities and the limits of licensing without Sharia integration. Second, the UAE illustrates the harm-reduction theory of consumer protection in its most developed institutional form. On that theory, supervised access, hedged by limits on leverage, custody rules and obligations of disclosure, protects investors more reliably than an unenforceable ban. The subsections below trace how the Emirati regimes relate to the multi-dimensional framework proposed in Chapter 5 and identify the gaps that remain.

### 4.4.1 UAE Financial and Political Context

The UAE has positioned itself as the leading hub of fintech and digital assets in the Middle East, competing with Singapore and Hong Kong for international firms and talent. Regulation is divided among several authorities:

- the Virtual Assets Regulatory Authority (VARA) in the Emirate of Dubai outside the financial free zones;
- the Financial Services Regulatory Authority (FSRA) in the Abu Dhabi Global Market;
- the Dubai Financial Services Authority (DFSA) in the Dubai International Financial Centre;
- the Securities and Commodities Authority (SCA) at the federal level;
- the Central Bank of the UAE (CBUAE) for payments and banking.

The UAE also has a substantial Islamic finance sector. Since 2018 the CBUAE's Higher Sharia Authority has required Islamic financial institutions to follow AAOIFI's Shari'ah Standards. [Unverified: the date and scope of adoption should be confirmed.]

### 4.4.2 Regulatory Framework: VARA Regulations (2023) and the CBUAE Payment Token Services Regulation (2024)

VARA was established under Dubai Law No. 4 of 2022 and issued its Virtual Assets and Related Activities Regulations, together with activity-specific rulebooks, in 2023. Its licensing is activity-based. Separate rulebooks cover:

- advisory services;
- broker-dealer services;
- custody;
- exchange services;
- lending and borrowing;
- management and investment;
- transfer and settlement;
- the issuance of virtual assets.

Each rulebook carries requirements on capital, governance, risk management, technology and market conduct. The FSRA has operated a framework for crypto-asset activities in ADGM since 2018, and the DFSA regulates crypto tokens in the DIFC.

At the federal level, the CBUAE's Payment Token Services Regulation, published in June 2024, governs the issuance, conversion, custody and transfer of payment tokens. It distinguishes dirham payment tokens from foreign payment tokens. In general, only licensed dirham-backed tokens may be used for payments for goods and services in the UAE, while foreign stablecoins remain available for investment. The CBUAE approved the first licensed dirham-backed stablecoin in December 2024. [Unverified: details of the regulation are cited from law-firm commentary and press reports.]

### 4.4.3 Key Regulatory Provisions

The Emirati regimes share three groups of provisions. The first concerns licensing: requirements on capital adequacy, governance and the qualifications of board members, risk management, operational resilience, technology and cybersecurity, and the protection of client assets, with additional requirements for custody. The second concerns consumer protection and market conduct. It includes customer identification and due diligence, verification of the source of funds and of beneficial ownership, prohibitions of manipulation, insider dealing and deceptive practices, fair and orderly market requirements, complaint procedures and, for retail clients, stricter suitability and disclosure requirements. The third concerns the treatment of particular assets and activities: stablecoins (under the CBUAE regulation for payment use), security tokens (under securities law), and higher-risk products, which are subject to tighter conditions.

**Islamic finance and the Emirati regimes.** None of the Emirati virtual asset regimes requires Sharia review of virtual assets, and none maintains a Sharia board or a category of "Islamic digital assets". Sharia considerations enter the Emirati market in two ways. An Islamic financial institution that offers a digital-asset product must satisfy its own Sharia governance under the CBUAE's framework. And issuers may voluntarily obtain Sharia certification for products marketed as compliant. The Emirati model is therefore one of licensing with private, not public, Sharia assessment.

### 4.4.4 Innovation and Regulatory Sandbox

The UAE supports innovation through several channels: the ADGM RegLab, the DFSA innovation testing licence, the CBUAE's sandbox, and VARA's own arrangements for new activities. These offer time-limited relief, supervised testing and a pathway to full licensing. Tokenisation, including the tokenisation of sukuk and other Islamic instruments, has been among the areas explored. [Unverified: specific Islamic tokenisation projects in the UAE should be cited.]

### 4.4.5 Distinctive Features: Licensing Infrastructure for Islamic Products

The UAE's distinctive contribution to Islamic digital finance lies less in regulation specific to Islamic finance than in the breadth of its licensing infrastructure. Because the regimes admit a wide range of virtual asset activities under supervision, Islamic financial institutions and issuers can develop Sharia-compliant products within a licensed environment. Examples include tokenised sukuk, asset-backed tokens and dirham-backed stablecoins. Sharia governance is supplied by the institutions themselves, under the AAOIFI standards that the CBUAE has adopted for Islamic financial institutions. What the UAE lacks, by comparison with Malaysia, is a public Sharia authority that screens virtual assets and signals their status to investors.

### 4.4.6 Assessment: Strengths and Weaknesses

The Emirati approach has evident strengths. Its licensing regimes are comprehensive and activity-specific, with a clear pathway from testing to full licensing. Its approach to consumer protection is graduated, with stricter rules for retail clients. It has attracted major international firms and talent, building a deep ecosystem. And its payment-token regulation creates a clear framework for dirham-backed stablecoins, which corresponds closely to the conditions a Sharia analysis of fiat-backed stablecoins would impose (Chapter 3, §3.3.3).

Its weaknesses are partly institutional and partly specific to Islamic finance. The multiplicity of regulators creates complexity and some risk of inconsistency. The rapid growth of the market strains supervisory capacity and enforcement experience. Above all, from the perspective of this dissertation, the regimes are Sharia-neutral. Investors seeking Sharia-compliant exposure must rely on private certification or on the Sharia boards of individual institutions, and there is no public reference point comparable to Malaysia's.

### 4.4.7 Market Impact and Regional Leadership

The UAE's approach has made it the leading digital asset hub in the region. Major international exchanges have established operations there, a significant fintech ecosystem has developed, and institutional participation is growing. Its success is, in part, activity that more restrictive jurisdictions have declined to license and thereby ceded. Whether the UAE also becomes a centre of *Islamic* digital finance depends on whether public or industry-wide Sharia standards emerge to complement its licensing infrastructure.

## 4.5 Bahrain: CBB Prudential Regulation Framework

Bahrain completes the survey with a framework best characterised as *prudential*. It neither prohibits nor freely admits digital assets; instead it conditions their entry on the same disciplines of safety and soundness that govern the rest of the regulated financial sector. Bahrain hosts the headquarters of AAOIFI, and the CBB requires its Islamic financial institutions to follow AAOIFI's standards. Its crypto-asset regulation is nonetheless prudential rather than Sharia-based. Its recent rules on stablecoins, emphasising reserves and redemption, nevertheless sit close to the classical jurisprudential concern that a permissible digital instrument be tethered to real value rather than to speculation. Bahrain is therefore an instructive counterpoint to both poles of the spectrum. It is more accommodating than Saudi Arabia, yet more conservative than the UAE, and it shows how caution can be expressed through requirements on capital, custody and reserves rather than through exclusion.

### 4.5.1 Bahrain Financial Context

Bahrain is a long-established centre of Islamic finance, home to AAOIFI and to many Islamic banks, with a sophisticated regulatory environment. Its crypto-asset market is small but regulated, with institutional as well as retail participation through licensed providers.

### 4.5.2 Regulatory Framework: CBB Rulebook Crypto-Asset and Stablecoin Modules

The CBB regulates crypto-asset services under the crypto-asset module of Volume 6 (Capital Markets) of its Rulebook. The module licenses and supervises providers of crypto-asset services, including trading, custody and advice. In July 2025 the CBB added a Stablecoin Issuance and Offering (SIO) module. It prohibits any person from issuing or offering stablecoins to the public in or from Bahrain without a CBB licence. It requires issuers to match issuance and redemption with movements in reserves, to hold high-quality liquid reserve assets, and to honour legitimate redemption requests generally within five business days. Applicants must have at least three years of satisfactory track record. The CBB has since granted in-principle approval to a first issuer under the module. [Unverified: the date and contents of the original crypto-asset module, and the identity of the approved issuer, should be confirmed from the CBB Rulebook and announcements.]

### 4.5.3 Key Regulatory Provisions

The Bahraini framework applies the disciplines of prudential regulation to crypto-asset service providers. These include capital requirements scaled to risk, risk management frameworks, internal controls, internal and external audit, and requirements on governance, management expertise and fitness and propriety. They extend to standards of technology and cybersecurity, business continuity and incident reporting. Client assets must be segregated and protected in insolvency, with clear legal ownership and regular reconciliation, and market conduct rules prohibit manipulation, insider dealing and deceptive practices.

**Islamic finance and the Bahraini regime.** The crypto-asset and stablecoin modules do not require Sharia review. Islamic financial institutions in Bahrain are subject to AAOIFI's standards and to their own Sharia boards, so any crypto-asset product they offer would require Sharia approval at the institutional level. The SIO module's requirements of full, high-quality reserves and prompt redemption correspond to the conditions that the analysis of Chapter 3 (§3.3.3) identifies for fiat-backed stablecoins. This is a convergence of prudential and juristic concerns, not an incorporation of Sharia into regulation.

### 4.5.4 Assessment: Strengths and Weaknesses

The Bahraini approach balances innovation and risk management through a clear and proportionate licensing framework. Its prudential requirements are sophisticated, its consumer protection is strong, and its rules on stablecoins are among the most developed in the region. Its location as the headquarters of AAOIFI, and its requirement that Islamic financial institutions follow AAOIFI standards, place it well to develop Sharia-compliant digital assets at the institutional level.

Its weaknesses are those of a small market. It has a limited ecosystem and a smaller community of fintech firms than the UAE, and it is less attractive to international exchanges. Its prudential approach may slow innovation relative to more permissive neighbours, and its supervisory capacity, though respected, is limited in scale.

## 4.6 Comparative Regulatory Analysis

### 4.6.1 Regulatory Approaches Comparison

Table 4.1 summarises the five approaches.

**Table 4.1: Regulatory approaches compared (as of October 2026)**

| Jurisdiction | Approach | Posture | Support for innovation | Integration of Sharia in crypto regulation |

| Indonesia (OJK) | Licensing of digital financial assets with exchange-level lists (DAKD) | Moderate (spot and, since 2025, derivatives) | Medium (sandbox: 7 graduates) | None in regulation; OJK–DSN-MUI study under way; religious bodies rule separately |

Read across its rows, the comparison discloses a structure that is easy to miss when the frameworks are examined one at a time. Only Malaysia integrates Sharia assessment into the regulation of crypto-assets. The other four jurisdictions, whether restrictive or permissive, regulate crypto-assets on religiously neutral terms and leave Sharia assessment to religious bodies, to private certification or to the Sharia boards of individual institutions. Two variables that an observer might expect to move together, support for innovation and Sharia integration, therefore do not correlate. The UAE is highly supportive of innovation with no public Sharia integration. Saudi Arabia is restrictive with no Sharia integration in regulation. Malaysia combines moderate support for innovation with full integration. Islamic legal rigour, in other words, is not the binding constraint on innovation in these jurisdictions, and its absence from regulation does not make a jurisdiction permissive. This decoupling undercuts a common assumption in both popular and policy literature, namely that Islamic law is inherently hostile to digital-asset innovation. The evidence suggests instead that the restrictiveness of a framework is determined chiefly by the regulator's appetite for risk and its confidence in its own supervisory capacity.

A second pattern concerns the approaches themselves. Four regulatory technologies are on display. Indonesia practises licensing with exchange-level asset lists, having practised commodity containment under Bappebti until 2025. Malaysia practises licensing with integrated Sharia screening. The UAE and Bahrain practise licensing with prudential or activity-based supervision but without Sharia screening. Saudi Arabia practises non-recognition. These are not merely different intensities of the same policy but categorically different instruments, each carrying its own theory of how harm to consumers is best averted. The comparative sections that follow trace how each performs against the objectives of consumer protection, market development and Sharia compliance, and how each is tested by the borderless character of the technology.

### 4.6.2 Regulatory Innovation-Restriction Spectrum

On the single axis of encouragement of innovation, the five jurisdictions can be ordered from the most encouraging to the most restrictive. The UAE is first, followed by Malaysia, Bahrain and Indonesia, with Saudi Arabia last. The ordering reflects the choice between licensing and non-recognition, the availability of sandboxes, the range of assets admitted, and the burden of compliance.

The ordering rewards closer interpretation. The UAE and Saudi Arabia occupy the poles not because they disagree about the Islamic status of cryptocurrency, on which neither regulator has adopted a formal position, but because they draw opposite conclusions from the same underlying concern about speculative harm. Saudi Arabia reads the hazard as intractable and responds by withholding licences. The UAE reads it as manageable through disclosure, limits on leverage and continuous supervision, and responds by building an elaborate licensing apparatus to contain it. The intermediate positions of Malaysia and Bahrain are best understood not as compromises but as *conditional* engagements. Malaysia admits assets as Sharia-compliant only after screening, and Bahrain admits stablecoins only with full reserves and redemption rights. Each filter removes features that classical jurisprudence found objectionable. Indonesia sits lower on the spectrum than its market size would suggest because of its Sharia neutrality and its later start, even though, since POJK 23/2025, it permits derivatives that several of its own religious bodies exclude.

The policy significance of the spectrum lies in what it implies about regulatory arbitrage. Because the assets themselves are perfectly mobile, the position a jurisdiction adopts on this axis does not determine whether its residents transact in cryptocurrency. It determines only whether they do so inside or outside the regulator's supervisory reach. A restrictive posture, on this reading, does not eliminate the activity it disfavours; it exports it. This dynamic, examined in §4.7, is the most consequential constraint on the effectiveness of any national framework and the strongest structural argument for the harmonisation agenda advanced in Chapter 6.

### 4.6.3 Sharia Compliance Integration

On the degree to which Sharia assessment is integrated into the regulation of crypto-assets, the five jurisdictions fall into three groups. Malaysia alone integrates public Sharia screening into its regime. Indonesia has no Sharia provision in its crypto-asset regulation but has an OJK–DSN-MUI study under way, and its religious bodies have ruled on crypto-assets. The UAE, Bahrain and Saudi Arabia have no Sharia integration in their crypto-asset regulation, although the first two require their Islamic financial institutions to follow AAOIFI standards.

The ranking must be read with care, because the absence of Sharia integration has very different meanings in different jurisdictions. In the UAE and Bahrain it reflects a division of labour. Prudential regulators license the market, and Sharia governance is supplied by Islamic financial institutions and private certifiers for products marketed as compliant. In Saudi Arabia it accompanies a posture of non-recognition that is consistent with strict jurisprudential positions, though not formally grounded in a fatwa. In Indonesia it produces a gap between legal tradability and Sharia permissibility that religious bodies outside the supervisory apparatus have tried to fill (MUI 2021, Muhammadiyah 2026, NU 2026). The same absence of Sharia in regulation thus coexists with permissive, restrictive and contested markets, which reinforces the chapter's central claim that jurisprudential commitment does less explanatory work than is commonly assumed.

Malaysia shows what integration looks like when it exists. Its Shariah Advisory Council, operating within the SC, converts a doctrinal judgment into an enumerated, tradable universe of assets and, since March 2026, makes its endorsement a condition of offering an asset as compliant. What distinguishes Malaysia from the others is therefore less the depth of scholarly involvement than the maturity of the administrative machinery that translates a fatwa into a supervisable rule. That distinction anticipates the institutional proposal developed in Chapter 6.

### 4.6.4 Consumer Protection Framework

On the intensity of consumer protection within the regulated market, the prudential and licensing regimes of Bahrain and the UAE rank highest. Malaysia's capital market regime follows, then Indonesia's licensing with asset lists and conduct rules. Saudi Arabia, which offers no regulated market, ranks lowest on this measure but offers the "protection" of non-recognition. The relevant mechanisms are licensing and oversight, capital requirements, protection of client assets, market conduct standards and dispute resolution.

The placement of Saudi Arabia contains a deliberate provocation, because it exposes the central theoretical fault line in the comparative study of digital-asset regulation. On one theory of protection, the *paternalist* or *ḥifẓ al-māl*-maximising view, the surest way to shield retail investors from an instrument saturated with *gharar* is to deny them access altogether. On this reading Saudi Arabia offers the strongest protection precisely because it removes the hazard rather than merely supervising it. On the competing theory, the *harm-reduction* view that informs the frameworks of Bahrain and the UAE, non-recognition offers only the *appearance* of protection, because it cannot prevent residents from reaching offshore, unlicensed venues where no standards of capital, custody or disclosure apply. Under the harm-reduction theory the excluded investor is *less* protected than the licensed one, having been pushed into precisely the unregulated environment that classical jurisprudence, properly understood, seeks to avoid.

The dissertation does not resolve this dispute in the abstract, because the correct answer is empirical and specific to each jurisdiction. Prohibition genuinely protects where enforcement can deny access, and merely displaces harm where it cannot. The relevant question for each regulator is therefore not "is cryptocurrency permissible?" but "does this state possess the enforcement capacity to make a prohibition real?". That is a question of institutional fact rather than fiqh. The frameworks of Bahrain and the UAE reflect a judgment that, given porous digital borders, supervised access protects consumers more reliably than an unenforceable ban. This is the same *maṣlaḥa*-grounded logic that animates the harmonisation recommendations in Chapter 6. There the argument is made that the protective purpose of Islamic financial law is better served by a coordinated regulatory perimeter than by a patchwork of national prohibitions that sophisticated users can circumvent at will.

### 4.6.5 Market Development Outcomes

In terms of market development, the UAE shows the deepest ecosystem. Malaysia has a growing ecosystem, Bahrain a moderate one, and Indonesia a very large licensed retail market (23.2 million accounts and 34 licensed entities) with a thinner institutional and Islamic segment. Saudi Arabia shows minimal domestic development. The outcomes concern exchange development, the growth of the fintech ecosystem, regional competitiveness, the attraction of talent and institutional participation.

This ranking should be read as the *observed outcome* against which the preceding normative comparisons can be tested. Its ordering, with the UAE highest and Saudi Arabia lowest, broadly tracks the innovation–restriction spectrum of §4.6.2, which is unsurprising. Indonesia is the exception: a large market has developed under a licensing regime that is moderate in its encouragement of innovation, driven by retail demand. The correlation carries a policy implication that is easily overlooked. Ecosystem depth, talent attraction and institutional participation are not costless by-products of permissiveness. They are the very outcomes that a national economic-development strategy seeks to capture, and they accrue disproportionately to whichever jurisdiction offers the most credible regulatory home. In a region of mobile capital and mobile talent, regulatory posture functions as a competitive instrument. The UAE's high placement reflects a deliberate bid to become the region's digital-asset hub, and its success in attracting exchanges and fintech firms is, in part, activity that more restrictive jurisdictions have declined to license and thereby ceded.

This dynamic reframes the restrictiveness of the Saudi framework, and of the pre-2025 Indonesian framework. The cost of a cautious posture is not confined to domestic investors who are denied regulated products. It extends to the forgone development of a domestic industry, the emigration of fintech talent, and the loss of the tax base and supervisory visibility that a licensed onshore market would generate. Whether that cost is worth bearing is a legitimate policy judgment that each state is entitled to make according to its own weighting of caution against growth. The comparative point is that the judgment is a real trade-off with measurable consequences, not a free exercise of jurisprudential principle. The fragmentation of these judgments across the region also imposes a collective cost, in the form of forgone economies of scale and regulatory arbitrage, that no single jurisdiction can remedy alone. Section 4.7 takes up this collective-action problem directly.

## 4.7 Harmonization Gaps and Challenges

### 4.7.1 Regulatory Fragmentation

The five jurisdictions differ on every basic dimension of regulation, as Table 4.2 shows.

**Table 4.2: Fragmentation across the five jurisdictions (as of October 2026)**

| Issue | Indonesia | Malaysia | Saudi Arabia | UAE | Bahrain |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Legal characterisation | Digital financial asset | Prescribed security | Unrecognised; not legal tender | Virtual asset; payment token | Crypto-asset; stablecoin |
| Legal status | Lawful to trade; not currency | Lawful to trade on registered exchanges | No licensed market | Lawful under licence | Lawful under licence |
| Treatment | Licensing with exchange lists | Capital market licensing with Sharia screening | Non-recognition | Activity-based licensing | Prudential licensing |
| Market development | Large retail market | Moderate | Minimal | High | Moderate |
| Support for innovation | Medium | Medium | Low | High | Medium |

This fragmentation has four consequences. The first is regulatory arbitrage: businesses migrate to permissive jurisdictions, investors use offshore platforms, activity flows into unregulated channels, and restrictive regulation loses effectiveness. The second is inconsistency of standards. There is no common Islamic framework for digital assets, requirements differ across jurisdictions, and institutions operating regionally face considerable complexity. The third is competitive disadvantage. Restrictive jurisdictions lose talent and investment to permissive ones, and innovation concentrates in a few centres, notably the UAE. The fourth is a gap in Islamic finance coordination: Sharia assessment is integrated into regulation only in Malaysia, the authorities' positions diverge (Chapter 3), and Islamic financial institutions remain uncertain about regional participation.

### 4.7.2 Specific Coordination Challenges

Three areas pose particular challenges for coordination.

**Stablecoins.** Indonesia has admitted a rupiah stablecoin issuer (IDRP) to graduation from its sandbox (June 2026). The UAE regulates payment tokens and restricts payments within the UAE to licensed dirham-backed tokens (2024). Bahrain has a dedicated module for the issuance and offering of stablecoins (2025). Malaysia's treatment is developing, and Saudi Arabia has none. There is no common standard for the classification of stablecoins or for the requirements they must meet. The only express Sharia ruling on stablecoins, the 2026 Karachi fatwa, is negative.

**Islamic digital assets.** There is no common standard or mutual recognition for Islamic digital assets. Indonesia's regulation is Sharia-neutral, while its religious bodies conditionally permit crypto-assets (MUI 2021; Muhammadiyah and NU 2026). Malaysia screens assets through a public Sharia authority. Saudi Arabia does not recognise crypto-assets. The UAE and Bahrain rely on institutional Sharia boards and private certification.

**Cross-border activity.** There is no mechanism for coordinated cross-border supervision. Indonesia and Malaysia regulate domestic platforms, the UAE and Bahrain license international activity, and Saudi Arabia has no licensed channel.

### 4.7.3 Identified Harmonization Gaps

Five gaps emerge from the comparison. The first is the absence of a **common definition**. Jurisdictions characterise crypto-assets as financial assets, securities, virtual assets or unrecognised instruments, which creates confusion and prevents standardised treatment. The second is the **alignment of Islamic principles**. Approaches to Sharia integration diverge, there is no common standard for Islamic digital assets, and requirements for Sharia compliance are inconsistent. The third concerns **consumer protection standards**: levels of protection, identification and AML requirements, standards of custody and fund protection, and mechanisms of dispute resolution all vary. The fourth is **institutional coordination**. No body coordinates the Islamic financial regulation of digital assets, communication between regulators is limited, and there is no data sharing, joint supervision or joint enforcement. The fifth concerns **innovation frameworks**: sandboxes and innovation approaches are disparate, Islamic fintech development is uncoordinated, and new types of asset are treated inconsistently.

## 4.8 Best Practices and Regulatory Learning

### 4.8.1 Regulatory Best Practices

Each jurisdiction offers practices that others could adopt.

**From the UAE, innovation with oversight.** The UAE offers comprehensive, activity-based licensing, sandboxes with a clear pathway to full licensing, proportionate requirements graduated by client type, and a dedicated framework for payment tokens that confines domestic payment use to licensed local-currency stablecoins.

**From Malaysia, Sharia screening within the regulator.** Malaysia offers the integration of a public Sharia authority into the supervisory process. It maintains an enumerated list of endorsed assets and, since 2026, makes endorsement a condition of offering an asset as Sharia-compliant. Its framework is also notable for placing digital assets within an established capital market regime.

**From Bahrain, prudential discipline.** Bahrain offers capital adequacy and governance requirements, risk-based supervision, protection of client assets and, in particular, a rigorous framework for stablecoins built on full reserves and prompt redemption.

**From Indonesia, exchange-level asset lists and the sandbox pathway.** Indonesia offers published asset lists (DAKD) with heightened-monitoring flags, as on ICEX ("Pass With Notes"). It has comprehensive identification and AML requirements and standards against manipulation. Its sandbox has graduated backed models, among them gold, government securities, property usufruct and a rupiah stablecoin.

### 4.8.2 Regulatory Integration Recommendations

The lessons can be drawn together into recommendations for three groups of jurisdictions.

**Restrictive or Sharia-neutral jurisdictions.** This group comprises Saudi Arabia and, on the Sharia dimension, Indonesia. These jurisdictions should consider admitting properly structured digital assets, recognising asset-backed tokens, and creating pathways for stablecoins. They should also establish sandboxes for Islamic fintech and adopt an approach graduated by type of risk. For Indonesia, the priority is a Sharia screening standard for the assets on its exchange lists, which could be developed through the existing OJK–DSN-MUI study.

**Permissive jurisdictions.** For the UAE, the recommendations are to develop public or industry-wide Sharia standards for virtual assets offered as compliant, to strengthen consumer protection further, to build supervisory capacity, to pursue international coordination, and to monitor systemic risk.

**Balanced jurisdictions.** Malaysia and Bahrain should consider expanding their sandbox programmes, clarifying the pathways for Islamic digital assets (in Bahrain, a Sharia dimension for stablecoins issued under the SIO module), strengthening supervisory capacity, and taking a lead in regional coordination.

## 4.9 Chapter Conclusion

This chapter set out to trace how five jurisdictions with large Muslim populations translate jurisprudential conviction into administrative practice. The comparative evidence yields a conclusion more subtle than the surface impression of fragmentation would suggest. The five frameworks do diverge sharply, from Saudi non-recognition to Emirati licensing. On close reading, however, the divergence is not primarily a divergence over Islamic law. With the exception of Malaysia, none of the regulators examined has incorporated Islamic legal assessment into its crypto-asset regulation at all. What separates them is a set of policy judgments: how grave the hazard of speculative, unbacked assets is once a supervisory regime is in place, how much confidence the regulator has in its own enforcement capacity, and how heavily it weights the *maṣlaḥa* of financial-sector development against the *ḥifẓ al-māl* imperative of investor protection. The chapter's principal analytical contribution is to disentangle two layers. The first is the jurisprudential question, which in most jurisdictions is left to religious bodies or to private institutions. The second is the regulatory judgment, which is made on religiously neutral grounds. Disentangling them shows that the region's regulatory patchwork is a product of contestable policy choices rather than of irreconcilable religious doctrine.

That finding matters because it makes harmonisation thinkable. If the fragmentation were rooted in genuine jurisprudential disagreement among regulators, a coordinated framework would require resolving a theological dispute. Because it is rooted instead in divergent appetites for risk and unequal supervisory capacities, coordination becomes a matter of institutional design. The task is to build shared machinery for setting standards and arrangements for mutual recognition that let each state retain its own weighting of risk while eliminating the arbitrage and duplication that fragmentation imposes on all of them. The jurisprudential disagreement examined in Chapter 3 does not disappear. It is relocated to the Sharia layer, which Malaysia shows can be institutionalised and which the IIFRC proposal of Chapter 6 seeks to coordinate.

This is the collective-action problem that the borderless character of the technology makes unavoidable. No national framework, however well crafted, can fully protect its own residents or capture the development gains of a licensed market while its neighbours' rules diverge and offshore venues remain a click away. The chapter therefore points beyond the national frame towards the coordinated architecture developed in Chapters 5 and 6, for which the comparative analysis here supplies the empirical foundation.

### Summary of Regulatory Landscape

The regulatory landscape can be summarised in three observations. First, regulatory approaches are diverse. They range from non-recognition (Saudi Arabia) to comprehensive licensing (the UAE), with different legal characterisations, different degrees of encouragement for innovation, and, except in Malaysia, no integration of Sharia assessment into crypto-asset regulation. Second, there are fundamental gaps: no common definition, no coordinating mechanism for the Islamic financial regulation of digital assets, divergent approaches to Islamic digital assets, limited cross-border supervisory coordination and inconsistent standards of consumer protection. Third, jurisdictions are pursuing distinct strategies. The UAE seeks to be the region's digital-asset hub, and Malaysia has built a balanced framework with integrated Sharia screening. Bahrain maintains a prudential approach with a leading stablecoin regime, and Indonesia licenses a very large market through exchange-level asset lists while leaving Sharia assessment to religious bodies. Saudi Arabia withholds recognition while experimenting with central bank digital currency. The absence of coordinated Islamic financial regulation of digital assets creates opportunities for innovation but also risks to systemic stability and consumer protection.

### Implications for Chapter 5

The regulatory analysis lays the foundation for the task of Chapter 5: a unified framework for classifying digital assets that integrates the Islamic legal principles of Chapter 2, the jurisprudential positions of Chapter 3 and the regulatory practices compared here. Such a framework is designed to address the fragmentation identified in this chapter and to support a coordinated Islamic approach to the regulation of digital assets.

## 4.10 Policy Implications for Regulators

The comparison shows that regulatory fragmentation reflects legitimate differences of policy rather than regulatory failure, but also that it creates opportunities for coordination. Regulatory variation reflects different priorities: consumer protection, innovation, stability and monetary control. Harmonisation should align policies where possible while accommodating legitimate differences. Each regulator should therefore understand its own priorities and identify pathways to harmonisation consistent with them. Four actions follow.

**Identifying a regulatory philosophy.** A regulator should first make its philosophy explicit. A focus on consumer protection, as in Indonesia, emphasises disclosure, restrictions on retail access and institutional safeguards. A focus on innovation and integrated Sharia screening, as in Malaysia, emphasises sandboxes, clear pathways to compliance and regulatory certainty. A focus on stability, as in Bahrain, emphasises prudential requirements, risk management and the monitoring of systemic risk. A focus on monetary control, as in Saudi Arabia, emphasises the development of central bank digital currency, controlled institutional access and the prevention of spillovers.

**Designing minimum standards consistent with that philosophy.** A consumer-protection approach implies stricter requirements for backing, lower tolerance of volatility and, for higher-risk assets, institutional-only access. An approach centred on innovation implies clearer pathways to compliance, graduated requirements and access to sandboxes. A stability approach implies prudential capital requirements, stress-testing and enhanced monitoring. A monetary-control approach implies restricted access, priority for CBDC and limits on spillovers.

**Joining a coordination mechanism suited to that philosophy.** The IIFRC proposed in Chapter 6 offers three implementation models. Full integration corresponds to Malaysia. A separated model corresponds to Indonesia, where legal listing is by OJK and Sharia assessment by religious bodies. A conservative model corresponds to Saudi Arabia. Each regulator can select the model that reflects its approach, benefiting from shared standard-setting while keeping its policy flexibility.

**Establishing information-sharing with aligned jurisdictions.** Regulators focused on consumer protection can coordinate with Indonesia, those focused on innovation and Sharia screening with Malaysia, and those focused on stability with Bahrain. Regulators focused on monetary control can coordinate with Saudi Arabia on CBDC, and regulators interested in licensing infrastructure with the UAE.

The expected outcomes are coherence, because regulation would reflect and advance the regulator's own philosophy, and efficiency, because shared expertise would replace duplicated work. Transparent communication of the philosophy would increase legitimacy, and differentiated policies would allow approaches tailored to local circumstances. A regulator could implement this approach within about two years. In the first three months it would clarify its philosophy and objectives, and in the following three it would design standards and thresholds aligned with them. In the second half of the first year it would join a coordination mechanism and identify aligned jurisdictions, and in the second year it would take part in coordination and mutual learning, adjusting its standards as evidence accumulates.
