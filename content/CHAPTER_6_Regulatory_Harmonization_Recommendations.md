# CHAPTER 6: REGULATORY HARMONIZATION RECOMMENDATIONS

## 6.1 The Harmonization Problem and Opportunity

The preceding chapters have documented a systematic fragmentation in the regulation of cryptocurrency across jurisdictions with Muslim-majority populations. Chapter 4 identified five distinct approaches:

- Indonesia supervises crypto-assets as digital financial assets under OJK, with exchange-level lists of tradeable assets.
- Malaysia has a regulated digital-asset framework administered by the Securities Commission, with ex ante Sharia screening by its Shariah Advisory Council.
- Saudi Arabia maintains a restrictive posture amounting to de facto prohibition.
- The United Arab Emirates operates comprehensive licensing regimes.
- Bahrain applies prudential regulation.

This fragmentation creates opportunities for regulatory arbitrage. It disadvantages jurisdictions that impose higher standards, and it exposes Islamic financial institutions operating across borders to inconsistent and sometimes conflicting requirements.

Fragmentation also reflects legitimate differences of policy priority and institutional capacity. Saudi Arabia's restriction reflects concern for monetary control and stability. Indonesia's approach reflects the priority of consumer protection in a very large retail market, together with a deliberate decision to leave Sharia assessment to religious bodies. Malaysia's framework reflects ambitions for leadership in financial technology, and the UAE's licensing regimes reflect its competitive positioning as a financial centre. These differences cannot, and should not, be eliminated by a harmonisation imposed from above.

The opportunity therefore lies in what this chapter calls *structured harmonisation*. Jurisdictions would share a classification methodology (Chapter 5) and agree on minimum standards, while each retains discretion over how those standards are implemented. The chapter proposes institutional mechanisms to achieve this balance. Section 6.2 sets out the institutions, §6.3 the minimum standards, §6.4 the models of differentiated implementation, and §6.5 the mechanisms of mutual recognition, cooperation and enforcement. Sections 6.6 to 6.9 address the roadmap, the principal challenges, the expected benefits and the risks. Section 6.10 examines Indonesian practice as a partial working model.

## 6.2 Institutional Framework for Islamic Cryptocurrency Regulation

### 6.2.1 Establishment of Inter-Islamic Finance Regulatory Council (IIFRC)

The central proposal of this chapter is an Inter-Islamic Finance Regulatory Council (IIFRC): a coordinating body for the regulation of digital assets across Islamic jurisdictions. It is modelled on the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision and on the International Organization of Securities Commissions (IOSCO), but specialised to the context of Islamic finance.

**Membership and governance.** The Council would draw its members from the central banks and financial regulators of fifteen to twenty jurisdictions. A core group with voting rights would comprise six jurisdictions:

- Saudi Arabia (SAMA);
- the United Arab Emirates (the Central Bank of the UAE, with the participation of the emirate-level virtual asset authorities);
- Malaysia (Bank Negara Malaysia and the Securities Commission);
- Indonesia (OJK);
- Qatar (QCB);
- Bahrain (CBB).

An extended group with advisory status could include Pakistan, Bangladesh, Brunei, Egypt, Jordan, Kuwait, Turkey, Tunisia and Morocco. The Islamic Development Bank, AAOIFI and the Islamic Financial Services Board (IFSB) would participate as observers.

The chair would rotate among the core members for two-year terms. A full-time executive director and a technical secretariat of eight to ten regulatory specialists would support the Council. Its work would be organised through four standing committees:

- classification and standards;
- regulatory frameworks;
- supervisory cooperation;
- financial stability.

The heads of member regulators would meet quarterly and the technical committees monthly, with *ad hoc* working groups formed for emerging issues. Member contributions would fund an annual budget in the order of US$2–5 million. That figure is the author's estimate based on the scale of comparable secretariats.

**Functions.** The Council would perform four functions.

- **Setting standards.** It would maintain the classification framework of Chapter 5 and review classifications annually in light of changes in dimensional scores. It would also publish standardised scoring guidelines and issue technical guidance on applying the *maqasid* to new types of digital asset.
- **Coordinating regulation.** It would establish minimum standards for recognition in Categories A and B, operate a framework of mutual recognition for qualifying assets, coordinate positions on emerging asset types, and develop responses to cross-border arbitrage.
- **Supporting supervisory cooperation.** Members would share regulatory intelligence on risks, follow common protocols for information-sharing in anti-money laundering and counter-terrorist financing supervision, coordinate stress-testing methodologies, and develop the capacity for joint examination of cross-border activities.
- **Liaising with Islamic jurisprudence.** The Council would maintain a formal relationship with AAOIFI and with the principal Islamic authorities, update its standards as new jurisprudential positions emerge, and commission research on open questions.

### 6.2.2 Shariah Advisory Council on Digital Assets

Sharia assessment differs in kind from regulatory determination. The proposal therefore establishes a separate Shariah Advisory Council (SAC) to provide authoritative jurisprudential guidance to the IIFRC and its members.

The Shariah Advisory Council would comprise seven to nine senior scholars drawn from the four Sunni schools of law. They would represent the geographical diversity of the Muslim world, including scholars from the Gulf, South Asia, North Africa and Southeast Asia. The council would include members with technical expertise in digital assets. The Sharia boards of the central banks of major jurisdictions would participate as observers.

The council would have three functions.

- **Sharia assessment.** It would review classifications against the *maqasid* framework, issue opinions on new types of digital asset, and advise the IIFRC on opportunities for jurisprudential convergence.
- **Methodology.** It would refine the application of the *maqasid* to digital assets and develop guidance on legitimate variation in Sharia interpretation between jurisdictions.
- **Liaison.** It would maintain relationships with the principal authorities, including Dar al-Ifta al-Misriyyah, the Darul Uloom Karachi, MUI, Muhammadiyah and the Nahdlatul Ulama, and commission comparative research.

Since these authorities divided in 2026 on the threshold question of whether crypto-assets are *mal* (Chapter 3, §3.5), the council's first task would be to articulate how the IIFRC framework accommodates that disagreement. It would not attempt to resolve the disagreement by majority vote.

## 6.3 Minimum Standards for Category Recognition

### 6.3.1 Category A: Islamic-Compliant Digital Assets - Minimum Standards

Recognition in Category A by IIFRC members would require a digital asset to satisfy minimum scores on each dimension of the classification framework, as well as a minimum composite score. Table 6.1 sets out the requirements.

**Table 6.1: Minimum standards for Category A**

| Dimension | Minimum Score | Rationale |
|-----------|--------------|-----------|
| Asset Backing (Dim 1) | 4/5 points | Ensure tangible underlying value |
| Value Stability (Dim 2) | 4/5 points | Enable use as medium of exchange |
| Productive Utility (Dim 3) | 3/5 points | Demonstrate genuine economic benefit |
| Governance (Dim 4) | 3/5 points | Minimum transparency and community participation |
| Regulatory Recognition (Dim 5) | 3/5 points | Recognition in at least one major Islamic jurisdiction |
| **Composite Score** | **≥20 points** | Overall compliance threshold (Chapter 5, §5.3) |

The requirement of a minimum on each dimension, and not only a composite threshold, is deliberate. A high score on stability or utility should not compensate for inadequate asset backing or governance.

**Mutual recognition.** When an asset achieves Category A status in one member jurisdiction, other members would recognise the classification within six months unless they identify a specific risk. Recognition of the classification does not require identical regulatory treatment. It signifies only that the asset's Sharia-compliance status is accepted.

**An illustration: USDC.** On the scoring of Chapter 5, USDC scores 19 points, one point below the Category A threshold. Its dimensional scores are:

- asset backing 4;
- stability 5;
- utility 5;
- governance 2, below the minimum of 3;
- regulatory recognition 3.

Improving governance to 3 points, through institutional oversight or community governance mechanisms, would raise the composite to 20 and satisfy every minimum. Under the mutual recognition protocol, approval by one member regulator would then initiate recognition across the Council. The illustration is hypothetical: no regulator has assessed USDC on this framework. It also leaves aside the June 2026 Darul Uloom Karachi fatwa, which held that stablecoins are not *mal*. A jurisdiction whose Sharia authority follows that fatwa would decline recognition, and the protocol permits it to do so.

### 6.3.2 Category B: Conditional-Compliance Digital Assets - Minimum Standards

Category B covers assets whose compliance is conditional on further development. Table 6.2 sets out the lower thresholds that apply.

**Table 6.2: Minimum standards for Category B**

| Dimension | Minimum Score | Rationale |
|-----------|--------------|-----------|
| Asset Backing (Dim 1) | 2/5 points | Partial backing or stability mechanism |
| Value Stability (Dim 2) | 3/5 points | Emerging stability target, some volatility accepted |
| Productive Utility (Dim 3) | 2/5 points | Defined utility pathway with adoption roadmap |
| Governance (Dim 4) | 2/5 points | Minimum transparency; institutional oversight accepted |
| Regulatory Recognition (Dim 5) | 2/5 points | Under active evaluation by regulator |
| **Composite Score** | **≥15 points** | Overall compliance threshold |

Assets in Category B would receive the status of a "regulatory pathway" rather than full approval. That status would permit institutional custody, access for qualified (but not retail) investors, development activity and innovation pilots, and integration into the services of financial institutions under enhanced monitoring. In return, the asset would be subject to annual recertification, enhanced AML/CFT monitoring, and quarterly reporting on stability and utility metrics. It would also need a development roadmap towards Category A.

### 6.3.3 Categories C and D: Restricted/Prohibited - Unified Standard

Assets scoring below 15 composite points would be subject to a uniform position across the Council. Category C (10–14 points) would be restricted for retail investors and closed to institutional participation. Category D (5–9 points) would be prohibited. Unclassified assets would be treated as restricted pending assessment.

These categories are not permanent. An asset could seek reclassification by demonstrating improvement on its weakest dimensions, and the annual assessment cycle gives a one-year window for remediation. Conversely, an expedited procedure would allow rapid downgrading if an asset's scores deteriorated, for example after a collapse in stability.

In Indonesia, these categories would be applied to the more than one thousand assets on the exchange-level lists (DAKD) of ICEX and CFX (Chapter 4, §4.1.11). Doing so would require a Sharia screening process that does not yet exist. Developing it is the most immediate practical application of this section.

## 6.4 Jurisdictional Differentiation Framework

The IIFRC establishes minimum standards, but jurisdictions retain authority over implementation. The framework anticipates three models for Category A and three for Category B, each reflecting a different weighting of policy priorities.

### 6.4.1 Category A Implementation Models

**Full integration.** In the first model, associated with Malaysia, the UAE and Bahrain, assets in Category A are treated as regulated assets. Banks, asset managers and insurers may hold them, and retail investors may access them subject to investor-qualification requirements. Settlement within the banking system is permitted. Malaysia's framework, under which the Securities Commission's Shariah Advisory Council screens digital assets before they are offered, is the closest existing example.

**Conditional acceptance.** In the second model, assets in Category A may be traded and held, but institutional participation is confined to qualified institutions, and settlement takes place outside the banking system. This corresponds broadly to the Indonesian position. There, crypto-assets are lawful digital financial assets, but the banking system's exposure to them is limited and their use as a means of payment is prohibited. The rationale is consumer protection and the management of capacity constraints. The model contemplates transition to full integration as supervisory capacity develops and a Sharia screening standard is established.

**Conservative approach.** In the third model, associated with Saudi Arabia, asset-backed stablecoins in Category A would be accepted only for specified uses, such as remittances and cross-border settlement, by licensed institutions. Retail access would be excluded, and a central bank digital currency would be prioritised as the preferred alternative. The rationale is monetary control and caution. Wider acceptance would follow the development of the CBDC.

Despite these differences, all three models recognise the same assets as belonging to Category A. A project therefore undergoes a single assessment, and service providers face a consistent classification across jurisdictions. Arbitrage is reduced, because a project cannot obtain a more favourable classification by choosing its forum.

### 6.4.2 Category B Implementation Models

Three corresponding models apply to Category B.

- **Innovation sandbox model (the UAE and Malaysia).** Assets are admitted only to regulated testing programmes. Value is capped, participants are qualified investors, and testing windows last twelve to twenty-four months. Success is measured by progress towards Category A.
- **Consumer protection model.** Retail access is permitted subject to enhanced risk warnings, limits on the proportion of a retail portfolio that may be invested, and a mandatory cooling-off period of, for example, forty-eight hours. This model reflects the priorities of jurisdictions with large retail markets, such as Indonesia.
- **Institutional-only model (Bahrain).** Assets are available only to qualified institutional investors, subject to additional capital buffers, scenario analysis and liquidity stress-testing. Its rationale is the protection of financial stability.

## 6.5 Mutual Recognition and Regulatory Cooperation Mechanisms

### 6.5.1 Qualified Cryptocurrency Registry

The IIFRC would maintain a public registry of all assets approved in Category A or B by any member regulator. For each asset, the registry would record:

- the composite and dimensional scores;
- the date of assessment;
- the approving regulator's decision and its conditions;
- the implementation model adopted.

Changes in classification would be notified to all members in real time.

The registry would serve three purposes. It would make regulatory decisions transparent, allowing market participants to identify approved assets. It would trigger the six-month recognition review in other jurisdictions when an asset is approved in one. And it would make downgrades immediately visible to all regulators, supporting risk monitoring.

### 6.5.2 Mutual Recognition Agreement (MRA) Framework

The mutual recognition of Category A classifications would rest on an agreement among members.

**Scope.** The agreement would cover recognition of the classification determination only. Supervision would remain with the originating regulator.

**Conditions.** Recognition would depend on three conditions. The originating regulator must continue to supervise the asset. The asset must maintain its Category A score, subject to annual recertification. And there must be no systemic-risk or market-abuse incident involving the asset.

**Procedure.** The originating regulator would notify the IIFRC within thirty days of approval, using standardised documentation. Other members would then have six months to object. Recognition would follow automatically if no objection were filed.

**Dispute resolution.** Disputes would be handled at three levels. An IIFRC mediation committee would hear disagreements between jurisdictions. A technical expert panel would resolve disputes over scoring. Policy-level conflicts would be escalated to the heads of member regulators.

A hypothetical sequence illustrates the procedure. Suppose a Malaysian regulator approves a stablecoin in Category A and notifies the IIFRC in the following month. In the third to eighth months, other members conduct their review. In the eighth month, an Emirati authority recognises the classification without objection. In the ninth month, Indonesia's OJK declines automatic recognition but permits the asset under its existing framework. In the twelfth month, Saudi Arabia maintains its restrictions in line with its monetary policy. The net result is recognition in three of the six core jurisdictions through a single assessment. The scenario shows that the framework produces partial, not universal, convergence, and that this is an intended feature rather than a failure.

### 6.5.3 Supervisory Cooperation Framework

Beyond recognition, members would cooperate in supervision in three areas. The first is monitoring risk: members would share information on market developments quarterly, issue real-time alerts on stability events, governance changes or compliance violations, and use common stress-testing scenarios. The second is cross-border supervision. This would include joint examination of digital asset service providers operating in several jurisdictions, coordinated enforcement against AML/CFT violations, and a shared framework of responsibility for supervisory failures. The third is capacity building. The Council would provide technical assistance and training to regulators with less experience of digital assets and develop shared regulatory technology.

### 6.5.4 Non-Compliance and the Graduated Enforcement Ladder

The mechanisms above presuppose good-faith participation. They do not answer the question a defensible institutional proposal must confront: *what happens when a member regulator ignores an IIFRC classification, recognises an asset the Council has ruled non-compliant, or withholds recognition from an asset the Council has certified?* This is the most common failure mode of Islamic-finance standard-setting, and its resolution is what separates a coordinating council from a discussion forum.

**The objection, stated at its strongest.** A candid reviewer will press the following. Section 6.7.1 commits the IIFRC to "no binding enforcement" in order to preserve monetary sovereignty. But a body with no enforcement is precisely what already exists. AAOIFI and the IFSB have issued Shariah and prudential standards for two decades; neither produced a unified cryptocurrency standard (Chapter 4, §4.7), because both are advisory bodies whose pronouncements bind only where a national legislature independently adopts them. AAOIFI standards are mandatory in Bahrain, Sudan, Syria, Jordan, Oman, and Qatar's QFC, but merely persuasive elsewhere (AAOIFI, *Adoption of AAOIFI Standards* status reports; Islamic Financial Services Board, 2015, *IFSB-17: Core Principles for Islamic Finance Regulation*). The IFSB itself operates on an explicit "comply-or-explain" basis and can do no more than survey and publish adoption gaps (IFSB, *Islamic Financial Services Industry Stability Report*, annual implementation surveys). If the IIFRC is one more advisory body, member regulators will rationally free-ride: absorb the reputational benefit of membership while quietly deviating whenever a domestic constituency or a well-capitalised project makes deviation attractive. This is a textbook collective-action problem, and asserting that classification is "objective" does not solve it.

**Response: enforcement without coercion.** The objection conflates *enforcement* with *sanction by mandate*. Sovereignty forecloses the latter — the IIFRC cannot compel a central bank to license or delist an asset — but it does not foreclose the former. The mature transnational regulatory networks all enforce without a supranational police power, through graduated, reputational, and privilege-based sanctions administered by peers: the FATF's mutual-evaluation and Increased-Monitoring ("grey list") process (FATF, *Methodology for Assessing Technical Compliance*, 2013, as revised); the IOSCO Multilateral Memorandum of Understanding, whose signatories are screened, tiered, and can be moved to a monitored appendix for non-cooperation (IOSCO, MMoU, 2002); and the Basel Committee's Regulatory Consistency Assessment Programme (RCAP), which publicly grades jurisdictions "compliant," "largely compliant," "materially non-compliant," or "non-compliant" (Basel Committee on Banking Supervision, *RCAP Handbook*, 2016). None wields a mandate; each imposes real cost — lost market access, higher counterparty risk premia, reputational damage — sufficient to move sovereign regulators. The IIFRC adopts the same logic, tuned to the mutual-recognition privilege established in §6.5.2: the currency of enforcement is *access to recognition*, which a member forfeits by defection but cannot obtain unilaterally.

**Islamic-legal basis for collective regulatory accountability.** That this enforcement architecture is not merely borrowed from secular regulatory practice but grounded in the Islamic legal tradition is essential to the proposal's coherence. Four established doctrines converge:

1. **Siyāsa sharʿiyya (Shariah-oriented governance).** Legitimate authority may enact discretionary, non-textual policy measures to secure the public welfare, provided they do not contradict a definitive text (Ibn Taymiyya, *al-Siyāsa al-Sharʿiyya fī Iṣlāḥ al-Rāʿī wa'l-Raʿiyya*; Ibn al-Qayyim, *al-Ṭuruq al-Ḥukmiyya*). A coordinated enforcement ladder is a paradigmatic *siyāsa* instrument: procedural machinery, not revealed law, justified by the interest it protects.
2. **Taʿzīr (discretionary, calibrated sanction).** Where no fixed penalty (ḥadd) applies, the legitimate authority may impose graduated corrective measures proportioned to the harm and to the goal of deterrence and reform rather than retribution (al-Māwardī, *al-Aḥkām al-Sulṭāniyya*). The soft→medium→hard ladder is a direct application: each rung is a taʿzīr measure scaled to the gravity and persistence of the breach.
3. **Ḥisba (institutional oversight of the market).** The classical *muḥtasib* corrected market wrongdoing through an escalating sequence — private counsel (naṣīḥa), public admonition, then sanction — never leaping to punishment (al-Māwardī, *al-Aḥkām al-Sulṭāniyya*; Ibn Taymiyya, *al-Ḥisba fī'l-Islām*). The IIFRC ladder reproduces this sequence institutionally, with the Council as a collective muḥtasib over the cross-border digital-asset market.
4. **Farḍ kifāya (collective obligation) grounding collective accountability.** The protection of the financial system's Shariah integrity is a communal duty; where it is neglected, culpability falls on the whole community capable of discharging it (al-Ghazālī, *al-Mustaṣfā*; al-Nawawī). Because the obligation is collective, accountability for its breach is legitimately collective — which is precisely what an inter-jurisdictional accountability mechanism operationalises. The maxim *taṣarruf al-imām ʿalā al-raʿiyya manūṭ bi'l-maṣlaḥa* — the authority's power over those it governs is bound to the public interest (Ibn Nujaym, *al-Ashbāh wa'l-Naẓāʾir*) — supplies the limiting principle: enforcement is legitimate only so far as it serves *maṣlaḥa*, and no further.

**The ladder.** Enforcement proceeds through three tiers, each requiring a documented finding by the Shariah Advisory Council and the technical panel, notice, and a right to be heard (the appeal mechanism of §6.7.3):

| Tier | Trigger | Measure | Islamic basis | Secular analogue |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Soft — Engagement & Note of Divergence** | First, isolated deviation from an IIFRC classification | Confidential secretariat inquiry; if unresolved, a formal *Note of Divergence* entered in the Council register; member must comply or publish its reasoned explanation | Naṣīḥa; first tier of ḥisba | IFSB "comply-or-explain" |
| **Medium — Public flagging & probation** | Repeated or unexplained divergence; material market impact | Public annotation in the Qualified Cryptocurrency Registry that the jurisdiction's determination diverges from IIFRC classification; time-bound remediation plan; suspension of rapporteur and voting rights on the affected category | Taʿzīr proportioned to harm; public admonition (ḥisba, tier two) | Basel RCAP grading; FATF "increased monitoring" |
| **Hard — Suspension & expulsion** | Persistent, bad-faith breach; systemic-risk export | Suspension of mutual-recognition privileges (the member's Category A determinations cease to be auto-recognised; its firms lose passporting under §6.5.2); suspension from Council governance; expulsion as last resort | Taʿzīr at its upper bound; withdrawal of the covenant of cooperation (taʿāwun, Qurʾān 5:2) | IOSCO MMoU monitored-signatory / removal |

**Due process and reversibility.** Consistent with the juristic understanding of taʿzīr as reformative rather than punitive, every measure is (i) proportional to the breach, (ii) reversible on cure — a member returning to conformity is restored to full privileges — and (iii) subject to the independent appeal panel of §6.7.3. This preserves the sovereignty commitment of §6.7.1: no rung of the ladder compels a domestic policy outcome. What it does is attach a graduated, legitimate, and Shariah-grounded *cost* to defection, converting the IIFRC from an advisory body into a coordinating authority whose classifications carry consequence.

## 6.6 Implementation Roadmap and Timeline

The roadmap proceeds in three phases. Because no step towards an IIFRC had been taken as of October 2026, the timetable begins in 2027. It should be read as a realistic sequence rather than a forecast, since each phase depends on political commitments that cannot be assumed.

### Phase 1: Foundation (2027-2028)

The first phase would establish the institutions. In the first half of 2027, an interim steering committee would be formed, the Shariah Advisory Council appointed, and governance documents and operating procedures drafted. The Council would then be formally launched with its six core members. It would publish its standardised classification methodology, and the Shariah Advisory Council would issue its initial guidance on applying the *maqasid*. That guidance would include how the framework accommodates the 2026 disagreement on *mal*.

In 2028 the Qualified Cryptocurrency Registry would be launched, together with an initial assessment of twenty to thirty major digital assets, including Bitcoin, Ether and the principal stablecoins. Training programmes would begin in member jurisdictions. By the end of the phase, the first annual classification update would be published, the mutual recognition agreement finalised, and extended members recruited. The target is a functioning classification framework applied in eight to ten jurisdictions.

### Phase 2: Operationalization (2029-2030)

The second phase would put the cooperative mechanisms into operation. In 2029 the mutual recognition protocol and the supervisory cooperation arrangements would take effect. Sandbox programmes in member jurisdictions would be linked to the IIFRC framework, and the first mutual recognition of a Category A asset would be completed. In 2030 the focus would shift to stabilising the classification of the major assets, widening the programmes for approving Islamic digital assets, and coordinating the development of central bank digital currencies across members. The aim is to reduce the five current regulatory approaches to the three implementation models of §6.4. The target is simultaneous recognition of Category A assets in at least five jurisdictions.

### Phase 3: Integration (2031-2033)

The third phase would integrate the framework with the wider architecture of digital finance. As central bank digital currencies are launched in core member jurisdictions, the Council would develop interoperability frameworks between CBDCs and recognised digital assets. It would explore a pooled reserve of Islamic digital assets and extend the framework to emerging technologies, including decentralised finance and tokenised securities. Over this period the three implementation models might converge further, to two.

## 6.7 Addressing Harmonization Challenges

### 6.7.1 Sovereignty Preservation Concerns

The most obvious obstacle is that central banks resist supranational authority over regulatory decisions. The proposal meets this concern by designing the IIFRC as a coordinating body rather than a supranational regulator. Member regulators retain full authority over domestic policy. The IIFRC establishes minimum standards that operate as a floor, not a ceiling, and jurisdictions may impose stricter requirements. Mutual recognition is optional in individual cases, and disputes are resolved through mediation rather than mandate. The enforcement ladder of §6.5.4 attaches costs to defection, such as the loss of recognition privileges, but it never compels a domestic policy outcome. A Saudi Arabia that maintains its restrictive posture while participating fully in the Council's classification work therefore represents a valid outcome of harmonisation, not a failure of it.

### 6.7.2 Conflicting Social Priorities

A second challenge is that jurisdictions pursue different goals: innovation, stability, consumer protection and monetary control. The framework accommodates this through its multiple implementation models. Every asset in Category A meets the same minimum standards, but jurisdictions choose the model of implementation that suits their priorities without compromising the integrity of the classification. Malaysia may emphasise innovation, Indonesia consumer protection and Saudi Arabia monetary control. The result is a unified framework of assessment that preserves policy differentiation.

### 6.7.3 Regulatory Capture Risks

A third challenge is the risk that large projects will influence classification decisions. The proposal addresses it in five ways:

- the Shariah Advisory Council assesses alignment with the *maqasid* independently of regulatory pressure;
- governance by multiple regulators ensures that no single national interest dominates;
- classification rests on explicit metrics, such as the percentage of asset backing, bands of price stability and measures of governance transparency;
- the registry and scoring methodology are public, so classifications can be verified externally;
- projects may challenge a classification before an independent expert panel, which also serves as the appeal body for the enforcement ladder.

### 6.7.4 Capacity Constraints in Lower-Capacity Jurisdictions

Finally, smaller jurisdictions such as Djibouti, Sudan and Mauritania may lack the capacity to supervise digital assets. The IIFRC's technical secretariat would provide support for capacity building, and shared regulatory technology would lower the cost of implementation. Grants of technical assistance could fund assessment infrastructure. In some cases a jurisdiction could delegate assessment to a more experienced neighbour, as Djibouti might to Bahrain.

## 6.8 Expected Benefits and Impacts

The benefits of the framework would fall to four groups.

### 6.8.1 Benefits for Regulatory Bodies

For regulators, a common methodology would remove the incentive for forum-shopping and so reduce arbitrage. Shared assessment would avoid duplication of effort and lower costs. Cooperative information-sharing would improve the detection of risk, and technical assistance would strengthen less experienced regulators. Joint supervision would allow cross-border risks to be addressed collectively.

### 6.8.2 Benefits for Cryptocurrency Projects

For projects, a single assessment would determine regional recognition, removing the need to comply separately with five or more divergent frameworks. Category A approval would open institutional access in several jurisdictions at once. The dimensional framework would identify clear routes to improvement, especially through asset backing. Regulatory cooperation would also provide greater long-term certainty than repeated, uncoordinated reassessment.

### 6.8.3 Benefits for Islamic Financial Institutions

For Islamic financial institutions, IIFRC classifications would provide an authoritative reference point for Sharia assessment, though not a substitute for the opinion of their own Sharia boards. Standardised criteria would support consistent risk management across jurisdictions. Institutions that participate early in approved assets would gain a first-mover advantage, could offer digital asset services within a unified compliance framework, and could operate across the region under mutual recognition.

### 6.8.4 Benefits for Islamic Finance Ecosystem

For the ecosystem of Islamic finance as a whole, a clear regulatory pathway would encourage the development of Islamic digital assets. Stablecoins and other compliant digital assets could extend access to the unbanked, and settlement in digital assets could reduce the friction and cost of payments. Coordination on central bank digital currencies would help to modernise Islamic monetary systems. Together, these developments would position Islamic jurisdictions to lead in the field of digital finance that is consistent with Islamic law.

## 6.9 Potential Risks and Mitigation Strategies

### 6.9.1 Regulatory Fragmentation Persistence

The first risk is that jurisdictions continue to pursue conflicting approaches despite the effort at harmonisation, so that the practical benefits of coordination remain limited. The mitigation is gradualism. The Council would begin with non-binding coordination, such as information-sharing and joint working groups, and formalise its arrangements as trust and capacity develop. It would also start with core members that already have strong regulatory relationships. Early successes, such as the mutual recognition of a major asset-backed token, would be used to demonstrate the value of the framework.

### 6.9.2 Cryptocurrency Market Volatility Disruptions

The second risk is that a major market event, such as the de-pegging of a leading stablecoin, undermines the credibility of the Council and its methodology. The mitigation lies in four measures. Assets would be rescored quarterly in light of market events. A rapid downgrade procedure would handle failures of stability. Each member would retain authority to take emergency action outside the IIFRC framework. And the Council would communicate clearly that each classification is a point-in-time assessment.

### 6.9.3 Emerging Technology Disruption

The third risk is that new technologies, such as layer-2 scaling, cross-chain bridges and tokenised securities, emerge faster than the framework can adapt. The mitigation is institutional. A dedicated working group on emerging technology would operate within the Council, and rapid assessment pathways would be available for new types of asset. The dimensional framework is flexible enough to accommodate new characteristics, and an annual methodology review would keep it relevant.

## 6.10 Indonesian Practice as a Partial Working Model (2025–2026)

The IIFRC remains a proposal. Indonesia's Financial Services Authority (OJK), however, has developed a body of national practice since 2025 that corresponds to several elements of the harmonisation framework. This section examines that correspondence. It does not claim that OJK has adopted the framework, and it identifies where Indonesian practice falls short of the proposal.

### 6.10.1 OJK's Practice and the Proposed Principles

Table 6.3 sets the principles of the IIFRC proposal alongside the corresponding Indonesian practice as of October 2026.

**Table 6.3: IIFRC principles and Indonesian practice**

| Harmonization principle | Proposed IIFRC mechanism | Indonesian practice (October 2026) |
|---|---|---|
| Unified assessment methodology | Five-dimensional classification | Sandbox assessment parameters include clarity of underlying and of economic benefit; no Sharia dimension |
| Sharia coordination | IIFRC Shariah Advisory Council | OJK reports a joint study with DSN-MUI towards a Sharia standard; no standard yet |
| Minimum standards | Category A/B/C requirements | Seven sandbox graduates (gold, government securities, property usufruct, rupiah stablecoin, custody, crypto fund management; Chapter 4, §4.1.8); exchange-level asset lists (DAKD) |
| Registry | Qualified Cryptocurrency Registry | DAKD of ICEX and CFX, which record legal tradeability, not Sharia status |
| Supervisory cooperation | Cooperation among regulators | Domestic coordination within the digital asset ecosystem; no cross-border mechanism |

The correspondence is real but partial. Indonesian practice shows that several of the mechanisms proposed here are administratively feasible: assessment against explicit criteria, graduated admission through a sandbox, and published lists of admitted assets. Indonesia lacks precisely the elements that distinguish the IIFRC proposal: a Sharia dimension in the assessment and a cross-border mechanism of recognition. The framework proposed in this chapter should therefore be read as an extension of emerging practice rather than as a codification of practice already in place.

### 6.10.3 GOIDR (Gold Indonesia Republic) Token: Pathway A Implementation in Real Time

The GOIDR gold token of PT Indonesia Blockchain Persada graduated from the OJK sandbox on 8 August 2025. By October 2026 it was listed on both licensed exchanges: on ICEX with a "Pass With Notes" flag, and on CFX following a change of ticker from GIDR to GOIDR (Chapter 4, §4.1.9). It is the closest existing example of the asset-backing pathway (Pathway A, Chapter 5.4), and it illustrates four features relevant to harmonisation. The assessment of these features is the author's.

**Transparency of assessment.** The token has a verifiable underlying asset: physical gold held by Pegadaian, according to OJK. Its dimensional scores could therefore be assessed and disclosed publicly, though no such disclosure exists yet, and the methodology would be reproducible in other jurisdictions.

**A pathway to Sharia compliance.** In the author's assessment, the token falls within the exception of the November 2021 Ijtima' Ulama decision, because it has a clear underlying asset. OJK's 2025 presentation described the model as meeting point 3 of the 2021 MUI decision, but that is the regulator's reading; no DSN-MUI fatwa or AFSI determination on the token has been located. The model could be replicated in other jurisdictions using local underlying assets.

**A foundation for mutual recognition.** Regulatory recognition by OJK allows institutional participation. According to OJK (2025), one token equals one gram of gold and may be redeemed for physical gold through the GIDR wallet; the detailed redemption terms remain to be confirmed from the issuer's documentation. Because the token moves on a blockchain, it could in principle be transferred across borders without concentrating custody.

**A source of data.** The listing will generate evidence on demand for asset-backed tokens among Muslim investors, on whether Sharia characteristics affect adoption, and on the efficiency of settlement. That evidence could inform future standard-setting by an IIFRC.

### 6.10.4 Implications for Inter-Islamic Regulatory Coordination

Indonesian practice yields three lessons for the design of the IIFRC.

**The dimensional framework can reduce the burden of assessment.** If OJK, or a Sharia body working with it, scored GOIDR on the five dimensions, other regulators could apply the same assessment to their own markets. A Malaysian regulator could test it against its own Sharia screening, and a Saudi regulator could decide whether its policy permits assets of that category. The assessment would be done once and applied many times, with implementation adapted to each jurisdiction.

**Coordination may begin as cooperation within an ecosystem rather than as formal authority.** Indonesia coordinates the digital asset sector through industry associations, the regulator's sandbox and published asset lists, rather than through a formal council with binding powers. This suggests that harmonisation across jurisdictions could also begin with voluntary coordination among leading regulators and build towards a formal institution as practice converges. The Indonesian case also shows the limit of that approach. After more than a year of coordination, there is still no Sharia standard, which suggests that some formal mandate is needed for the Sharia dimension.

**Sandboxes allow low-risk experimentation.** GOIDR's passage from the sandbox to exchange listing allowed OJK to gather data on demand and operational risk and to refine its requirements, without exposing the wider market to systemic risk. The IIFRC should adopt the same experimental logic. Rather than freezing standards from above, it should establish a flexible framework that permits controlled innovation and iterative improvement.

---

## 6.11 Conclusion

The recommendations of this chapter respond to the fragmentation documented in Chapter 4 while respecting legitimate differences between jurisdictions. The Inter-Islamic Finance Regulatory Council provides an institutional framework for coordination, and minimum standards combined with mutual recognition allow practical convergence. A graduated enforcement ladder grounded in *siyasah shar'iyyah*, *ta'zir* and *hisbah* gives that convergence consequence without compromising sovereignty.

The framework balances two imperatives. The first is convergence towards common standards, which reduces arbitrage and the burden of duplicated assessment. The second is flexibility for each jurisdiction to reflect its own priorities, whether innovation, stability, consumer protection or monetary control. The result is structured harmonisation: a unified methodology of assessment with differentiated models of implementation.

Indonesian practice since 2025 shows that several of these mechanisms are administratively feasible at the national level. It also shows that the Sharia dimension does not emerge on its own from regulatory practice. It must be built deliberately. If the three-phase roadmap is pursued from 2027, Islamic jurisdictions could by the early 2030s offer Islamic financial institutions a unified framework for operating across borders, support digital asset innovation consistent with Islamic principles, and establish the ecosystem of Islamic finance as a reference point for the regulation of digital assets.
