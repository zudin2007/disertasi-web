## 1. INTRODUCTION: THE CLASSIFICATION CRISIS

### 1.1 Problem Statement

Islamic finance faces an unprecedented problem of classification as cryptocurrency spreads across Muslim-majority societies. Indonesia illustrates the scale. By August 2026, the market supervised by the Financial Services Authority (OJK) counted 23.21 million crypto-asset accounts, with total assets of Rp54.62 trillion and 34 licensed entities (OJK, 2026). Comparable markets have grown in Malaysia, Pakistan, Turkey and the Gulf. Global rankings of adoption consistently place several Muslim-majority countries near the top, although the figures depend heavily on methodology. [Unverified: country rankings and user estimates should be cited from a dated Chainalysis Global Crypto Adoption Index.]

Despite this penetration, no binding Islamic jurisprudential standard for digital assets exists. Instead, the field operates under what may be called a *tripartite fragmentation*. Three distinct jurisprudential positions coexist, each defended by respected scholars and fatwa bodies, and there is no established mechanism for resolving the differences between them.

The fragmentation has immediate consequences for policy. In Indonesia, the Seventh Ijtima' Ulama of the MUI Fatwa Commission ruled in November 2021 that cryptocurrency is *haram* as currency. As a commodity, it held, cryptocurrency is invalid for trade unless it meets the Sharia requirements of a tradeable good with a clear underlying asset and a clear benefit (Keputusan Ijtima' Ulama Komisi Fatwa se-Indonesia VII, 2021). The financial regulator, OJK, has meanwhile assumed supervision of crypto-assets under POJK No. 27/2024, effective January 2025 and amended by POJK No. 23/2025 (enacted 31 October 2025). This is a religiously neutral licensing regime. A study of Sharia classification conducted with the National Sharia Board (DSN-MUI) remains unresolved as of October 2026, so the two normative systems are formally unconnected (see Chapter 4, §4.1).

In Malaysia, the Securities Commission regulates digital assets. Its Shariah Advisory Council resolved in 2020 that investing and trading in digital currencies and tokens on registered exchanges is permissible in principle, and since March 2026 its guidelines require exchanges to obtain the Council's endorsement before offering a digital currency as Sharia-compliant. The UAE and Bahrain have established comprehensive, religiously neutral licensing frameworks for virtual assets: VARA in Dubai, the FSRA in Abu Dhabi and the Central Bank of Bahrain. Saudi Arabia, by contrast, maintains official caution. The Saudi Central Bank (SAMA) offers no permission for cryptocurrency as a medium of exchange or investment, while pursuing central bank digital currency experiments.

This divergence between jurisdictions creates cascading problems for three groups. Regulators must decide how to design prudential rules for digital assets when their jurisprudential foundation is contested. They must choose whether cryptocurrency should be supervised like a currency, with strict oversight of monetary mechanics, like a commodity, with oversight of market trading, or like an Islamic financial instrument, with supervision by a Sharia board. Practitioners, meaning Islamic banks and fintech companies seeking to offer Sharia-compliant products, lack clear technical specifications. They do not know what a product must do to qualify as compliant, or whether the classification of the underlying asset suffices or the contractual mechanics of the product must also satisfy additional requirements. Users, the millions of Muslims who hold crypto-assets, lack authoritative guidance on whether they may hold Bitcoin, trade it, or participate in decentralised finance. The absence of agreement creates a compliance vacuum, in which users either follow individual scholars, at the risk of criticism from others, or act under religious uncertainty.

### 1.2 Research Question

This chapter addresses the first of the dissertation's three research questions:

**RQ1:** How do Islamic jurisprudential frameworks classify cryptocurrency (currency vs. commodity), and what are the methodological reasons for divergence across Indonesia, Malaysia, Saudi Arabia, Bahrain, and UAE?

The chapter's central argument is that the present fragmentation does not stem from ignorance or from a simple disagreement about facts. It reflects genuine methodological divergence. Scholars ask different foundational questions and apply different tools of Islamic legal reasoning, including analogy (*qiyas*), juristic preference (*istihsan*), public interest (*maslahah*) and the linkage of an instrument to its underlying asset. They thereby arrive at conclusions that are internally sound but mutually contradictory. Understanding these methodological differences is a precondition both for clarifying the scholarly positions and for identifying possible pathways to agreement.

### 1.3 Chapter Structure and Contribution

The chapter proceeds in six steps. Section 2 traces how cryptocurrency entered the discourse of Islamic finance and which earlier classifications of assets scholars drew upon. Section 3 sets out each of the three jurisprudential positions in detail, with its primary sources and underlying reasoning. Section 4 compares the methods each position employs and identifies where they agree and where their disagreement becomes fundamental. Section 5 applies the objectives of the law (*maqasid*) to derive a five-dimensional specification of compliance that can be used across positions. Section 6 proposes a unified framework of classification that acknowledges the three positions, identifies areas of agreement, and suggests what evidence could shift them. Section 7 concludes and explains how the jurisprudential analysis will be tested against market reality in the chapters that follow.

The chapter fills three gaps in the literature. First, existing studies catalogue different fatwa positions but rarely compare their methodologies systematically; this chapter makes that comparison explicit. Second, scholarship on Islamic finance articulates the *maqasid* in the abstract but seldom operationalises them as testable criteria of compliance; this chapter develops five concrete dimensions. Third, the literature tends to assume that the three positions are irreconcilable; this chapter identifies specific areas in which they could converge in light of new evidence or clarification.

## 2. HISTORICAL CONTEXT: EMERGENCE OF DIGITAL ASSETS IN ISLAMIC FINANCE DISCOURSE

### 2.1 Pre-Cryptocurrency Islamic Finance Framework

To understand how Islamic jurisprudence addresses cryptocurrency, it is necessary to recognise the conceptual framework it inherited. Classical jurisprudence developed detailed categories for financial matters, among them money (*al-naqd* or *thaman*), property (*al-mal*), custom (*al-'urf*) and contract (*al-'aqd*), each carrying specific legal consequences. Money was subject to the rules of *riba*, so that gold and silver had to be exchanged in equal measure and on the spot when exchanged for their own kind. Property could be traded at market rates but was subject to other restrictions; the sale of wine (*khamr*), for example, was prohibited on grounds of its substance. Contracts required offer and acceptance, the capacity of the parties and a lawful subject matter.

Classical jurists also confronted media of exchange that lacked the intrinsic value of gold and silver. The most important were copper coins (*fulus*), whose exchange value exceeded the value of their metal. The jurists debated whether *fulus* should be treated as money subject to the rules of *riba* or as ordinary commodities. Imam Malik observed that if people adopted leather as currency, he would dislike its exchange for gold or silver on deferred terms. Ibn Taymiyyah (d. 728/1328) argued that the dirham and dinar have no natural or scriptural definition and that their status rests on custom. Al-Maqrizi (d. 845/1442) analysed the economic harm caused by the debasement of *fulus* in Mamluk Egypt. When paper currency spread in Muslim societies in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, modern jurists extended this earlier debate, asking whether paper money should be treated as a representation of gold and silver, as a commodity, or as an independent form of money. The prevailing answer, adopted by the Saudi Council of Senior Scholars in 1973 and later by the International Islamic Fiqh Academy, was that it is an independent form of *thaman* (Chapter 2, §2.0.1). The method that emerged was to assess a new financial form by asking which established category of Islamic law it most closely resembles.

### 2.2 The Fintech Transition (2008–2017)

The global financial crisis of 2008 and the rapid innovation in financial technology that followed obliged Islamic finance to engage with digital assets more broadly. Mobile banking, digital wallets and, eventually, blockchain applications raised new questions. Could money exist purely in digital form, without physical notes? How does the prohibition of *riba* apply when settlement becomes near-instantaneous and fully auditable? Could self-executing smart contracts replace the approval of a human Sharia board if they were properly programmed?

Centres of Islamic finance such as Malaysia and Bahrain began to issue guidance on digital payments and financial technology in the late 2010s. Bahrain's regulatory sandbox for financial technology dates from 2017, and Malaysia's framework for digital banks from 2020. These early frameworks established that digital representation does not in itself disqualify an instrument from being Sharia-compliant. What matters is whether the underlying transaction complies with Islamic principles.

### 2.3 The Cryptocurrency Pivot (2017–Present)

Cryptocurrency introduced three complications absent from earlier discussions of digital assets.

The first was **decentralisation without a sovereign or institutional issuer**. Fiat currency derives its authority from a state, and corporate digital tokens from an issuing entity. Bitcoin and most other cryptocurrencies are issued by no single authority; miners or validators perform protocol-level functions. This challenged assumptions about who bears responsibility for compliance and how Sharia oversight could be exercised.

The second was the **predominance of speculative trading**. Earlier digital assets, such as online banking and e-wallets, served mainly as means of payment or storage. In cryptocurrency markets the relationship was reversed: most trading volume is speculative rather than payment-based (Chainalysis, 2025). This raised immediate concerns under the *maqasid* about *maysir* and *gharar*.

The third was **programmed scarcity and algorithmic monetary policy**. Bitcoin's supply is capped by its protocol, and Ether's issuance is governed algorithmically. This raised philosophical questions. Does a fixed supply constitute a kind of intrinsic value? Does algorithmic rather than human monetary policy change the Sharia assessment? Does the combination create a new category of asset requiring new jurisprudence?

AAOIFI, the principal standard-setter in Islamic finance, addressed digital assets at the 21st AAOIFI Shari'ah Boards Conference in Manama in May 2023. Koutoub Moustapha Sano, Secretary General of the International Islamic Fiqh Academy, is reported to have defined digital assets there as anything that can be stored and transmitted electronically and is linked to rights of ownership or use. He added that the ruling on these and other new transactions must be based on the general texts of the Shari'ah, its general maxims and its higher objectives (Sano, 2023). [Unverified: the wording should be checked against the conference record before being quoted.] The statement was not a binding ruling. As of October 2026, AAOIFI has issued no dedicated standard on digital assets (Chapter 3, §3.1.2). This silence is itself significant, since it has effectively left jurisdictional bodies to develop their own frameworks, which helps to explain the present fragmentation.

### 2.4 Three Parallel Jurisprudential Responses (2017–2026)

Against this background, three distinct jurisprudential responses crystallised between 2017 and 2026, each grounded in a different view of how Islamic law should approach unprecedented financial innovation. They are set out in Section 3 and are used as the dissertation's common taxonomy throughout. **Position A** treats crypto-assets as digital property that is conditionally permissible. **Position B** treats them as prohibited, at least under present conditions. **Position C** defers judgment (*tawaqquf*).

## 3. THE THREE JURISPRUDENTIAL POSITIONS

### 3.1 POSITION A: CRYPTOCURRENCY AS DIGITAL PROPERTY (CONDITIONALLY PERMISSIBLE)

#### 3.1.1 Position Overview and Primary Advocates

The first position classifies cryptocurrency as property (*mal*). It concludes that holding and trading cryptocurrency is permissible (*mubah*), subject to conditions concerning speculation, transparency and, for some advocates, backing. Use as money in jurisdictions whose law excludes it is not permitted.

The position has institutional and scholarly advocates. Its clearest institutional expressions are the 2026 determinations of Muhammadiyah, which held that crypto may constitute *mal mutaqawwam* and that transactions are *mubah* subject to conditions, and of the 35th Muktamar of the Nahdlatul Ulama, which held that Bitcoin meets the criteria of *mal* and *thaman* and is valid as a digital asset (Chapter 3, §3.4.5). The 2020 resolution of the Shariah Advisory Council of the Securities Commission Malaysia also belongs here, as does the asset-side exception in the 2021 MUI decision. Among individual scholars, the position has been argued by Mufti Faraz Adam (United Kingdom) and by Mufti Muhammad Abu-Bakar, whose 2018 analysis for Blossom Finance concluded that Bitcoin can be treated as *mal* and is in principle permissible. Research papers from institutions in Malaysia and Indonesia have developed it further. Regulators such as VARA and the Central Bank of Bahrain do not adopt Sharia positions, but their licensing regimes create the conditions under which Position A can be applied in practice.

#### 3.1.2 Core Jurisprudential Argument

The argument of Position A begins with an analogy: cryptocurrency should be treated like precious metals or other commodities rather than like fiat currency.

**Definition.** Cryptocurrency, on this view, shares the properties of pre-modern commodity money. Its supply is finite (Bitcoin's is capped at 21 million), it is divisible into tradeable fractions, and it is portable and not tied to geography. It can function as a medium of exchange, although that use is currently limited, and it does not depend on any sovereign or institutional credit.

**Analogy (*qiyas*).** Given these properties, the most fitting analogies are to assets that Islamic law permits to be owned and traded. These are gold and silver, agricultural commodities such as wheat and dates, which may be bought and sold at market rates, and digital goods such as software and intellectual property, which modern scholars accept as property.

***Riba*.** If cryptocurrency is a commodity, the rules of *riba al-fadl* and *riba al-nasi'ah* apply only to exchanges of like for like within the *ribawi* categories. Exchanging cryptocurrency for fiat currency would then be permissible, as is the exchange of gold for currency, subject to spot settlement where gold or silver is involved. Exchanging one cryptocurrency for another at fair market rates would also be permissible. Lending cryptocurrency for a guaranteed return, such as lending one Bitcoin to be repaid as one and a half, would be prohibited.

***Gharar*.** Proponents acknowledge that *gharar* is relevant but argue that managed uncertainty is not prohibited. Every commodity market involves uncertainty about future price. *Gharar* is excessive only where neither party understands the transaction, where the outcome is determined by chance, or where the transaction serves no economic purpose beyond wagering. On this view, trading in cryptocurrency can satisfy Islamic requirements under three conditions: traders understand that they are dealing in volatile assets, regulation limits leverage and speculation, and Sharia boards monitor for *maysir*.

#### 3.1.3 Malaysia's Operational Model (Case Study of Position A)

Malaysia provides the clearest practical expression of Position A. Its framework has three elements.

**Classification and licensing.** Digital currencies and digital tokens are prescribed as securities under the capital markets legislation. Digital asset exchanges must register with the Securities Commission, and Bank Negara Malaysia has confirmed that digital assets are not legal tender.

**Sharia screening.** The Shariah Advisory Council of the Securities Commission resolved in 2020 that investing and trading in digital currencies and tokens on registered exchanges is permissible in principle, subject to its requirements. The Council maintains a list of digital assets it regards as compliant; Stellar, for example, was added after a meeting in December 2024. Since a revision of the Securities Commission's Guidelines on Islamic Capital Market Products and Services on 30 March 2026, exchanges offering a digital currency as Sharia-compliant must first obtain the Council's endorsement.

**Market conduct.** Market conduct and investor protection rules apply under the Securities Commission's framework. [Unverified: whether specific assets such as Bitcoin and Ether are on the Council's list, and the detailed screening criteria, should be confirmed from the Council's published resolutions.]

The Malaysian model shows that Position A can be operationalised through *ex ante* screening. A jurisprudential judgment becomes an enumerated list of tradeable assets administered by a public Sharia authority. Regulated trading in Malaysia grew from RM13.93 billion in 2024 to RM17.14 billion in 2025, according to press reports of official data. [Unverified: figures from secondary reports.]

#### 3.1.4 Jurisprudential Precedents Within Islamic Law

Proponents of Position A cite the capacity of Islamic jurisprudence to absorb new forms of asset.

The first precedent is token money. Classical jurists accepted *fulus*, copper coins whose value exceeded their metal content, as money by custom. Modern jurists accepted paper currency as an independent form of *thaman*, although it lacks any commodity backing. Fiat currency is now accepted throughout Islamic finance.

The second precedent is the share in a joint-stock company. Shares were novel when they emerged, and Islamic law came to accept them as valid property by analogy with partnership interests and other forms of property.

The third precedent is intellectual property. Patents and copyrights, assets with no physical form, are now widely accepted as *mal*; the International Islamic Fiqh Academy recognised such rights in 1988.

From these precedents proponents argue that cryptocurrency is simply the latest such innovation. Just as Islamic jurisprudence integrated paper money and intangible property rights, it can accommodate digital assets of this kind.

### 3.2 POSITION B: CRYPTOCURRENCY AS MEDIUM OF EXCHANGE (LIKELY PROHIBITED)

#### 3.2.1 Position Overview and Primary Advocates

The second position begins from a different classification. It treats cryptocurrency as a form of money or medium of exchange rather than as a commodity, or denies that it is property at all, and concludes that its use and trading should be prohibited or severely restricted.

Its principal advocates are four. Dar al-Ifta al-Misriyyah ruled in 2017/2018, under Grand Mufti Shawki Allam, that Bitcoin trading is impermissible. Mufti Muhammad Taqi Usmani, with the Darul Uloom Karachi fatwa of 10 June 2026, adopted the position in its strictest form. MUI's Fatwa Commission adopts it with respect to the use of crypto as currency, in the 2021 Ijtima' Ulama decision; DSN-MUI has issued no fatwa on crypto-assets as of October 2026. Turkey's Presidency of Religious Affairs (Diyanet) issued a restrictive statement in 2017. Saudi Arabia's posture of non-recognition is consistent with the position but is a regulatory stance rather than a fatwa.

Prohibitive views have also been expressed by individual scholars associated with Saudi and other institutions. They are not, however, formal institutional rulings, and they are reported here only as secondary accounts (Erismen, 2025).

#### 3.2.2 Core Jurisprudential Argument

Proponents of Position B reject the commodity analogy on the ground that cryptocurrency functions in practice as money, or as nothing at all, rather than as a commodity. Their argument has five steps.

**Definition.** Cryptocurrency was designed as money. Bitcoin's white paper describes it as "a purely peer-to-peer version of electronic cash" (Nakamoto, 2008). In practice, most holders treat it as a store of value or a vehicle of speculation, not as an input to production. On this view its volatility is irrelevant to its function, since fiat currency also has no commodity backing and varies in value. Cryptocurrency is therefore money, even though it is issued privately and without a central authority.

***Riba* and monetary legitimacy.** Islamic law treats money strictly. Historically, gold and silver were the standard, and they had use value of their own. Modern fiat currency derives its value from state decree and general acceptance. Cryptocurrency fails both tests: it lacks state backing, which legitimises fiat money, and it lacks the use value of gold and silver. It is therefore neither legitimate fiat money nor legitimate commodity money, which makes its use as a medium of exchange suspect.

***Gharar*.** Money must have reasonably stable purchasing power to function as a reliable medium of exchange. Bitcoin's price fluctuates so widely that holders cannot know, even approximately, what purchasing power they hold. This, on the argument of Position B, defeats the principle that the parties to a transaction must know with reasonable certainty the value they exchange, and excessive *gharar* renders the transaction void (*batil*).

***Maysir*.** Current markets are dominantly speculative: most volume consists of trading for profit, not payment. Restrictive scholars emphasise that the dominant motivation of trading is capital gain without regard to underlying value or use. That matches the definition of *maysir* as wagering without productive purpose. Even if some uses of cryptocurrency might be permissible in theory, current practice is therefore *maysir*. The emphasis attributed here to restrictive scholars, including Usmani, is the author's paraphrase; Usmani's documented 2026 ruling rests instead on the denial of *mal*-status (§3.2.5).

**Absence of value.** A classical principle holds that valid wealth must have intrinsic or functional value. Precious metals have functional value, since they can be worked into ornaments and were used as coinage. Fiat currency has functional value as legal tender, accepted by the state in payment of taxes. Cryptocurrency, on this argument, has neither: it is not legal tender and has no functional use beyond speculation. It is therefore not valid *mal*. The 2026 Karachi fatwa states this conclusion in its strongest form, describing a crypto balance as a record of notional numbers.

#### 3.2.3 Primary Sources: The Prohibition Rulings

**Indonesia: the Seventh Ijtima' Ulama of the MUI Fatwa Commission (9–11 November 2021).** The Indonesian ruling was issued by the Fatwa Commission's national forum, not by DSN-MUI, a distinction whose regulatory consequences are examined in Chapter 4 (§4.1). It comprises three operative points:

1. The use of cryptocurrency **as currency is *haram***, on grounds of *gharar* (excessive uncertainty) and *dharar* (harm), and because it contravenes Law No. 7/2011 on Currency and Bank Indonesia Regulation No. 17/2015.
2. Cryptocurrency **as a commodity or digital asset is invalid for trade** (*tidak sah diperjualbelikan*), on grounds of *gharar*, *dharar* and *qimar* (gambling), and because it fails the requirements of a Sharia-valid tradeable good (*sil'ah*): existence, definite value, ascertainable quantity, established ownership and deliverability.
3. **Exception:** cryptocurrency that meets the *sil'ah* requirements, possesses an underlying asset, and has a clear benefit **is valid for trade**.

Two aspects deserve emphasis. First, the ruling's grounds are *gharar*, *dharar* and *qimar*, not the triad of *gharar*, *maysir* and *riba* frequently attributed to it in secondary accounts. Second, point 3 makes the prohibition conditional. The ruling itself constructs the category under which asset-backed digital assets can be valid, a category that Indonesian regulatory practice has since begun to populate (Chapter 4, §4.1.8–4.1.9). In the taxonomy of this dissertation, the decision belongs to Position B on the monetary question and to a conditional form of Position A on the asset question.

**Egypt: the ruling of Dar al-Ifta under Grand Mufti Shawki Allam (2017/2018).** Egypt's fatwa authority ruled cryptocurrency trading impermissible. As reported, its reasoning holds that cryptocurrency is not accepted as a medium of exchange by the legitimate authorities, is not subject to state supervision, exposes users to fraud and deception, and is volatile in a way that can harm individuals and states (Dar al-Ifta al-Misriyyah, 2017/2018). Unlike the Indonesian ruling, the Egyptian fatwa articulates no exception category. The ruling is presented here as sourced paraphrase only, since the Arabic text on dar-alifta.org should be consulted before any verbatim excerpt is given.

**The wider map of early prohibitions.** The Indonesian and Egyptian rulings sit within a broader first wave of restrictive responses. According to a secondary survey, this wave included Turkey's Presidency of Religious Affairs (Diyanet), statements by members of Saudi Arabia's Council of Senior Scholars (including the royal adviser Abdullah bin Muhammad al-Mutlaq), the Palestinian fatwa authority, and the Fourth Doha Islamic Finance Conference, which recommended restrictions on the use of cryptocurrency (Erismen, 2025). The map also contains a position often omitted from three-way classifications: principled abstention (*tawaqquf*). Its example is Sheikh Abdurrahman al-Barrak (Saudi Arabia), who declined to rule on the permissibility of trading but held that zakat is due on crypto holdings that reach the *nisab* and complete the *hawl* (Erismen, 2025). That view implicitly treats cryptocurrency as *mal*, as zakatable wealth, while withholding judgment on trading it. The abstention matters analytically, because it shows that even within restrictive scholarship the property status of cryptocurrency and the permissibility of trading it are separable questions.

**The character of the prohibitions: *al-man'u*, not necessarily *tahrim*.** Abdul Sattar Abu Ghuddah, chairman of the Al Baraka Group's Sharia board, has characterised the restrictive rulings as *al-man'u*: a preventive restriction within the discretionary authority (*siyasah shar'iyyah*) of the public authority (*wali al-amr*, represented by central banks) to secure the public interest. On this reading they are not *tahrim*, a categorical prohibition of the thing in itself. Two considerations support this reading. First, cryptocurrency belongs to the novel matters (*al-mustajiddat*), where the operative maxim is that the default is permissibility (*al-asl fi al-ashya' al-ibahah*). Second, the prohibitions of *dharar* and *dirar* justify state restriction of genuinely harmful instruments without settling their intrinsic status (Erismen, 2025). On this reading, rulings of Position B are structurally revisable as regulatory conditions change. The Indonesian test of that hypothesis is examined in Chapter 4 (§4.1). The Karachi fatwa of 2026 does not fit this reading, since its denial of *mal*-status is a claim about the nature of the thing itself.

**Terminological anchor.** Throughout this dissertation, the operative definitions of the prohibited elements follow DSN-MUI Fatwa No. 117/DSN-MUI/II/2018 (General Provisions 18–22):

- *riba* is an increment in the exchange of *ribawi* goods (*riba fadl*), or an increment stipulated on a debt principal in return for deferral (*riba nasi'ah*);
- *gharar* is uncertainty in a contract concerning the quality, quantity or delivery of its object;
- *maysir* is any contract undertaken with an unclear purpose and imprecise calculation, speculation or chance;
- *tadlis* is the concealment of a defect in the object by the seller in order to deceive the buyer;
- *dharar* is an act that causes harm or loss to another party.

Adopting the fatwa's own definitions, rather than definitions from secondary literature, anchors the analysis in the same normative vocabulary that Indonesian authorities themselves use.

These sources show the core rationale of the position. Most advocates of Position B do not claim that cryptocurrency can never be permitted. They argue that its current market structure, which is speculation-dominant, unregulated and lacking intrinsic value, makes it impermissible now. This leaves an opening for Position C, and for movement within Position B, if circumstances change. The Karachi fatwa is again the exception, because its reasoning concerns the nature of the asset rather than the state of the market.

#### 3.2.4 Dar al-Ifta Egypt's 2017 Ruling (Case Study of Position B)

The Egyptian ruling exemplifies the reasoning of Position B. It rests on the absence of authoritative backing and supervision, the exposure of users to fraud and *gharar*, and the harm caused by volatility. It articulates no exception category, in contrast to the third point of the Indonesian ruling, which makes it among the most categorical of the major prohibitive rulings. It did not distinguish between different cryptocurrencies, for instance between Bitcoin and stablecoins, or between different uses, such as payment and speculation; its prohibition applied broadly.

The ruling was among the earliest pronouncements of a state fatwa authority and is widely cited in subsequent scholarship on the prohibitive position. The Indonesian ruling of 2021 rests on partially overlapping grounds, namely *gharar* and harm to the public. It adds grounds of its own, *qimar* and conflict with the national currency law, and, crucially, an exception category absent from the Egyptian ruling.

#### 3.2.5 Mufti Taqi Usmani's Jurisprudentially Nuanced Position B

**The documented ruling (June 2026).** The only datable crypto-specific ruling located is the fatwa of the Darul Ifta of Jamia Darul Uloom Karachi, dated 10 June 2026 and signed by Mufti Muhammad Taqi Usmani with five other scholars. As reported in July 2026, it holds that cryptocurrency, including stablecoins such as USDT, is not *mal* but a record of notional numbers, and that purchases made with it are impermissible (Chapter 3, §3.3.2). This places Usmani's documented position at the strictest end of Position B. It is stricter than the "bridge" reading set out below suggests, because it denies the property status of the asset rather than merely objecting to the current market.

**Analytical reconstruction.** The layered analysis and conditional framework that follow are the author's reconstruction of how Usmani's general methodology in Islamic finance *could* be applied to cryptocurrency. They are not a summary of published rulings by Usmani on cryptocurrency. Where they conflict with the 2026 fatwa, the fatwa prevails.

**A layered method.** On the reconstruction, the method distinguishes three levels of analysis. At the level of theoretical permissibility, a digital asset could in principle satisfy the requirements of Islamic law if it were properly designed and used. At the level of actual market structure, current cryptocurrency markets exhibit dominant features, namely speculation, volatility and *maysir*, that conflict with Islamic principles. At the level of jurisdictional maturity, the assessment at the second level could change as markets mature and are integrated into regulated systems.

**Intrinsic value and valid wealth.** A central question is what makes something valid wealth. The 2026 fatwa answers it negatively for crypto-assets: a balance of cryptocurrency is a record of numbers, not a thing that can be owned. Secondary accounts of Usmani's position also hold that a medium of exchange must derive its standing from intrinsic utility, from state backing as legal tender, or from linkage to productive assets, conditions that cryptocurrency does not currently meet (Muneeza et al., 2023; full reference to be added to the reference list). [A direct citation to a primary text by Usmani, such as the Urdu or Arabic text of the fatwa, is required before final submission.]

***Gharar*.** On uncertainty, the reconstruction distinguishes the ordinary commercial *gharar* tolerated in commodity markets, where an underlying good with real supply and demand exists, from the excessive *gharar* of an asset whose price has no referent beyond collective sentiment. The distinction is jurisprudentially orthodox. Its application to cryptocurrency is where Positions A and B part ways (see Section 5).

***Maysir*.** The empirical premise of the *maysir* argument is that speculative trading, not payment, dominates cryptocurrency transactions. Industry analyses consistently support this qualitative claim. [Specific payment-share figures to be cited from the Chainalysis Geography of Cryptocurrency reports or BIS working papers.] On this premise, scholars of Position B conclude that participation in current markets is wagering on price movements without productive purpose, which is the definition of *maysir*.

**Conditions under which the assessment could change.** On the reconstruction, the ruling could in principle change under four conditions. The 2026 fatwa identifies no such conditions, and its treatment of stablecoins cuts against the second.

1. **If cryptocurrency came to be used primarily for payment**, its legitimate utility would be demonstrated, the concern of *maysir* would be substantially reduced, and *gharar* could be managed through regulatory limits on positions.
2. **If cryptocurrency were backed by productive assets**, such as stablecoins collateralised by income-generating assets or sukuk, it would acquire value of its own, speculative distortion could be contained, and concerns of *riba* could be addressed through Sharia-compliant collateral.
3. **If cryptocurrency were integrated into regulated financial systems with Sharia oversight**, position limits could prevent *maysir*, Sharia boards could monitor compliance continuously, and restrictions on leverage and derivatives would limit speculation.
4. **If volatility were structurally reduced**, for example through widespread adoption as a stable medium of exchange, *gharar* would approach acceptable levels.

### 3.3 POSITION C: CRYPTOCURRENCY AS EVOLVING ASSET (DEFERRED JUDGMENT)

#### 3.3.1 Position Overview and Primary Advocates

The third position takes a fundamentally different approach. Rather than placing cryptocurrency in an existing category of Islamic law, as property or as money, it holds that cryptocurrency is a genuinely novel class of asset. On this view its Sharia status cannot be definitively determined until the maturation of the market clarifies its actual function and risk profile.

The clearest institutional expression of this position is Resolution 237 (24/8) of the International Islamic Fiqh Academy, adopted in Dubai in November 2019. It called for further research on the rulings applicable to cryptocurrencies rather than issuing a ruling, and a follow-up symposium was held in Jeddah in November 2021. The *tawaqquf* of Sheikh Abdurrahman al-Barrak, noted in §3.2.3, is an individual expression of the same stance. AAOIFI's continuing silence, pending its forthcoming position paper, has a similar practical effect, though it is not a declared position. Some academic scholars likewise argue that empirical evidence should precede any definitive ruling.

#### 3.3.2 Core Jurisprudential Argument

Proponents of Position C begin from a meta-jurisprudential claim: analogical reasoning (*qiyas*) may be inappropriate for cryptocurrency because cryptocurrency lacks sufficient resemblance to any established category of asset.

**Rejection of forced analogy.** The commodity analogy of Position A breaks down, on this view, because most use of cryptocurrency is speculative rather than commodity-based. The money analogy of Position B breaks down because cryptocurrency lacks the state backing and legal tender status that historically legitimised money. Forcing cryptocurrency into either category may violate a basic principle of Islamic legal reasoning, since *qiyas* requires substantial similarity between the new case and the precedent.

**An evolving-asset doctrine.** Proponents argue that some assets can be properly classified only after their market function has stabilised. Paper currency circulated in Muslim societies for decades before the major juristic councils settled its status in the 1970s and 1980s. Corporate shares similarly required extended observation before AAOIFI could issue standards on them. Intellectual property is a modern invention that required a new category rather than a comfortable fit with the classical ones.

**The requirement of empirical evidence.** On this view, a Sharia assessment requires three questions to be answered. What is the asset's actual function in markets, not in theory but in observed reality? Is it stable enough to be evaluated against Sharia principles, given that *gharar* is difficult to assess if the asset is fundamentally volatile? And what evidence would move it towards permissibility or prohibition?

**Jurisdictional maturity.** Proponents distinguish current cryptocurrency markets, which are immature, speculation-dominated and fragmented by regulation, from potential future markets. If cryptocurrency became payment-dominated, regulated and backed by real assets, the assessment could change markedly.

**Practical implications.** Position C is neither an indefinite prohibition nor an unconditional permission. Unlike Position B, it does not issue a permanent ruling of *haram*. Unlike Position A, it does not permit all current uses. In practice, some of its proponents favour conditional permission in regulated environments with Sharia supervision, pending evidence of market maturation. Others counsel abstention until a ruling is made.

#### 3.3.3 Institutional Implementation: UAE and Bahrain Models

The regulatory frameworks of the UAE and Bahrain are sometimes read as institutional analogues of Position C, because they admit crypto-assets under supervision while leaving their Sharia status open. That reading must be stated with care. Neither framework adopts a Sharia position.

**UAE.** Dubai's Virtual Assets Regulatory Authority (VARA), established under Dubai Law No. 4 of 2022, licenses and supervises virtual asset service providers under its Virtual Assets and Related Activities Regulations (2023) and accompanying rulebooks. Abu Dhabi's Financial Services Regulatory Authority (FSRA) regulates virtual assets in the Abu Dhabi Global Market. Both frameworks are religiously neutral: they do not require Sharia review of virtual assets. Sharia considerations enter only where an Islamic financial institution or an issuer chooses to offer a Sharia-compliant product subject to its own Sharia governance.

**Bahrain.** The Central Bank of Bahrain regulates crypto-asset services under the crypto-asset module of Volume 6 (Capital Markets) of its Rulebook. In July 2025 it added a Stablecoin Issuance and Offering (SIO) module, which requires issuers to be licensed, to hold high-quality liquid reserves and to honour redemption requests generally within five business days. Like the Emirati frameworks, Bahrain's regime is prudential rather than Sharia-based. [Unverified: the date and contents of the original crypto-asset module should be confirmed against the CBB Rulebook.]

What the Emirati and Bahraini frameworks share with Position C is not a Sharia judgment but a method. They admit crypto-assets under supervision and adjust their requirements as evidence accumulates. That method creates the conditions in which a deferred Sharia judgment could later be made on the basis of observed market function.

## 4. COMPARATIVE JURISPRUDENTIAL METHODOLOGY

### 4.1 Methodological Framework Comparison

The three positions differ less on facts, on which they largely agree, than on the method by which Islamic law is applied to an unprecedented situation. Table 1.1 summarises the differences.

**Table 1.1: Methodological comparison of the three positions**

| Dimension | Position A | Position B | Position C |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Analogy (Qiyas)** | Commodity → permissible | Money → prohibited (or not *mal* at all) | No direct analogy applicable |
| **Evidence Base** | Market potential (if regulated) | Current market reality (speculation), or the nature of the asset | Conditional on market evolution |
| **Decisiveness** | Definitive now (conditional) | Definitive now (prohibited) | Deferred (pending evidence) |
| **Revision Mechanism** | If conditions are met | If the market changes fundamentally (not under the Karachi reasoning) | Built in through empirical review |
| **Time Horizon** | Present assessment | Present assessment | Future assessment |

### 4.2 Points of Agreement

Most proponents of all three positions agree on six foundational points. The current market is dominated by speculation. *Gharar* exists, in the sense that price uncertainty is real and significant. Concerns of *maysir* are legitimate, since gambling-like trading predominates. Pure cryptocurrency lacks intrinsic value, in that Bitcoin has no underlying productive asset. The fragmentation of regulation is a problem, since there is no global standard. And institutional oversight is needed, so that Sharia boards can monitor crypto products.

The disagreement is therefore not over whether cryptocurrency is currently problematic, on which most agree that it is, but over the legal consequence of that problematic status. The exception is the strict form of Position B, which disagrees at a more fundamental level, over whether a crypto-asset is property at all.

### 4.3 Points of Disagreement

The positions diverge on three specific questions.

**Does analogy apply?** For Position A, the commodity analogy holds despite speculation, because speculation can be managed through regulation. For Position B, it fails, because actual use is monetary rather than commodity-based, or because the asset is not property. For Position C, neither analogy applies, because cryptocurrency is *sui generis*.

**What follows from the current state of the market?** For Position A, the current market is problematic but can be remedied, and conditional permission is therefore appropriate now. For Position B, the current market is problematic and lacks a pathway to remedy, so prohibition is appropriate now. For Position C, it is problematic and it is unclear whether remedy is possible, so judgment should be deferred.

**Is there evidence of permissibility?** For Position A, evidence exists now in the form of emerging regulatory frameworks and growing institutional interest. For Position B, evidence is absent and unlikely to appear, because speculation is structural rather than accidental. For Position C, evidence will emerge as markets mature.

## 5. MAQASID SHARIAH FRAMEWORK: FIVE-DIMENSIONAL COMPLIANCE ANALYSIS

### 5.1 Application of Islamic Jurisprudential Objectives

Beyond the positions of individual scholars and bodies, Islamic jurisprudence offers a higher-order framework in the *maqasid al-shari'ah*, the objectives the law serves. Classical jurists, from al-Ghazali onwards, identified five necessities that the law protects:

- religion (*din*), which the law protects so that religious practice is not compromised;
- life (*nafs*), which it protects by safeguarding human welfare and survival;
- intellect (*'aql*), which it protects by supporting rational decision-making and education;
- property (*mal*), which it protects by enabling legitimate wealth creation and its preservation;
- lineage (*nasl*), which it protects by supporting family and social structures.

Chapter 2 operationalises these objectives through five evaluative criteria for financial instruments. Chapter 5 translates them into a five-dimensional framework for classifying digital assets. The present section introduces that framework.

### 5.2 Five-Dimensional Cryptocurrency Compliance Framework

For the classification of digital assets, the *maqasid* can be operationalised into five concrete dimensions. Each dimension is linked to the necessities it chiefly serves.

**Dimension 1: asset backing (*mal*).** The question is whether the asset is backed by real assets or productive capacity. Bitcoin has no backing. A collateralised stablecoin has backing, which counts in its favour provided the collateral is itself Sharia-compliant. An Islamic decentralised finance token backed by income-generating assets also has backing.

**Dimension 2: value stability (*mal* and *nafs*).** The question is whether holders can reasonably predict the asset's purchasing power. Bitcoin is extremely volatile. A dollar-backed stablecoin targets variance of less than one percent. Ether is moderately to highly volatile.

**Dimension 3: productive utility (*'aql* and *mal*).** The question is whether the asset facilitates real economic activity or merely speculation. Bitcoin has some payment utility and is widely held as a store of value. Smart contract platforms enable productive contracts. Meme coins are pure speculation.

**Dimension 4: governance and accountability (*din* and *nafs*).** The question is whether there are mechanisms that prevent fraud, manipulation and excessive speculation, and whether governance is transparent. Chapter 5 scores this dimension as a combination of decentralisation, transparency and accountability, so a fully decentralised protocol with transparent rules (such as Bitcoin) and a regulated issuer with audited reserves can both score well, for different reasons.

**Dimension 5: regulatory recognition (*din*, *mal* and *nafs*).** The question is whether the asset is recognised and regulated by legitimate authorities in key jurisdictions, and in particular by Sharia authorities. Bitcoin is admitted to regulated trading in Malaysia, the UAE, Bahrain and Indonesia but restricted in Saudi Arabia, and it is not generally recognised by Islamic authorities. Ether has a similar status. Unregistered tokens lack any recognition.

### 5.3 Compliance Scoring Methodology

Each dimension is scored from 0 to 5, so that the composite score ranges from 0 to 25. Within each dimension, a score of 0–1 indicates a serious violation, 1–2 a major concern that may be remediable, and 2–3 mixed concerns with conditional acceptance possible. A score of 3–4 indicates general compliance with minor concerns, and 4–5 full compliance.

The composite score determines the category, as set out in full in Chapter 5 (§5.3) and summarised in Table 1.2.

**Table 1.2: Composite categories of the classification framework (see Chapter 5, §5.3)**

| Composite score | Category | Assessment under the framework |
|---|---|---|
| 20–25 | A | Compatible with Islamic law; permissible with confidence |
| 15–19 | B | Conditionally compliant; permissible with reservations |
| 10–14 | C | Problematic; disliked or prohibited depending on conduct |
| 5–9 | D | Non-compliant; prohibited |
| Separate | E | Central bank digital currency; permissible in principle, subject to design review |

On the illustrative scores of Chapter 5, Bitcoin scores 13 (asset backing 1, stability 1, utility 4, governance 5, recognition 2) and therefore falls in Category C. A hypothetical Islamic digital asset backed by Sharia-compliant productive assets, stable within a narrow band, enabling Islamic contracts, governed by a Sharia board together with issuer oversight, and recognised in several jurisdictions, would score in the low 20s and fall in Category A. The categories are the conclusions of the framework, not rulings of any authority; holders of the strict form of Position B would not accept that any crypto-asset can reach Category A.

## 6. SYNTHESIS: TOWARD A UNIFIED CLASSIFICATION FRAMEWORK

### 6.1 The Convergence Hypothesis

This dissertation proposes that the three positions need not be viewed as irreconcilable. They can instead be treated as different points on a continuum that can be tested empirically. As markets mature and evidence accumulates, the positions may converge through demonstrated changes in market function. Three pathways of convergence are conceivable.

The first runs **from Position B to Position A**. Its trigger would be a rise in the share of payment volume in total crypto activity to a majority. Commerce would then predominate over speculation, reducing the concern of *maysir*, and greater market stability would reduce *gharar*. The reconstructed conditional framework of §3.2.5 would allow such a shift. The 2026 Karachi fatwa, however, does not endorse it, because its reasoning concerns the nature of the asset, not the state of the market.

The second runs **from Position C to Position A**. Its trigger would be the maturation of regulatory frameworks and the strengthening of Sharia oversight, so that crypto-assets become part of the formal financial system and Sharia authorities issue affirmative rulings. The 2026 determinations of Muhammadiyah and the Nahdlatul Ulama can be read as an instance of this pathway at the national level in Indonesia.

The third runs **from Position C to Position B**. Its trigger would be the persistence of speculative dominance and rising volatility, so that market dysfunction worsens rather than improves and institutional oversight proves ineffective. The empirical review of Position C would then yield evidence supporting prohibition.

Events in 2026 show that convergence is not assured. In the same year, Indonesian bodies moved along the second pathway while the Karachi fatwa moved in the opposite direction. The hypothesis should therefore be understood as identifying the conditions under which convergence could occur, not as predicting that it will.

### 6.2 Testable Convergence Indicators

The dissertation proposes six empirical indicators of the likelihood of convergence, set out in Table 1.3. The target values are the author's proposals and are offered as hypotheses for testing.

**Table 1.3: Proposed indicators of convergence**

| Indicator | Measurement | Proposed target for convergence |
|---|---|---|
| Payment volume ratio | Share of volume used for transactions rather than speculation | More than 50 percent payment-based |
| Price volatility | Monthly standard deviation of Bitcoin's price | Below 5 percent, approaching fiat currency levels |
| Regulatory maturity | Number of OIC jurisdictions with comprehensive crypto frameworks | Ten or more |
| Sharia authority positions | Share of major Sharia authorities with conditional permissibility rulings | More than 60 percent |
| Asset backing | Share of crypto market capitalisation in collateral-backed assets | More than 30 percent among major assets |
| Institutional participation | Islamic banks offering crypto-related products | Fifty or more active Islamic banks |

### 6.3 Implementation Case Studies: How the Unified Framework Operates in Practice

The convergence hypothesis and its indicators are theoretical. This section grounds them in the experience of six jurisdictions, drawing only on documented regulatory facts. It does not use hypothetical individual investors or unpublished market data.

#### 6.3.1 Case Study 1: Malaysia (Position A in Practice)

Malaysia is the most direct operationalisation of Position A. Digital assets are regulated as securities by the Securities Commission, exchanges must be registered, and the Commission's Shariah Advisory Council has resolved since 2020 that trading in digital assets on registered exchanges is permissible in principle. Since March 2026, exchanges have needed the Council's endorsement before offering a digital currency as Sharia-compliant (§3.1.3).

The Malaysian case confirms that Position A can work operationally when users receive clear signals of Sharia screening from a public authority. It also reveals a limit. A domestic regulatory framework cannot capture users fully while offshore platforms offer more assets and higher leverage. The question of how far screened domestic platforms displace unregulated offshore trading is empirical, and it is one the research agenda of Chapter 8 is designed to address.

#### 6.3.2 Case Study 2: Indonesia (Live Transition Experiment)

Indonesia offers the clearest example of jurisprudential positions in live negotiation. The 2021 Ijtima' Ulama decision, which is Position B on money with an asset-side exception, coexists with a religiously neutral licensing regime under POJK No. 27/2024, amended by POJK No. 23/2025. The connective tissue between them is missing. That connective tissue would be a DSN-MUI fatwa or a Sharia standard of the kind the statutory pathway of Law No. 4/2023 (P2SK) is designed to absorb. OJK reports that it is coordinating with DSN-MUI on a study towards such a standard, but none had been issued as of October 2026.

In the meantime, regulatory practice has begun to populate the asset-side exception through the sandbox. The gold-tokenisation model GOIDR, whose physical gold is held by Pegadaian according to OJK, graduated from the OJK sandbox on 8 August 2025. By October 2026 it was listed on both licensed exchanges. OJK's 2025 presentation described the token (one gram of gold per token, redeemable for physical gold) as meeting point 3 of the 2021 MUI decision, but that is the regulator's reading; no Sharia authority has issued a determination on the token. In the author's assessment, however, it is the closest Indonesian instance of the 2021 exception, which validates a crypto-asset with a clear underlying asset and benefit (Chapter 4, §4.1.9). Meanwhile, the mass organisations moved ahead of MUI. Muhammadiyah (4 March 2026) declared crypto transactions *mubah* with conditions, and the 35th NU Muktamar (29 August 2026) held Bitcoin to meet the criteria of *mal* and *thaman* and to be valid as a digital asset. Both exclude its use as money in Indonesia (Chapter 3, §3.4.5).

The Indonesian case does not map onto a single position. It is a live experiment in whether the exception clause of a restrictive ruling can carry a jurisdiction, one class of asset at a time, from Position B towards Position A. The mechanism is institutional: graduation from the sandbox, then exchange listing, then a study of Sharia classification. It could be replicated in other jurisdictions with similar starting points. It also shows that, where a national Sharia standard is slow to emerge, other religious authorities may move first. The full analysis is in Chapter 4 (§4.1).

#### 6.3.3 Case Study 3: Saudi Arabia's Regulatory Gap (Position B Enforcement)

Saudi Arabia's posture is consistent with Position B, enforced through non-recognition rather than through an explicit statutory prohibition of holding. No domestic exchange is licensed to offer crypto trading, and SAMA has warned the public that virtual currencies are not legal tender and are not licensed. [Unverified: the dates and terms of SAMA's warnings should be confirmed.] At the same time, the Saudi Central Bank has participated in central bank digital currency experiments, including Project mBridge.

The case suggests a structural consequence of Position B enforced through regulation. Residents who wish to hold crypto-assets cannot use domestic regulated platforms, and they turn instead to offshore platforms without Saudi supervision or Sharia oversight. The objective of limiting cryptocurrency within the supervised system is achieved, but at the cost of pushing activity beyond the regulator's view. The scale of such offshore activity is not reliably documented. If Saudi Arabia were to issue a central bank digital currency, it might later permit complementary frameworks, which would represent a movement from Position B towards Position C.

#### 6.3.4 Case Study 4: Bahrain's Stablecoin Strategy (Position A Modified)

Bahrain has built a prudential framework for crypto-asset services and, in July 2025, a dedicated module for the issuance and offering of stablecoins (§3.3.3). The stablecoin module requires licensing, high-quality liquid reserves and timely redemption. Those requirements correspond closely to the conditions that a Sharia analysis of fiat-backed stablecoins would impose (Chapter 3, §3.3.3, Category 3). The CBB has since granted in-principle approval to a first issuer under the module. [Unverified: the identity and status of the approved issuer should be confirmed from CBB announcements.]

The Bahraini case shows how a prudential regulator can create the conditions under which stablecoins could satisfy several dimensions of the framework at once: backing, stability and governance. It does not show that stablecoins are accepted by Sharia authorities. The 2026 Karachi fatwa expressly rejects them, and no Sharia ruling on Bahraini stablecoins has been located. The convergence of the three positions on stablecoins, which a purely prudential reading might suggest, is therefore not borne out by the jurisprudential record.

#### 6.3.5 Case Study 5: UAE VARA's Tiered System (Position A + C Synthesis)

The UAE combines several regulatory regimes: VARA in Dubai, the FSRA in Abu Dhabi, and federal oversight by the Central Bank of the UAE and the Securities and Commodities Authority. These regimes license a wide range of virtual asset activities, distinguish between activities by their risk, and impose requirements that are stricter for retail clients and for higher-risk products. They are religiously neutral (§3.3.3).

For the classification framework, the significance of the UAE is institutional. Its regimes show that a jurisdiction can admit a broad range of virtual assets under graduated supervision. Investors and institutions holding different jurisprudential positions can then act on those positions within a single regulated market. An adherent of Position A could hold screened assets. An adherent of Position B would abstain. An adherent of Position C could wait for evidence. The framework does not require agreement on theory; it creates conditions in which different positions can coexist and adjust as evidence emerges. What the UAE lacks, as of October 2026, is a public Sharia screening mechanism comparable to Malaysia's.

#### 6.3.6 Case Study 6: Pakistan (Convergence Pathway Uncertain)

Pakistan illustrates how quickly regulatory and jurisprudential conditions can change, and how they can move in opposite directions. In July 2025 a presidential ordinance (Ordinance No. VII of 2025) established the Pakistan Virtual Assets Regulatory Authority (PVARA) as an autonomous federal regulator empowered to license and supervise virtual asset service providers. In September 2025 PVARA invited expressions of interest from established exchanges. [Unverified: the subsequent parliamentary status of the ordinance should be confirmed.] In June 2026, however, the Darul Uloom Karachi fatwa signed by Usmani held crypto-assets, including stablecoins, not to be *mal*. PVARA subsequently met Usmani and argued that fully reserved stablecoins pegged to national currencies should be treated differently (Chapter 3, §3.3.2).

Pakistan therefore presents a direct confrontation between an emerging licensing regime and the strictest form of Position B, issued by the country's most influential scholar of Islamic finance. Convergence is not inevitable. It depends on institutional leadership, on whether a regulator champions a framework, and on scholarly engagement, on whether scholars examine evidence from regulated markets. It also depends on political support, on whether governments give priority to financial inclusion and innovation over caution. The outcome of the dialogue between PVARA and the Karachi scholars will be a critical test of the convergence hypothesis.

### 6.4 Integrated Classification Proposal

The dissertation proposes a unified framework that draws the three positions together into three tiers, using the categories of Chapter 5.

The first tier comprises assets that are **currently problematic** (Categories C and D). It includes pure cryptocurrencies, such as Bitcoin, Ether and most other tokens, in their current form. For these the recommendation is restriction, with any holding confined to informed investors acting on the advice of a Sharia board. Their status is a conditional prohibition pending evidence of market change, except under the strict form of Position B, for which the prohibition is unconditional.

The second tier comprises assets that are **conditionally permissible** (Categories B and A). It includes asset-backed tokens and collateralised stablecoins, traded on regulated exchanges with Sharia oversight. These are permissible subject to conditions, with institutional monitoring.

The third tier comprises assets that are **compliant with confidence** (upper Category A). It includes Islamic stablecoins backed by Sharia-compliant productive assets, sukuk tokenised on a blockchain under institutional oversight, and Islamic decentralised finance protocols with Sharia governance. Such assets would be permissible and their development encouraged.

## 7. CHAPTER CONCLUSION AND TRANSITION TO EMPIRICAL STUDY

### 7.1 Jurisprudential Consensus and Disagreement

This chapter has shown that Islamic scholars and institutions hold three substantively different positions on the classification of cryptocurrency. Beneath this surface disagreement lies a measure of agreement on fundamental points. Current markets exhibit problematic characteristics: the dominance of speculation, *gharar* and *maysir*. Crypto products require careful institutional oversight. For most authorities, the Sharia status of crypto-assets is not permanently fixed but depends on market conditions. And regulatory frameworks and institutional participation significantly influence the Sharia assessment.

The legitimate disagreements are methodological. Scholars disagree on whether analogical reasoning applies to cryptocurrency, on whether its problematic features can be remedied through regulation, and on whether the assessment should be definitive (Positions A and B) or deferred pending evidence (Position C). The strict form of Position B adds a deeper disagreement, over whether a crypto-asset is property at all.

### 7.2 Research Validation Agenda

The dissertation's three-phase empirical agenda, set out in Chapter 8 and Appendix G, is designed to test these positions against market reality. The first phase, jurisprudential validation, would verify that the three positions accurately represent scholarly opinion, identify the conditions each position specifies for its own modification, and document the empirical measures each uses. The second, a study of market perception, would survey Islamic financial institutions on their assessment of digital assets, measure their readiness for regulatory frameworks, and assess how the market perceives mechanisms of Sharia compliance. The third, a study of regulatory implementation, would examine how OJK (Indonesia), the Securities Commission (Malaysia) and VARA (UAE) implement their frameworks. It would assess whether implementation reduces concerns of *gharar* and *maysir* and document evidence that supports or contradicts the convergence hypothesis.

### 7.3 Bridge to Regulatory Analysis

The following chapters move from the taxonomy of positions to their foundations and consequences. Chapter 2 examines the *maqasid* foundation of the assessment. Chapter 3 analyses the positions of the principal authorities in detail. Chapter 4 examines how five jurisdictions translate Islamic legal principles into regulatory practice, and Chapters 5 and 6 develop the classification framework and the proposal for harmonisation.

### 7.4 Contribution to Islamic Finance Literature

This chapter contributes to the scholarship of Islamic finance in four ways. It systematises jurisprudential methodology, moving beyond the observation that scholars disagree to an explicit comparison of the tools of legal reasoning each position employs (*qiyas*, *istihsan*, *maslahah*) and of why they reach different conclusions. It operationalises the *maqasid*, translating abstract objectives into five concrete and measurable dimensions applicable to any digital asset. It identifies mechanisms of convergence, specifying the empirical conditions under which the positions could reach agreement rather than assuming that disagreement is permanent. And it establishes a framework for empirical research that connects jurisprudential theory to testable predictions.

### 7.5 Conclusion

The classification of cryptocurrency under Islamic law is, to a significant degree, an empirical question. As markets mature, does the practical function of crypto-assets move towards payment, asset backing and institutional integration? Or do speculation, volatility and institutional gaps persist? The three positions can be read as different predictions about the answer. Position A predicts that conditional remedy is possible and already occurring. Position B predicts that the fundamental problems are structural and unlikely to be resolved. Position C predicts that accumulating evidence will resolve the question. The question is not wholly empirical, however. The strict form of Position B, articulated in the 2026 Karachi fatwa, rests on a claim about the nature of a digital record that no market evidence can settle.

The dissertation's empirical agenda is designed to evaluate the testable parts of these predictions, while the jurisprudential analysis of Chapters 2, 3 and 5 addresses the parts that evidence cannot resolve. Together they aim to clarify which position best reflects the actual role of crypto-assets in Islamic finance and in Muslim communities.
